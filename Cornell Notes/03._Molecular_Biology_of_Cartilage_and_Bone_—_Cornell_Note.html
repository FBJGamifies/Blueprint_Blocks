LO 1.01 • Tissue Properties

Cartilage Characteristics, Types & Matrix

Vascularity Avascular
Three Subtypes Hyaline, Elastic, Fibrocartilage
Mechanical Profile Resilient, flexible, low-friction
Primary Matrix Secrets Type II collagen, GAGs, proteoglycans

Structural Characteristics & Extracellular Matrix of Cartilage

LO 1.01 • Cartilage Overview & Matrix Slides
Core Physical Properties & Subtypes
  • Avascular & Resilient Tissue: Cartilage is an avascular, resilient tissue designed to withstand mechanical load without direct blood vessel penetration.
  • Biomechanical Balance: It provides a low-friction surface while remaining flexible with some rigidity to support joint articulation and structural airways.
  • Three Cartilage Classifications: Lecture divides cartilage into three primary histological types: Hyaline Cartilage, Elastic Cartilage, and Fibrocartilage.
Extracellular Matrix (ECM) Synthesis
  • Collagen Framework: Cartilage cells synthesize and maintain the cartilaginous matrix by secreting Type II collagen fibrils for tensile support.
  • Hydration & Compression Molecules: Alongside collagen, cells secrete glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) and proteoglycans (specifically aggrecan) that give the matrix its resilient cushioning properties.
  • Matrix Maintenance: Continuous turnover and synthesis of Type II collagen and proteoglycan aggrecan preserve tissue flexibility and low-friction mechanics.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

"Remember the four core hallmarks of cartilage right away: it is resilient, avascular, flexible with some rigidity, and forms a low-friction surface—powered by Type II collagen, GAGs, and proteoglycans."

Second-Order Board Clue

When a vignette contrasts resilient, avascular skeletal matrix against vascularized bone, link Type II collagen and aggrecan to cartilage, whereas Type I collagen defines mineralized osteoid.

LO 1.02 • Chondrogenesis

Appositional vs. Interstitial Growth & Cell Morphology

Appositional Growth MSCs in perichondrium
Interstitial Growth Chondrocyte division (isogenous)
Chondroblast Shape Elliptical (perichondrium)
Chondrocyte Niche Cuboidal in lacunae (up to 8)

Dual Modes of Chondrogenesis & Spatial Cell Maturation

LO 1.02 • Chondrogenesis & Cartilage Cells Slides
Appositional vs. Interstitial Mechanisms
  • Appositional Growth (Differentiation): Mesenchymal Stem Cells (MSCs) differentiate within the perichondrium to form new cartilage along the outer surface of existing cartilage.
  • Interstitial Growth (Cell Division): Mature chondrocytes divide mitotically inside the tissue to form isogenous groups, expanding new cartilage from within an existing cartilage mass.
  • Lineage Progression: Peripheral chondroblasts synthesize surrounding matrix, become encased, and transition into deeper chondrocytes.
Chondroblast vs. Chondrocyte Histology
  • Chondroblasts: Possess an elliptical shape, reside generally in the perichondrium, and actively give rise to mature chondrocytes.
  • Chondrocytes: Transition to a cuboidal shape in deeper cartilage regions, encasing themselves within matrix pockets called lacunae.
  • Isogenous Clusters: Following interstitial division inside lacunae, chondrocytes cluster into isogenous groups containing up to 8 cells sharing a territorial matrix.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

"Contrast the two growth mechanisms cleanly: appositional growth is differentiation of MSCs in the perichondrium at the surface, whereas interstitial growth is chondrocyte cell division into isogenous groups of up to 8 cells deeper inside."

Second-Order Board Clue

A histology image showing cuboidal cells arranged in clusters of 4–8 inside lacunae confirms interstitial growth by mature chondrocytes, whereas surface elliptical cells mark appositional growth.

LO 1.03 • Transcription Control

Osteo-Chondro-Progenitor Lineage & Sox-9

Common Precursor Mesenchymal stem cells (MSC)
Chondrogenic Signal BMP signaling → Sox-9
Sox Trio Factors Sox-9, Sox-5, and Sox-6
Osteogenic Counter-Branch Wnt/β-catenin & RunX2

Bipotential MSC Lineage Commitment & Sox-9 Transcriptional Targets

LO 1.03 • MSC Lineage & Sox-9 Slides
Osteo-Chondro-Progenitor Branching
  • Shared Origin: Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) act as the common osteo-chondro-progenitor cell giving rise to both chondroblasts and osteoblasts.
  • Chondroblast Commitment: Driven by BMP signaling activating the DNA-binding protein Sox-9 and the full Sox Trio (Sox-9/5/6).
  • Osteoblast Commitment: Diverges toward pre-osteoblasts and osteoblasts under the control of Wnt/β-catenin and RunX2.
Downstream Functions of Sox-9
  • Trio Amplification: Sox-9 directly upregulates Sox-5 and Sox-6 proteins to drive maturation of cartilage cells.
  • ECM Gene Expression: Together, the Sox Trio controls transcription of Type II collagen and proteoglycan aggrecan in cartilage cells.
  • Systemic & Gonadal Roles: Beyond regulating human skeletal development (pelvic, vertebral, cranial, and long bones), Sox-9 is essential for male sexual development.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

"Sox-9 is a DNA-binding transcription factor downstream of BMP signaling. If Sox-9 is knocked out (Sox-9 null), chondrogenic cells fail to mature and pelvic, vertebral, cranial, and long bones malform."

Second-Order Board Clue

A fetus presenting with simultaneous severe chondrodysplasia and 46,XY sex reversal implicates a transcription factor governing both Sox-5/Sox-6 cartilage maturation and male sexual development: Sox-9.

LO 1.04 • Genetic Pathology

Campomelic Dysplasia (Bent-Limb Dysplasia)

Inheritance & Gene Autosomal dominant SOX9
Radiograph Hallmarks Bowed tibia/femur, absent fibula
Lethal Complication Respiratory insufficiency
Gonadal Finding 75% genetic males: female genitalia

Molecular Defect, Radiographic Triad & Clinical Course of Campomelic Dysplasia

LO 1.04 • Campomelic Dysplasia Slides
Genetic Defect & Matrix Collapse
  • Autosomal Dominant Etiology: Caused by an autosomal dominant mutation in the Sox-9 transcription factor.
  • Failed Maturation: Mutant Sox-9 hinders expression of Sox-5 and Sox-6, arresting chondrocyte maturation.
  • ECM Disruption: Hindered transcription of Type II collagen and proteoglycan aggrecan yields structurally weak cartilage throughout the body.
Radiographic & Clinical Findings
  • 27-Week Prenatal Radiograph: Shows ventral bowing or shortened tibia and femur paired with a hypoplastic or fully absent fibula.
  • Axial & Craniofacial Defects: Patients exhibit craniofacial defects, bowing/angulation of long bones, and hypoplasia of pelvic and scapular bones.
  • Mortality & Sex Reversal: Weak airway/lung cartilage causes fatal newborn respiratory insufficiency; additionally, 75% of genetic males develop female genitalia.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

"Why do many newborns with Campomelic Dysplasia not survive? Weak cartilage in the lungs causes respiratory insufficiency—and don't miss the 27-week radiograph showing ventral bowing of the tibia/femur and an absent or hypoplastic fibula."

Second-Order Board Clue

A newborn with 46,XY karyotype and female external genitalia develops neonatal respiratory insufficiency and bowed tibiae with an absent fibula; the defective protein is a BMP-activated DNA-binding factor (Sox-9) that normally induces aggrecan.

LO 2.01 • Bone Formation

Osteoblast Lineage & Matrix Mineralization

Lineage Drivers Wnt/β-catenin & RunX2
Collagen Type Type I collagen fibers
Ca2+ Binding Proteins Osteonectin & Osteocalcin
Phosphate Enzyme Alkaline phosphatase (ALP)

Osteoblast Differentiation, Secretory Proteins & Crystallization

LO 2.01 • Osteoblasts & Lineage Slides
MSC Differentiation & Organic Matrix
  • Transcriptional Commitment: Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) differentiate into pre-osteoblasts and mature osteoblasts via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway upregulating RunX2.
  • Secretory Collagen Scaffold: Mature osteoblasts are active secretory cells that synthesize and deposit Type I collagen fibers to form the structural organic scaffold of bone.
  • Non-Collagenous Matrix Proteins: Osteoblasts secrete osteonectin (a Ca2+-binding glycoprotein) and osteocalcin (a Ca2+-binding protein) into the nascent matrix.
Enzymatic Mineralization Cascade
  • Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) Action: Osteoblasts produce alkaline phosphatase (ALP), which enzymatically raises local PO4^3- ion levels at mineralization sites.
  • Matrix Vesicle Secretion: Osteoblasts secrete specialized vesicles loaded with bone matrix proteins directly into the unmineralized bony matrix.
  • Crystal Nucleation: High local calcium bound by osteonectin/osteocalcin combined with ALP-elevated PO4^3- drives crystallization of the bone matrix.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

"Know the exact biochemical roles of the three bone matrix products: Osteonectin is the Ca2+-binding glycoprotein, Osteocalcin is the Ca2+-binding protein, and Alkaline Phosphatase raises local PO4^3- ions so vesicles trigger crystallization."

Second-Order Board Clue

A defect in alkaline phosphatase (ALP) prevents local elevation of PO4^3- ions within secreted matrix vesicles, impairing crystallization despite intact Type I collagen secretion.

LO 2.02 • Mechanosensation

Osteocytes & Canalicular Network

Cellular Origin Matrix-encased osteoblasts
Morphology Dendritic processes in canaliculi
Cell Coupling Gap junctions
Primary Role Mechanosensory matrix regulator

Osteocyte Differentiation, Canalicular Signaling & Remodeling Command

LO 2.02 • Osteocytes Slides
Structural Encasement & Connectivity
  • Terminal Maturation: Once osteoblasts finish secreting osteoid and encase themselves inside mineralized bone matrix, they differentiate into osteocytes.
  • Canalicular Architecture: Osteocytes extend long dendritic processes radiating through microscopic tunnels called canaliculi.
  • Intercellular Communication: Adjacent osteocytes and surface bone cells communicate directly via gap junctions linking their dendritic tips.
Mechanosensory & Regulatory Function
  • Mechanical Load Sensors: Osteocytes serve as the mechanosensory cells of bone, detecting fluid shear and mechanical strain across the skeleton.
  • Central Matrix Maintenance: They play a central coordinating role in maintaining the integrity of the mineralized bony matrix.
  • Dual Effector Control: In response to mechanical cues, osteocytes actively regulate both osteoblast (formation) and osteoclast (resorption) activity.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

"Osteocytes are not passive trapped cells—they are the mechanosensory hub of bone, wired through canaliculi and gap junctions to govern both osteoblast deposition and osteoclast resorption."

Second-Order Board Clue

Microscopic identification of star-shaped cells extending dendritic processes through canaliculi and coupled via gap junctions points to the primary mechanosensory cell coordinating bone remodeling.

LO 2.03 • Bone Resorption

Osteoclast Origin, Morphology & Function

Stem Cell Origin Hematopoietic stem cells
Cell Architecture Large, motile, polarized

Primary Function Matrix degradation & resorption

Hematopoietic Lineage & Resorptive Identity of Osteoclasts

LO 2.03 • Osteoclasts Slide
Distinct Embryologic Origin & Morphology
  • Separate Stem Lineage: Unlike chondroblasts, osteoblasts, and osteocytes (all MSC-derived), osteoclasts are derived from hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs).
  • Distinctive Size & Polarity: Osteoclasts are large motile cells that exhibit clear structural polarization toward the bone surface they actively degrade.
  • Multinucleated Syncytium: Formed by fusion of hematopoietic precursors, osteoclasts are characteristically multinucleated.
Bone Matrix Degradation
  • Resorptive Role: The primary physiological function of osteoclasts is the degradation and resorption of mineralized bone matrix.
  • Motile Remodeling: Because they are motile and polarized, osteoclasts migrate across bone surfaces to carve resorption bays as part of normal skeletal turnover.
  • Lineage Contrast: Any signaling mutation strictly confined to the MSC / RunX2 axis affects osteoblasts directly without arising from the hematopoietic osteoclast precursor pool.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

"Always separate the lineages on exams: osteoblasts and osteocytes come from Mesenchymal Stem Cells, whereas osteoclasts are large, motile, polarized, multinucleated cells derived from hematopoietic stem cells."

Second-Order Board Clue

A bone biopsy reveals large, multinucleated, polarized cells along an eroded trabecular edge; their precursor originates in the hematopoietic stem cell compartment rather than the Sox-9/RunX2 MSC pool.

LO 2.04 • Remodeling Axis

Dynamic Bone Homeostasis

Tissue Nature Dynamic, constantly remodeled
Deposition Arm Osteoblasts (Formation)
Resorption Arm Osteoclasts (Resorption)
Pathologic Trigger Disturbances in balance

Coupled Bone Deposition & Resorption in Skeletal Maintenance

LO 2.04 • Bone Homeostasis Slide
Coupled Remodeling Equilibrium
  • Dynamic Skeletal Turnover: Unlike static structural scaffolding, bone is a dynamic tissue that is constantly being resorbed and newly formed throughout life.
  • Homeostatic Balance: Skeletal integrity depends on a precise equilibrium between matrix deposition by osteoblasts and matrix resorption by osteoclasts.
  • Osteocyte Oversight: Embedded osteocytes bridge both arms, sensing mechanical demand to tune osteoblast formation and osteoclast resorption.
Disease Emergence from Imbalance
  • Pathologic Consequences: Any disturbance that uncouples deposition from resorption—or arrests osteoblast maturation—leads directly to structural bone disease.
  • Maturation Arrest Example: When RunX2 becomes abnormally hyperactive (as in Fibrous Dysplasia), osteoblasts fail to mature properly, yielding weak, under-mineralized osteoid instead of organized trabeculae.
  • Ectopic Bone Formation: Conversely, aberrant inflammatory and BMP signaling recruits MSCs outside the skeleton to form extraskeletal bone in soft tissues.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

"Bone is constantly turning over. Homeostasis requires a tight balance between osteoblast formation and osteoclast resorption, orchestrated by osteocytes—and disturbing that balance is where disease begins."

Second-Order Board Clue

When a question describes defective skeletal remodeling due to failed osteoblast maturation despite abundant immature osteoid, distinguish impaired osteoblast differentiation from primary hyperactivity of hematopoietic osteoclasts.

LO 3.01 • Fibrous Dysplasia

Fibrous Dysplasia: Forms, Presentation & Histology

Core Tissue Lesion Immature fibrous tissue replaces bone
Monostotic Form 1 bone, 20s–30s, most common
Polyostotic Form Multiple bones, children
Common Sites Tibia, femur, humerus, ribs
Trabecular Histology Thin, discontinuous, disorganized

Clinical Subtypes, Anatomical Distribution & Histopathology of Fibrous Dysplasia

LO 3.01 • Fibrous Dysplasia Overview & Histology Slides
Clinical Forms & Skeletal Manifestations
  • Disease Definition: A rare bone disease in which normal bone is replaced with immature fibrous tissue; while it can affect any skeletal region, it is most common in the tibia, femur, humerus, and ribs.
  • Monostotic vs. Polyostotic: Monostotic disease (the most common form) is limited to 1 bone type and generally appears in the 20s to 30s; polyostotic disease involves multiple bones and generally manifests in children.
  • Clinical Morbidity: Patients experience chronic bone pain, bony deformities, unequal limb length, bowing of long bones, and structurally weak bone with a high fracture rate.
Histologic Architecture
  • Fibrous Infiltration: Microscopic sections demonstrate dense infiltration of fibrous tissue surrounding and replacing bony trabeculae.
  • Trabecular Deformity: Remaining bony trabeculae are characteristically discontinuous, abnormally thin, disorganized, and irregular in shape.
  • Mineralization Defect: Lesions contain excessive osteoid alongside under-mineralized bone, explaining the mechanical fragility and bowing.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

"Contrast the two presentations: monostotic fibrous dysplasia hits 1 bone in the 20s–30s and is the most common form, while polyostotic involves multiple bones and manifests earlier in children—both leaving thin, disorganized trabeculae and excessive unmineralized osteoid."

Second-Order Board Clue

A 26-year-old with chronic femoral pain and a pathologic fracture shows discontinuous, irregular trabeculae embedded in fibrous tissue with excessive osteoid; this represents the monostotic form of Fibrous Dysplasia.

LO 3.02 • Signaling Crosstalk

Fibrous Dysplasia: GNAS, cAMP/PKA & Wnt Crosstalk

Mutated Gene GNAS (Gs protein)
Signaling Defect Constitutive Gs & cAMP/PKA
Transcription Paradox Excessive RunX2 activation
Cellular Result Impaired osteoblast maturation
Therapeutic Targets Inhibit Frizzled & Adenylyl Cyclase

Pathologic Wnt/β-catenin & cAMP/PKA Crosstalk + Therapeutic Blockade

LO 3.02 • Causes & Treatment Slides + 03b Notes
Molecular Mechanism of RunX2 Hyperactivation
  • GNAS Mutation: Fibrous Dysplasia is caused by a mutation in the GNAS (Guanine Nucleotide Binding alpha stimulating) gene, which encodes a Gs protein.
  • Constitutive cAMP/PKA Cascade: Mutant Gs remains constitutively active, driving Adenylyl Cyclase (AC), cAMP accumulation, and persistent PKA activation.
  • Pathway Crosstalk & Arrest: PKA crosstalks with the Wnt/β-catenin pathway (normally acting via the Frizzled receptor to suppress the β-catenin inhibitory complex), causing excessive RunX2 activation that paradoxically impairs osteoblast differentiation and maturation.
Palliative Care & Targeted Interventions
  • Supportive Standard Care: Current clinical management relies on palliative care focused on managing fractures and progressive skeletal deformity.
  • Upstream Wnt Blockade: Because Wnt/β-catenin synergy amplifies RunX2 overactivation, one therapeutic strategy is to inhibit the Frizzled receptor.
  • Downstream GPCR Blockade: Simultaneously or alternatively, therapies aim to inhibit Adenylyl Cyclase (AC) to suppress excessive cAMP/PKA signaling and normalize osteoblast maturation.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

"Do not assume Fibrous Dysplasia is a loss of RunX2! Constitutive Gs and cAMP/PKA crosstalk with Wnt/β-catenin actually drives abnormally HIGH RunX2 levels—which stalls osteoblast maturation and leaves immature fibrous bone."

Second-Order Board Clue

To counteract excessive RunX2 activation caused by a gain-of-function GNAS mutation, targeted molecular strategies seek to inhibit either the Frizzled receptor or Adenylyl Cyclase (AC).

Core Definition Bone tissue outside skeleton
Genetic Forms FOP, POH, GNAS-based HO
Highest-Risk Cohort 65% combat amputations
Post-Op / Trauma Rates 40% hip arthroplasty, 30% fractures
Clinical Progression Asymptomatic → ↓ ROM, pain

Etiologic Categories, High-Risk Epidemiology & Clinical Staging of HO

LO 3.03 • Heterotopic Ossification Overview & Epidemiology Slides
Etiologies & Classification
  • Ectopic Localization: Heterotopic Ossification (HO) is the formation of true bone tissue outside the skeleton, most often within muscles and other soft tissues.
  • Genetic HO Disorders: Includes Fibrodysplasia Ossificans Progressiva (FOP), Progressive Osseous Heteroplasia (POH), and GNAS-based HO disorders.
  • Acquired Triggers: Driven by persistent inflammation following neurological injury (traumatic brain or spinal cord injury), surgery (arthroscopy, joint replacement), and severe trauma (burns, fractures, high blunt impact, blast injuries, amputations).
Epidemiology & Symptom Trajectory
  • Demographics & Sites: Affects children to elderly, with 50% of patients in their 20s–30s and a 3:2 male-to-female ratio; commonly affects the elbow, thigh, pelvis, shoulders (and knee).
  • Incidence Benchmarks: Develops in 65% of combat-related amputations, 40% of hip arthroplasty cases, and 30% of bone fractures or dislocations.
  • Clinical Evolution: Symptoms depend on stage—initially asymptomatic (discovered incidentally on radiographs), progressing to decreased or lost range of motion with localized pain and edema.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

"Pay attention to the trauma epidemiology: 65% of service members with combat amputations and 40% of hip arthroplasty patients develop HO, and it often starts completely silent until caught incidentally on radiograph or when joint ROM drops."

Second-Order Board Clue

A 28-year-old man recovering from a traumatic brain injury and total hip arthroplasty develops progressive hip stiffness, localized edema, and soft-tissue calcification around the joint—consistent with Heterotopic Ossification.

LO 3.04 • HO Mechanisms

HO Pathways: Endochondral vs. Intramembranous

Shared Upstream Trigger Mast cells + abnormal BMP
Genetic HO Defect Hypersensitive BMP receptor
Endochondral Route Cartilage intermediate (Sox Trio)
Intramembranous Route Direct bone (Wnt/RunX2)

Inflammatory BMP Recruitment & Divergent Ossification Cascades

LO 3.04 • 2 Paths for HO Development Slides
Endochondral Pathway (Severe Trauma & Genetic HO)
  • Upstream Cascade: Injury/insult triggers inflammation, mast cell recruitment, and abnormal BMP signaling acting on resident MSCs / progenitor cells.
  • Genetic & Severe Trauma Driver: Genetic HO features a hypersensitive BMP receptor increasing BMP signaling; nongenetic endochondral HO follows severe trauma (fractures, joint replacements, burns, TBI).
  • Chondrogenic Intermediate & Outcome: MSCs activate the Sox Trio (Sox-9/5/6) to build a cartilage intermediate replaced by bone inside deep skeletal muscles, joints, and tendons/ligaments, producing large bony masses that restrict movement.
Intramembranous Pathway (Low-Energy Trauma)
  • Direct Osteogenic Formation: Intramembranous ossification forms bone directly from MSC progenitors without passing through a cartilage intermediate.
  • Molecular Drivers & Triggers: Progenitor cells signal via Wnt/β-catenin and RunX2 following low-energy trauma (small injuries, blunt trauma, overuse injuries, contusions).
  • Anatomical Plane & Outcome: Occurs in superficial skeletal muscles, fascia, and connective tissue, producing smaller masses that stabilize over time.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

"Injury severity dictates which pathway nongenetic HO takes: severe trauma (burns, TBI, joint replacement) drives endochondral ossification via Sox-9/5/6 into large restricting masses in deep muscle, whereas low-energy blunt trauma drives intramembranous ossification via Wnt/RunX2 into smaller superficial masses."

Second-Order Board Clue

In genetic HO, a mutation rendering the BMP receptor hypersensitive induces mast cell recruitment and Sox Trio activation in MSCs, resulting in endochondral ossification with a cartilage intermediate.

Management of Heterotopic Ossification

Two Core Goals Improve symptoms & removal
Most Common Drug NSAIDs (indomethacin)
Surgical Recurrence 41% require repeat excision
Prophylactic Modality Local radiation therapy
Investigational Target BMP antagonists

Anti-Inflammatory Prophylaxis, Radiation, Excision & Investigational Targets

LO 3.05 • Management: Heterotopic Ossification Slides
Pharmacologic & Radiation Prophylaxis
  • NSAIDs (Most Common Strategy): Indomethacin and related NSAIDs are the most common treatment strategy, used primarily post-operatively to suppress the inflammatory cascade driving MSC osteogenesis.
  • Selective COX-2 Inhibition: Cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) inhibitors achieve similar prophylactic results to traditional NSAIDs.
  • Local Radiation Therapy: Targeted local radiation therapy helps reduce HO development following total hip arthroplasty and surgical excisions.
Surgical Excision & Molecular Pipeline
  • Indications for Excision: Surgical removal is best suited for severe trauma (endochondral) cases and mature heterotopic bone causing ankylosed joints.
  • High Re-Operation Burden: Even after surgical removal, 41% of patients require additional excision procedures due to recurrence.
  • Investigational Molecular Targets: Because abnormal BMP signaling initiates progenitor commitment, BMP antagonists are actively under investigation to block HO at its source.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

"Know the two major management goals—improve symptoms and remove mature bone—along with the key stats: NSAIDs like indomethacin are the most common post-op strategy, and 41% of patients undergoing surgical excision need repeat surgery."

Second-Order Board Clue

Following total hip arthroplasty in a high-risk patient, post-operative prophylaxis with indomethacin (or a COX-2 inhibitor) and/or local radiation therapy targets early inflammatory recruitment before ankylosed mature bone requires excision.
