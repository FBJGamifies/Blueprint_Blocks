LO 1.01 • Baseline & Matrix

Normal Bone & Osteoblastic Rimming

Normal Marrow Content Adipose and hematopoietic elements
Lamellar Matrix Pattern Parallel collagen fibers

Normal Radial Head Comparison & Histologic Matrix Rules

LO 1.01 • Case 1 (Q1) & Lecture Slides
Normal Bone Architecture (Case 1 / Q1)
  • Trabecular & Marrow Baseline: Normal radial head histology demonstrates trabecular bone enclosing a normal marrow space filled with adipose and hematopoietic elements.
  • Surface & Cortical Layers: A thin rim of cortical bone supports the outer margin, while articular cartilage caps the end of the bone.
  • Lamellar Bone: Mature normal bone consists of lamellar bone characterized by parallel collagen fibers.
Woven Bone & Benign Rimming
  • Woven Bone Formation: Woven bone displays a disorganized matrix rather than parallel collagen arrays; it appears in various bone lesions and in normal fracture repair.
  • Osteoblastic Rimming Definition: Osteoblasts line up neatly along the outer edge of trabeculae of woven bone.
  • Diagnostic Discriminator: Seeing uniform osteoblastic rimming around woven bone trabeculae is generally a reliable histologic sign of a benign bone lesion (e.g., osteoid osteoma) rather than osteosarcoma.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Always anchor tumor slides against Case 1 normal radial bone. Look at the collagen organization to separate lamellar from woven bone, and check the edge of the trabeculae for osteoblastic rimming.”

Second-Order Board Clue

When a biopsy of a bone lesion shows haphazard, disorganized woven bone trabeculae lined by a single orderly row of benign-appearing osteoblasts, think of reactive fracture callus or a benign osteoid-producing tumor rather than malignancy.

LO 1.02 • Benign Bone Tumor

Osteoid Osteoma

Size & Demographics Less than 2 cm; teens & 20s
Classic Site Cortex of femur or tibia
Clinical Hallmark Nocturnal pain relieved by NSAIDs
Radiologic Hallmark Cleared nidus with reactive sclerosis
Microscopic Hallmark Woven bone with osteoblastic rimming

Cortical Nidus with Dense Reactive Sclerosis & NSAID-Responsive Pain

LO 1.02 • Lecture Scenario & Case 2 (Q2)
Clinical Scenarios & Epidemiology
  • Demographics & Size: Benign bone-forming tumor measuring less than 2 cm in diameter, affecting teens and 20s with a 3:1 male-to-female predominance.
  • Location: Arises in the cortex of femur or tibia in 50% of cases.
  • Lecture & Case 2 Vignettes: Both the 10-year-old boy with 3 months of left thigh pain and Case 2 (20-year-old male with right leg pain) present with severe nocturnal pain that wakes them from sleep and is classically relieved by ibuprofen (NSAIDs).
Imaging & Nidus Histopathology
  • CT / X-Ray Appearance: Case 2 coronal CT of the tibia shows a medial cortical lesion containing a central cleared (lucent) nidus (meaning “nest”) surrounded by thick, white reactive cortical bone.
  • Microscopic Nidus: Resection reveals a small area of fibrous-appearing tissue (nidus) containing interconnecting trabeculae of woven bone lined by osteoblastic rimming.
  • Surrounding Bone: Beyond the nidus lies very thick, dense, sclerotic reactive bone.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Remember the terminology: nidus means nest. On X-ray and CT you see a cleared central nidus surrounded by thick reactive cortical bone, and microscopically the osteoblastic rimming signals a benign lesion.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A young adult or adolescent with night-time shin or thigh pain relieved by ibuprofen and a small (<2 cm) cortical lucency surrounded by sclerotic reactive bone has an osteoid osteoma.

LO 1.03 • Benign Bone Tumor

Osteoblastoma

Size Threshold Larger than 2 cm
Primary Site Posterior vertebral column
Key Negative Clue (Imaging) No reactive cortical bone
Key Negative Clue (Clinical) Does not respond to NSAIDs

Large Vertebral Analog Without Marked Sclerosis or NSAID Relief

LO 1.03 • Lecture Contrast to Osteoid Osteoma
Histologic Overlap & Size Rule
  • Histologic Similarity: Histologically similar to osteoid osteoma, forming trabeculae of woven bone with benign osteoblastic rimming.
  • Size Cutoff: Distinguished arbitrarily by size, measuring larger than 2 cm in diameter.
  • Lack of Sclerosis: Unlike osteoid osteoma, it usually exhibits no reactive cortical bone surrounding the lesion.
Spinal Localization & Clinical Behavior
  • Anatomic Predilection: Usually involves the posterior vertebral column, specifically the laminae and pedicles, rather than the femur or tibia cortex.
  • Pharmacologic Discriminator: Pain from osteoblastoma does not usually respond to NSAIDs, separating it clinically from osteoid osteoma.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Osteoblastoma is the larger (>2 cm) cousin of osteoid osteoma—look for the posterior vertebral column (laminae and pedicles), absence of reactive cortical bone, and lack of NSAID response.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Back pain from a >2 cm bone-forming lesion in the laminae and pedicles without surrounding cortical sclerosis and unresponsive to NSAIDs points directly to osteoblastoma.

LO 1.04 • Malignant Bone Tumor

Osteosarcoma

Peak Age & Site Distal femur and proximal tibia
Radiologic Hallmark Codman triangle
Microscopic Hallmark Highly atypical nuclei producing osteoid
Primary Molecular Driver RB1 gene mutation; p53, CDKN2A
Germline Risk Multiplier 1000-fold increased risk

Malignant Osteoid-Producing Sarcoma Near the Adolescent Growth Plate

LO 1.04 • Lecture Scenario & Case 3 (Q3)
Epidemiology & Tumor Suppressor Genetics
  • Epidemiology & Timing: The most common primary malignant bone tumor; 75% occur before age 20 (peak during the adolescent growth spurt), though it also occurs in older adults; 1.6:1 male-to-female ratio.
  • Location: Arises in the distal femur and proximal tibia near the growth plate (Case 3: 14-year-old male with left knee pain and an open growth plate; lecture scenario: 12-year-old boy with knee pain).
  • RB1 Pathway: RB1 gene mutation is present in up to 70% of sporadic osteosarcoma; a germline RB1 mutation confers a 1000-fold increased risk. Rb acts as a negative regulator of the G1-S checkpoint of the cell cycle (tumor suppressor). Additional drivers include p53 and CDKN2A.
Radiology, Gross Invasion & Histology
  • Codman Triangle & Invasion: Tumor breaks through and invades the cortex, lifting the periosteum to form a Codman triangle (lifted periosteum and reactive bone), invades the medullary cavity, and forms soft tissue masses.
  • Case 3 Gross Specimen: Coronal cut surface of the proximal tibia shows tumor within the medulla invading across the cortex into surrounding soft tissue while the growth plate remains open.
  • Diagnostic Histology: Malignant cells with highly atypical nuclei directly producing osteoid (pink extracellular matrix), in stark contrast to benign non-tumor woven bone.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“On boards and slides, osteosarcoma is defined by malignant cells with highly atypical nuclei surrounded by pink osteoid matrix—not just benign woven bone. Remember the 1000-fold risk with germline RB1.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A teenager with knee pain during a growth spurt whose radiograph shows a Codman triangle and soft tissue extension likely harbors loss of the G1-S checkpoint regulator RB1 (or p53 / CDKN2A).

LO 2.01 • Benign Cartilage

Osteochondroma

Prevalence & Ratio Most common benign bone tumor; M:F 3:1
Anatomic Location Meta-diaphyseal junction near growth plate
Gross Shape Mushroom shaped bony stalk
Microscopic Cap Hyaline cartilage cap resembling growth plate

Cartilage-Capped Exostosis Near Long Bone Growth Plates

LO 2.01 • Case 4 (Q4) & Lecture Slides
Demographics & Gross Morphology
  • Incidence & Demographics: The most common benign tumor of bone, presenting in late adolescence or early adulthood with a 3:1 male-to-female ratio; 85% are sporadic and solitary.
  • Location & Size: Develops at the meta-diaphyseal junction near the growth plate of long bones (also pelvis, scapula, ribs), measuring 1–20 cm.
  • Case 4 (Q4) X-Ray: 12-year-old male with a left knee mass showing a mushroom-shaped mass projecting from the distal femur.
Histopathology of Stalk & Cap
  • Outer Cap: Capped by a benign hyaline cartilage cap that histologically resembles the growth plate.
  • Inner Core: Beneath the cartilaginous cap, benign bone forms the inner portion of the head and bony stalk.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Look for the classic mushroom shape attached by a bony stalk with an outer cartilaginous cap that looks just like a normal growth plate.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A teenage boy with a painless, slow-growing mushroom-shaped mass near the distal femur growth plate has a benign osteochondroma (85% solitary, 15% syndromic).

LO 2.02 • Hereditary Syndrome

Multiple Hereditary Exostoses

Inheritance Pattern Autosomal dominant
Mutated Genes EXT1 or EXT2 loss of function
Biochemical Defect Heparan sulfate biosynthesis
Malignant Risk Secondary chondrosarcoma

Autosomal Dominant EXT1/EXT2 Loss & Heparan Sulfate Dysregulation

LO 2.02 • Lecture Genetics
Molecular Pathogenesis
  • Inheritance & Genes: Inherited in an autosomal dominant manner due to loss-of-function mutations in EXT1 or EXT2.
  • Enzyme Function: EXT1 and EXT2 encode glycosyltransferases required for heparan sulfate biosynthesis.
  • Cellular Consequence: Defective heparan sulfate causes chondrocyte progenitors in the growth plate to proliferate abnormally, producing multiple bilateral osteochondromas (e.g., 12-year-old male radiograph).
Malignant Transformation Link
  • Secondary Chondrosarcoma: Unlike solitary sporadic chondrosarcomas (which carry IDH1/IDH2 mutations), chondrosarcoma arising in the setting of multiple osteochondroma syndrome carries an EXT gene mutation.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Connect EXT1 and EXT2 directly to glycosyltransferases in heparan sulfate biosynthesis—when lost, growth plate chondrocyte progenitors proliferate abnormally into bilateral exostoses.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A child with multiple bilateral bony stalks around the knees has an autosomal dominant defect in heparan sulfate biosynthesis (EXT1/EXT2) and increased risk for chondrosarcoma.

LO 2.03 • Benign Cartilage

Enchondroma

Age & Compartment 20–50 yrs; medullary cavity
Classic Site Hands and feet (phalanges)
Radiology & Size Circumscribed lucency, intact cortex, <3 cm
Molecular Driver IDH1 or IDH2 mutation

Intramedullary Hyaline Cartilage Lobules of the Hands & Feet

LO 2.03 • Case 5 (Q5) & Lecture Slides
Clinical Presentation & Radiology
  • Demographics & Presentation: Benign tumor of hyaline cartilage occurring in patients 20–50 years old; may present with pain or a pathologic fracture.
  • Location: Arises inside the medullary cavity, usually in the hands and feet (also femur and humerus).
  • Case 5 (Q5) X-Ray: 25-year-old male with left middle finger pain showing a circumscribed lucency in the proximal phalanx with an intact cortex (usually smaller than 3 cm).
Pathology & IDH Driver
  • Microscopic Findings: Sharply circumscribed lobules of proliferating chondrocytes producing mature hyaline cartilage.
  • Genetics: Driven by IDH1 or IDH2 mutation.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Unlike osteochondroma on the surface, enchondroma sits inside the medullary cavity—think small bones of the hands and feet, circumscribed lucency with intact cortex, and IDH1/IDH2 mutations.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A young adult with a pathologic fracture through a well-demarcated lytic lesion in a phalanx containing lobules of hyaline cartilage harbors a mutation in IDH1 or IDH2.

LO 2.04 • Mosaic Syndromes

Ollier & Maffucci Diseases

Shared Mechanism Somatic mosaicism for IDH mutation
Ollier Phenotype Multiple enchondromas + deformities
Maffucci Differentiator Multiple hemangiomas
Associated CNS Tumor Glioma (Maffucci)

Post-Zygotic Somatic Mosaic IDH Enchondromatosis Syndromes

LO 2.04 • Case 5 Q6 & Lecture Slides
Somatic Mosaicism Mechanism
  • Post-Zygotic Timing: Both syndromes are caused by somatic mosaicism for IDH mutation—an early post-zygotic event that affects only some tissues, leaving some somatic cells mutated and others wild type.
  • Childhood Presentation: Both generally present in childhood with two to hundreds of multiple enchondromas.
Ollier vs. Maffucci Discriminators
  • Ollier Disease: Presents with multiple enchondromas (e.g., proximal femur cut surface with multiple nodules) causing limb deformities, without vascular tumors.
  • Maffucci Disease (Q6): Combines multiple enchondromas with multiple hemangiomas (e.g., multiple hemangiomas of the foot) and carries risk of other tumors such as glioma.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Somatic mosaicism means an early post-zygotic IDH mutation affects only some somatic cells while others remain wild type. Add multiple hemangiomas and glioma risk to distinguish Maffucci from Ollier.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A child with asymmetric limb deformities from dozens of medullary cartilage tumors plus soft-tissue hemangiomas of the foot has Maffucci disease due to somatic mosaicism for IDH.

LO 2.05 • Malignant Cartilage

Chondrosarcoma

Age & Axial Site 30s+; pelvis, shoulders, ribs
Radiologic Hallmark Rings and arcs (popcorn) calcification
Sporadic Genetics IDH1/IDH2 + CDKN2A methylation
Diagnostic Rule Correlate with malignant radiology

Axial Malignant Cartilage Tumor Requiring Radiologic-Pathologic Correlation

LO 2.05 • Case 6 (Q7) & Lecture Slides
Clinical & Radiologic Features (Case 6 / Q7)
  • Demographics & Site: Malignant cartilage-producing tumor affecting adults in their 30s and above (more common in men) in the axial skeleton (pelvis, shoulders, ribs).
  • Case 6 Vignette: 58-year-old female with a right hip mass; X-ray shows a large exophytic lesion of the right pelvis extending into soft tissues.
  • Calcification Pattern: Characteristic white rings and arcs mineralization pattern, also called popcorn calcification.
Genetics & Diagnostic Caveat
  • Molecular Pathways: Sporadic tumors harbor IDH1 or IDH2 mutations and CDKN2A silencing by DNA methylation; tumors arising from multiple osteochondroma syndrome harbor EXT mutations.
  • Critical Pathology Rule: Histology shows numerous lobules of proliferating cartilage producing hyaline cartilage, but diagnosis on histology alone is difficult and must be made in the context of malignant-appearing radiology.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Note carefully: the diagnosis of chondrosarcoma on histology alone is difficult! Always rely on malignant-appearing radiology—such as a large pelvic mass with rings-and-arcs calcification extending into soft tissue.”

Second-Order Board Clue

An older adult with a painful pelvic mass showing rings and arcs calcification and lobules of hyaline cartilage on biopsy likely exhibits IDH1/IDH2 mutation plus CDKN2A silencing.

LO 3.01 • Malignant Round Cell

Ewing Sarcoma

Age & Zone <20 yrs; diaphysis (femur, fibula, humerus)
Radiologic Reaction Onion-skin appearance
Microscopic Hallmark Small round blue cell tumor
Confirmatory Translocation t(11;22) EWS-FLI1 fusion
Clinical Mimic Osteomyelitis (fever, ESR, WBC)

Diaphyseal Translocation-Driven Small Round Blue Cell Sarcoma

LO 3.01 • Lecture Scenario & Case 7 (Q8–Q9)
Clinical Presentation & Radiology
  • Epidemiology & Mimicry: Second most common bone sarcoma in children (<20 years old). Can cause systemic symptoms—fever, elevated ESR, anemia, leukocytosis—requiring distinction from osteomyelitis.
  • Location & Spread: Usually arises in the diaphysis of long bones (femur, fibula, humerus; also flat bones), invading from the medullary cavity through cortex and periosteum into soft tissue.
  • Imaging Scenarios: Lecture case (10yo boy, fibula diaphysis) shows layers of reactive bone (onion-skin appearance) with soft tissue extension; Case 7 (12yo male, right arm pain) shows a humerus diaphysis tumor.
Histopathology & Molecular Confirmation (Q9)
  • Histology: Primitive small round blue cell tumor composed of uniformly round, mitotically active nuclei with small amounts of cleared cytoplasm.
  • Pathogenesis & Confirmation (Q9): Driven by EWSR1 translocation, usually partnered with FLI1 via t(11;22), generating the EWS-FLI1 fusion protein causing uncontrolled growth. Studies for t(11;22) confirm the diagnosis.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Always include osteomyelitis in the differential when a child presents with fever, high ESR, and leukocytosis—and confirm Ewing sarcoma with molecular studies for the t(11;22) EWS-FLI1 translocation.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A 12-year-old boy with a diaphyseal lesion showing an onion-skin periosteal reaction and sheets of uniform round blue cells with cleared cytoplasm should be tested for t(11;22).

LO 3.02 • Locally Aggressive

Giant Cell Tumor of Bone

Age & Skeleton Status 3rd–5th decade; closed growth plates
Anatomic Zone Epiphysis/metaphysis of knee
Neoplastic Cell Driver Mononuclear stromal cells (H3F3A)
Paracrine Signaling High RANKL recruits osteoclasts
Key Negative Clue Giant cells are NOT neoplastic

RANKL-Driven Epiphyseal Lytic Tumor of the Mature Skeleton

LO 3.02 • Case 8 (Q10) & Lecture Slides
Clinical Behavior & Zone Rule (Case 8 / Q10)
  • Behavior: Benign but causes local bone destruction with a high recurrence rate; locally aggressive and only rarely metastasizes.
  • Demographics & Site: Occurs in the 3rd–5th decade in a mature skeleton (growth plates closed), classically as a lytic lesion in the epiphysis/metaphysis of the knee (Case 8: 40yo female, left knee pain, lytic proximal tibia).
  • Diagnostic Rule: Difficult to diagnose on histology alone; the characteristic end-of-bone location in a skeletally mature adult is key.
Dual Cell Biology & RANKL Circuit
  • True Neoplastic Population: Mononuclear stromal cells (round-to-oval nuclei, moderate pink cytoplasm, collagen background) harbor an H3F3A mutation coding for an H3 family histone protein.
  • RANKL Recruitment: Neoplastic stromal cells secrete high levels of RANKL, driving proliferation of multinucleated osteoclasts (which are reactive, not neoplastic), alongside macrophages and a surrounding fibrous reaction.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Don’t be fooled by the name: the giant multinucleated osteoclasts are NOT the neoplastic cells! The H3F3A-mutated mononuclear stromal cells drive the tumor by pumping out RANKL.”

Second-Order Board Clue

In a 40-year-old with a lytic lesion reaching the epiphysis of the proximal tibia, the proliferating mononuclear stromal cells carry an H3F3A histone mutation and overexpress RANKL.

LO 3.03 • Incidental Fibrous Lesion

Nonossifying Fibroma

Age & Course Adolescents; spontaneous resolution
Radiologic Hallmark Metaphyseal lucency with sclerotic margin
Microscopic Pattern Storiform pattern spindle cells
Genetics / Syndrome KRAS / FGFR1; NF1
Management Rule Do not usually undergo biopsy

Self-Resolving Metaphyseal Spindle Cell Lesion with Sclerotic Margin

LO 3.03 • Case 9 (Q11) & Lecture Slides
Clinical Course, Imaging & Management
  • Presentation & Evolution: Benign spindle cell tumor in adolescents arising in the metaphyseal region of the distal femur or proximal tibia; usually an incidental finding that undergoes spontaneous resolution over time.
  • Case 9 (Q11) X-Ray: 14-year-old female with knee pain; proximal tibia shows a metaphyseal lucency bordered by a white sclerotic margin.
  • No-Biopsy Rule: These lesions do not usually undergo biopsy and are diagnosed on clinical context and radiology alone (importance: distinguish from malignant tumors).
Storiform Histology & Genetics
  • Teaching Slide Histology: Spindled cells arranged focally in a storiform pattern (resembling a cartwheel or whorled pattern) mixed with scattered osteoclast-type giant cells.
  • Mutations & NF1 Association: Sporadic lesions harbor KRAS and FGFR1 gene mutations; patients with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) may develop nonossifying fibroma as part of their tumor syndrome.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Remember that storiform means a cartwheel or whorled pattern. In real practice, nonossifying fibromas do not usually get biopsied—recognizing the benign sclerotic-rimmed metaphyseal lucency avoids mistaking it for cancer.”

Second-Order Board Clue

An incidental metaphyseal lucency with a sclerotic margin in a teenager—especially one with NF1 or sporadic KRAS/FGFR1 mutation—requires observation for spontaneous resolution rather than biopsy.

LO 3.04 • Fibro-Osseous Lesion

Fibrous Dysplasia

Molecular Cause (Q13) Somatic mosaicism for GNAS1
Microscopic Hallmark Woven bone in fibrous background
Anatomic Patterns Monostotic vs. Polyostotic
Syndrome Association Mazabraud syndrome

GNAS1 Mosaic Disorganized Woven Bone in Fibrous Stroma

LO 3.04 • Case 10 (Q12–Q13) & Lecture Slides
Clinical Patterns & Mazabraud Syndrome
  • Definition & Age: Benign developmental lesion composed of disorganized bone and fibrous tissue, presenting in childhood or adolescence.
  • Distribution Patterns: Occurs in monostotic (single bone) or polyostotic (multiple bones) forms.
  • Mazabraud Syndrome: Defined as polyostotic fibrous dysplasia plus soft tissue myxomas.
Case 10 Imaging, Histology & Genetics (Q12–Q13)
  • Case 10 (Q12) Vignette: 6-year-old male with leg length discrepancy; lateral tibia X-ray demonstrates two lucent lesions in the proximal and distal medulla.
  • Histopathology: Trabeculae of woven bone embedded within a fibrous tissue background.
  • Etiology (Q13): Caused by somatic mosaicism for the GNAS1 gene.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Pair Mazabraud syndrome with polyostotic fibrous dysplasia plus soft tissue myxomas, and know that somatic mosaicism for GNAS1 drives the disorganized woven bone in fibrous stroma.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A 6-year-old boy with leg length discrepancy, multiple medullary lucencies, and woven bone in a fibrous background has fibrous dysplasia caused by somatic mosaicism for GNAS1.

LO 3.05 • Secondary Malignancy

Skeletal Metastases

Axial Skeleton Targets Vertebrae, pelvis, skull
Appendicular Targets Femur and humerus
Key Anatomical Principle Central / proximal red marrow sites
Contrast vs. Enchondroma Spare distal hands & feet

Anatomic Distribution of Skeletal Metastases

LO 3.05 • Lecture Slide
Predilection Sites
  • Axial Skeleton: Most frequently involve the vertebrae, pelvis, and skull.
  • Proximal Long Bones: Also commonly involve the femur and humerus.
Spatial Diagnostic Contrast
  • Distal vs. Central Pattern: Whereas benign enchondromas favor the small bones of the hands and feet, skeletal metastases localize to the central axial skeleton (vertebrae, pelvis, skull) and proximal long bones (femur, humerus).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Memorize the five classic skeletal metastasis sites covered in lecture: vertebrae, pelvis, femur, humerus, and skull.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Multifocal bone lesions involving the vertebrae, pelvis, femur, humerus, or skull in an older patient should immediately prompt evaluation for skeletal metastases.
