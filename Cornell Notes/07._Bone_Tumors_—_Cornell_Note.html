First-Line Regimen / Complication

Osteoid Osteoma — Cortical Nidus < 2 cm

Femur / Tibia Cortex (~50%)

Clinical & Radiologic Correlation

Predominantly affects males in their teens and twenties (e.g., 10-year-old boy with 3 months of left thigh pain; Case 2: 20-year-old male with right leg pain). Severe nocturnal pain wakes the patient from sleep and resolves reliably with ibuprofen. AP and lateral radiographs plus CT show a small radiolucent cortical clearing (< 2 cm nidus, meaning "nest") surrounded by thick, white reactive cortical bone.

Histopathologic Architecture

Gross cross-section reveals a hemorrhagic central nidus encircled by sclerotic reactive bone. High-power microscopy demonstrates interconnecting trabeculae of woven bone (disorganized collagen matrix, in contrast to parallel lamellar bone) lined cleanly by a single layer of benign osteoblasts (osteoblastic rimming).

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Angelica Oviedo, MD)

Seeing osteoblastic rimming around woven bone trabeculae is a cornerstone morphologic sign that generally points toward a benign lesion rather than osteosarcoma. Both AP and lateral views help confirm cortical localization and visualize the radiolucent nidus clearly.

Second-Order Board Clues

Young male + nocturnal long-bone pain relieved by ibuprofen → look for a cortical lucency < 2 cm → expect disorganized woven bone trabeculae rimmed by benign osteoblasts.

Osteoblastoma

Benign
Molecular Driver
Clinical Hallmark
Diagnostic Identifier
Key Negative Clue
First-Line Regimen / Complication

Osteoblastoma — Posterior Vertebral Lesion > 2 cm

Refractory to NSAIDs

Anatomic & Radiologic Contrast

Although histologically similar to osteoid osteoma, osteoblastoma is defined by a diameter larger than 2 cm and preferentially involves the posterior vertebral column, laminae, and pedicles. Radiographs typically lack the thick surrounding reactive cortical bone characteristic of osteoid osteoma.

Clinical & Pathologic Differentiation

Unlike osteoid osteoma, pain from osteoblastoma does not usually respond to NSAIDs. Microscopically, trabeculae of woven bone show benign osteoblastic rimming without malignant pleomorphism, distinguishing it from osteosarcoma.

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Angelica Oviedo, MD)

Think of osteoblastoma as the "big axial cousin" of osteoid osteoma: same woven bone with benign osteoblastic rimming, but > 2 cm, sited in the posterior spine (laminae/pedicles), lacking reactive cortical bone, and unresponsive to NSAIDs.

Second-Order Board Clues

Histology shows benign woven bone + osteoblastic rimming, but the vignette specifies a 3.5 cm posterior vertebral pedicle mass refractory to ibuprofen → Osteoblastoma, not Osteoid Osteoma.

First-Line Regimen / Complication

Osteosarcoma — Malignant Osteoid-Producing Sarcoma

Most Common Primary Malignant Bone Tumor

Epidemiology, Radiology & Gross Pathology

Bimodal age distribution: ~75% occur before age 20 during the adolescent growth spurt (male predominance), with a second peak in older adults (>60 yrs, associated with Paget disease or prior radiation). Arises in the metaphysis of the distal femur or proximal tibia near the open growth plate. Radiographs demonstrate mineralized tumor breaking through the cortex into soft tissues (sunburst/spiculated extension) and lifting the periosteum to form a Codman triangle. Gross cut surfaces (Case 3) confirm medullary origin invading across cortex into soft tissue.

RB1 Two-Hit Pathogenesis & Histology

RB1 loss occurs in ~70% of sporadic cases, and germline RB1 mutation (retinoblastoma tumor syndrome via the two-hit hypothesis) confers a 1,000-fold increased risk of osteosarcoma; TP53 and CDKN2A are also mutated. In quiescent cells, hypophosphorylated RB binds E2F to block S-phase gene transcription; RB phosphorylation or loss releases E2F to drive unchecked mitosis. Histologically, sheets of large, atypical, pleomorphic malignant cells directly deposit unmineralized pink extracellular osteoid matrix.

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Angelica Oviedo, MD)

Always compare cellularity! Osteosarcoma is extremely cellular with atypical nuclei crowded around strands of pink osteoid, whereas non-tumor woven bone repairing a fracture has abundant matrix separating widely spaced, bland nuclei.

Second-Order Board Clues

Childhood survivor of bilateral retinoblastoma develops knee pain at age 13 with Codman triangle → germline RB1 two-hit loss → loss of E2F transcriptional arrest → malignant cells producing osteoid.

Osteochondroma

Benign
Molecular Driver
Clinical Hallmark
Diagnostic Identifier
Key Negative Clue
First-Line Regimen / Complication

Osteochondroma (Exostosis)

Metaphyseal Mushroom Stalk

Radiology & Gross Morphology

Presents as a lateral bony projection near the metaphysis of long bones (Case 4: 12-year-old boy with a left knee mass showing a mushroom-shaped mass of the distal femur). The defining radiologic hallmark is continuous cortex and marrow cavity extending directly from the host bone into the stalk.

Histology & Malignant Threshold

Covered externally by a benign hyaline cartilage cap resembling an organized growth plate undergoing endochondral ossification, while bone forms the inner portion of the head and stalk. A thickened cartilage cap (>1.5–2 cm in adults) raises suspicion for secondary chondrosarcoma.

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Angelica Oviedo, MD)

Recognize the classic mushroom-shaped X-ray contour on the distal femur and confirm the two-layer architecture: outer benign hyaline cartilage cap overlying inner mature bone of the head and stalk.

Second-Order Board Clues

12-year-old with painless distal femur projection continuous with host marrow → benign hyaline cartilage cap over bony stalk; rapid cap growth (>2 cm) after skeletal maturity → secondary chondrosarcoma.

Multiple Hereditary Exostoses

Syndromic
Molecular Driver
Clinical Hallmark
Diagnostic Identifier
Key Negative Clue
First-Line Regimen / Complication

Multiple Hereditary Exostoses (Multiple Osteochondroma Syndrome)

EXT1 / EXT2 Mutation

Pathogenesis & Inheritance

Caused by mutations in EXT genes (EXT1/EXT2), which impair heparan sulfate synthesis and disrupt normal chondrocyte proliferation polarity near the growth plate, producing multiple bilateral osteochondromas during childhood.

Malignant Progression Risk

Lecture radiographs show multiple bilateral osteochondromas around the knees of a 12-year-old male. Crucially, as noted in Dr. Oviedo's Chondrosarcoma slide, secondary chondrosarcoma arising in multiple osteochondroma syndrome carries an EXT gene mutation.

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Angelica Oviedo, MD)

Connect Learning Objective 3 directly to Chondrosarcoma genetics: sporadic chondrosarcomas carry IDH1/IDH2 mutations, whereas chondrosarcoma arising from multiple osteochondroma syndrome is driven by an EXT gene mutation.

Second-Order Board Clues

Adolescent with bilateral knee exostoses develops painful enlarging cartilage cap > 2 cm in adulthood → secondary chondrosarcoma → look for EXT1/EXT2 mutation rather than IDH1.

Enchondroma — Intramedullary Hyaline Cartilage Tumor

20–50 Years • Hands & Feet

Location & Imaging Features

Benign tumor of mature hyaline cartilage situated inside the medullary cavity, classically in patients aged 20–50 years. Most commonly affects the small tubular bones of the hands and feet (lecture slide & Case 5: 25-year-old male with left middle finger pain and lucency of the proximal/middle phalanx), though also found in the femur and humerus. May show punctate rings-and-arcs calcification within the medullary space.

Histology & IDH Biochemistry

Lobules of benign-appearing hyaline cartilage with bland chondrocytes in lacunae enclosed within marrow fat/trabeculae, without cortical destruction. Driven by somatic IDH1/IDH2 gain-of-function mutations generating 2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG), which alters epigenetic DNA/histone methylation.

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Angelica Oviedo, MD)

Case 5 reinforces the classic hand presentation: a young adult (25 years old) with a lucent medullary lesion in a phalanx that does not breach cortex—benign intramedullary hyaline cartilage.

Second-Order Board Clues

25-year-old with lucent lesion inside the middle phalanx + no soft tissue extension → Enchondroma → share IDH1/IDH2 driver mechanism with Ollier/Maffucci and sporadic chondrosarcoma.

Ollier & Maffucci

Somatic Mosaic
Molecular Driver
Clinical Hallmark
Diagnostic Identifier
Key Negative Clue
First-Line Regimen / Complication

Ollier Disease & Maffucci Disease (Multiple Enchondromatosis)

Post-Zygotic IDH Mosaicism

Ollier vs. Maffucci Clinical Distinction

Both present in childhood with anywhere from two to hundreds of intramedullary enchondromas causing severe limb deformities (demonstrated on proximal femur cut surface in lecture). Ollier disease manifests solely with multiple enchondromas; Maffucci disease combines multiple enchondromas with multiple soft tissue hemangiomas (e.g., vascular lesions of the foot) and elevates risk for glioma and chondrosarcoma.

Post-Zygotic Somatic Mosaicism

Both syndromes (Case 5 Q6) result from somatic mosaicism for an IDH mutation. Because the mutation arises as an early post-zygotic event, it affects only certain tissues/cell lineages—some somatic cells harbor the mutant IDH allele while others remain wild type.

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Angelica Oviedo, MD)

Memorize the exact Case 5 Q6 answer: Ollier = multiple enchondromas; Maffucci = multiple enchondromas + multiple hemangiomas (plus glioma risk); both are caused by somatic mosaicism for an IDH mutation.

Second-Order Board Clues

Child with asymmetric limb deformity, multiple hand/femur cartilaginous lucencies, and compressible bluish vascular nodules on the foot → Maffucci disease → post-zygotic somatic mosaicism (not autosomal dominant).

Chondrosarcoma — Axial Malignant Cartilage Neoplasm

Pelvis, Shoulders, Ribs

Epidemiology, Axial Location & Radiology

Malignant cartilage-producing tumor affecting adults 30s and older, more common in men (Case 6: 58-year-old female with right hip mass). Favors the axial skeleton: pelvis, shoulders, and ribs. Radiographs show a large destructive mass extending into soft tissues with endosteal scalloping and characteristic white rings and arcs ("popcorn") calcification.

Genetics & Diagnostic Integration

Sporadic tumors harbor IDH1 or IDH2 mutations alongside CDKN2A silencing by DNA methylation; tumors arising in multiple osteochondroma syndrome harbor EXT mutations. Histologically, sheets of atypical chondrocytes produce hyaline cartilage and infiltrate marrow spaces.

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Angelica Oviedo, MD)

Critical Caveat: Diagnosis of chondrosarcoma on histology alone is difficult because well-differentiated cartilage can mimic enchondroma. Diagnosis must be made in the context of malignant-appearing radiology (cortical breakthrough, soft tissue extension, rings-and-arcs calcification).

Second-Order Board Clues

58-year-old with large right pelvic mass, white rings-and-arcs calcification, and atypical hyaline cartilage infiltrating marrow → Chondrosarcoma → look for IDH1/IDH2 + epigenetic CDKN2A methylation.

Ewing Sarcoma

Malignant
Molecular Driver
Clinical Hallmark
Diagnostic Identifier
Key Negative Clue
First-Line Regimen / Complication

Ewing Sarcoma — Diaphyseal Small Round Blue Cell Tumor

t(11;22) EWS-FLI1

Clinical Mimicry & Diaphyseal Radiology

Second most common bone sarcoma in children (<20 years old; Case 7: 12-year-old male with right arm pain). Uniquely causes systemic inflammatory symptoms—fever, elevated ESR, anemia, and leukocytosis—requiring differentiation from osteomyelitis. Arises in the medullary cavity of the diaphysis (femur, fibula, humerus, flat bones), invading cortex into soft tissue and producing concentric "onion-skin" layers of reactive periosteal bone.

Histology & Translocation Confirmation

Sheets of uniformly round, mitotically active primitive blue cells with small amounts of cleared (glycogen-rich) cytoplasm. Driven by the t(11;22) translocation forming the EWS-FLI1 fusion protein, causing uncontrolled growth. Confirm diagnosis with molecular/cytogenetic detection of t(11;22) or EWS-FLI1 (Case 7 Q9).

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Angelica Oviedo, MD)

Any child or teen with bone pain + fever, high ESR, anemia, and leukocytosis AND a diaphyseal onion-skin lesion must trigger workup for Ewing sarcoma—not just osteomyelitis.

Second-Order Board Clues

12-year-old with arm pain, onion-skin periosteal layering, and cleared-cytoplasm small round blue cells → next step to confirm diagnosis → FISH/PCR for t(11;22) EWS-FLI1.

Giant Cell Tumor of Bone — Mature Epiphysis/Metaphysis

Closed Growth Plates

Age, Maturity & Epiphyseal Localization

Benign but locally aggressive with a high recurrence rate (rarely metastasizes). Occurs in the 3rd through 5th decade in a mature skeleton (growth plates closed)—e.g., Case 8: 40-year-old female with left knee pain. Radiographs reveal an eccentric lytic ("soap-bubble") lesion in the epiphysis/metaphysis region of the knee (proximal tibia / distal femur).

RANKL Mechanism & Cell Composition

Composed of three cell types: mononuclear stromal cells (round-to-oval nuclei, moderate pink cytoplasm, collagen background), multinucleated osteoclasts, and macrophages. Crucially, the mononuclear stromal cells are the neoplastic drivers carrying an H3F3A mutation (H3 histone family) and secreting high levels of RANKL, which drives proliferation of the non-neoplastic multinucleated osteoclasts (targetable by RANKL inhibition with denosumab).

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Angelica Oviedo, MD)

Difficult to diagnose by histology alone because giant cells appear in other lesions (like NOF)! The decisive clue is location at the end of a bone (epiphysis) in a mature skeleton (closed growth plates).

Second-Order Board Clues

40-year-old with lytic proximal tibia epiphyseal lesion → which cell carries the H3F3A mutation and overexpresses RANKL? → the mononuclear stromal cell, NOT the giant osteoclast.

Nonossifying Fibroma

Benign
Molecular Driver
Clinical Hallmark
Diagnostic Identifier
Key Negative Clue
First-Line Regimen / Complication

Nonossifying Fibroma — Adolescent Storiform Metaphyseal Lesion

Open Growth Plates

Clinical & Radiologic Features

Benign spindle cell lesion of the metaphyseal region of the distal femur or proximal tibia in adolescents (Case 9: 14-year-old female with knee pain). Commonly discovered as an incidental finding showing an eccentric, well-marginated metaphyseal lucency with a scalloped sclerotic rim. Driven by KRAS and FGFR1 mutations in sporadic cases.

Histology & Differential Trap

Fibrous spindle cells arranged in a classic storiform ("spiraling away") pattern accompanied by hemosiderin-laden macrophages and interspersed multinucleated giant cells—making its histology closely resemble Giant Cell Tumor of Bone.

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Angelica Oviedo, MD)

Major Distinction: Nonossifying fibroma can look histologically similar to Giant Cell Tumor of Bone, but NOF occurs in an immature skeleton (growth plates open) whereas GCTB occurs in a mature skeleton (growth plates closed).

Second-Order Board Clues

14-year-old with open distal femoral physis, eccentric metaphyseal lesion, and storiform ("spiraling away") spindle cells → Nonossifying Fibroma → check for KRAS / FGFR1 mutations.

Fibrous Dysplasia & GNAS1 Mosaic Syndromes

GNAS1 Gain-of-Function

Pathogenesis, Histology & Radiology

Benign lesion presenting in childhood or adolescence (Case 10: 6-year-old male with leg length discrepancy) composed of disorganized bone and fibrous tissue ("ground-glass" radiopacity). Caused by a somatic mosaic gain-of-function mutation in GNAS1 (constitutive Gs-alpha / adenylyl cyclase / cAMP signaling), which promotes cellular proliferation and stops osteoblast differentiation. Consequently, irregular curvilinear ("Chinese characters") woven bone trabeculae sit in fibrous stroma without osteoblastic rimming.

Monostotic, Polyostotic & Syndromic Variants

  • Monostotic / Polyostotic: Involvement of single vs. multiple bones causing deformity or fracture.
  • Mazabraud Syndrome: Polyostotic fibrous dysplasia + soft tissue myxomas.
  • McCune-Albright Syndrome: Polyostotic fibrous dysplasia + café-au-lait skin pigmentation + endocrine abnormalities (precocious puberty).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Angelica Oviedo, MD)

Remember why patients are somatic mosaics for GNAS1: the gain-of-function mutation blocks osteoblast differentiation, creating arrested woven bone in fibrous tissue; Case 10 Q13 directly tests GNAS1 somatic mosaicism.

Second-Order Board Clues

Girl with precocious puberty, jagged café-au-lait spots, and polyostotic ground-glass bone deformities → McCune-Albright syndrome → activating GNAS1 mutation arresting osteoblast maturation.

Skeletal Metastases — Adult vs. Pediatric Primaries

Axial Skeleton

Adult Primaries & Radiologic Pattern

In adults, metastatic carcinoma to the axial skeleton is far more common than primary bone tumors. Dr. Oviedo's high-yield adult primaries are Lung cancer, Breast cancer, Renal cell carcinoma, and Prostate cancer (alongside thyroid). Prostate metastases characteristically produce osteoblastic (sclerotic) lesions, whereas lung, breast, renal, and thyroid typically yield osteolytic defects.

Pediatric Skeletal Metastases

In children, the most frequent malignancies metastasizing to bone are Neuroblastoma and Wilms tumor—a crucial contrast against adult carcinomas.

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Angelica Oviedo, MD)

Contrast the two age lists explicitly taught in lecture: Adults → Lung, Breast, Renal cell carcinoma, Prostate (lecture image: known breast cancer patient); Children → Neuroblastoma and Wilms tumor.

Second-Order Board Clues

Older man with dense sclerotic (osteoblastic) lumbar vertebrae → Prostate adenocarcinoma; 4-year-old with abdominal mass and periorbital/bone metastases → Neuroblastoma or Wilms tumor.
