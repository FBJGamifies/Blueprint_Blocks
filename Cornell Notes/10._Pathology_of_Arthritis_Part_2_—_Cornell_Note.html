Suppurative (Septic) Arthritis & Common Pathogens

Lecturer Rule: Acute monoarthritis = septic arthritis until proven otherwise; urgent needle arthrocentesis is mandatory.

Synovial Infection Routes & Pathogen Triage Workbench

Select an infectious pathogen to inspect joint entry routes, host risk groups, and synovial fluid thresholds.

LO 1.01 • Pathogenesis

Synovial Vulnerability & Routes of Seeding

Most Common Route Hematogenous seeding
Anatomic Vulnerability Lacks limiting basement membrane
Alternate Entry Direct inoculation / osteomyelitis
Tissue Consequence Cartilage destruction in days

How Microbes Breach the Synovium & Destroy Articular Cartilage

LO 1.01 • Mechanism of Suppurative Arthritis
Routes & Synovial Barrier Defect
  • Primary Route: Hematogenous spread is the single most common route of bacterial joint infection, seeding the richly vascularized synovial membrane during bacteremia.
  • Barrier Vulnerability: Unlike most epithelia, the synovium lacks a limiting basement membrane, which allows circulating microbes to cross easily into the joint space.
  • Non-Hematogenous Routes: Organisms may also enter via direct inoculation (trauma, surgery, or arthrocentesis), contiguous spread from adjacent soft tissue infection, or extension from adjacent osteomyelitis.
Purulent Damage Kinetics
  • Inflammatory Pattern: Bacterial proliferation triggers acute purulent neutrophil-rich inflammation within the synovial cavity.
  • Rapid Chondrolysis: Articular cartilage can be irreversibly destroyed within days because it has poor regenerative capacity once enzymatic and purulent injury occurs.
  • Clinical Urgency: Because cartilage loss becomes permanent within days, delaying joint evacuation or antimicrobial therapy leads to chronic joint damage.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

“Septic arthritis is usually caused by hematogenous bacterial seeding of the synovium and can destroy articular cartilage within days if not promptly treated.”

Second-Order Board Clue

When a question asks why bacteremia readily seeds joints compared with other compartments, select the absence of a limiting basement membrane beneath synovial lining cells.

LO 1.02 • Microbiology

Pathogen Spectrum in Septic Arthritis

Overall #1 Pathogen Staphylococcus aureus
2nd Most Common Streptococcus spp.
Young Sexually Active Neisseria gonorrhoeae
Elderly / Immunocomp. Escherichia coli & Candida albicans
Key Negative Clue Viruses: non-purulent arthritis

Host-Stratified Infectious Causes of Arthritis

LO 1.02 • Bacterial, Fungal & Non-Purulent Contrasts
Common Bacterial Agents
  • Overall Leader: Staphylococcus aureus is the single most common cause of acute bacterial septic arthritis across general populations.
  • Second Place: Streptococcus spp. represent the second most common bacterial etiology.
  • Age & Host Links: Neisseria gonorrhoeae classically infects young, sexually active adults, whereas Gram-negative bacilli such as Escherichia coli target older adults and immunocompromised patients.
Less Common & Non-Purulent Agents
  • Chronic Granulomatous: Mycobacterium tuberculosis produces chronic granulomatous monoarthritis rather than acute purulent effusion.
  • Fungal Arthritis: Candida albicans and other fungi occur primarily in severely immunocompromised hosts.
  • Viral Distinction: Viruses (parvovirus B19, hepatitis B/C, rubella) cause infectious arthritis but typically do not produce purulent septic arthritis.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

“Staphylococcus aureus is the most common cause of acute septic arthritis.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A sexually active 22-year-old with acute knee monoarthritis points to Neisseria gonorrhoeae, whereas an elderly or immunocompromised patient shifts suspicion toward Gram-negative bacilli or Candida spp.

LO 1.03 • Diagnosis & Rx

Clinical Triage, Synovial Fluid & Urgent Drainage

Clinical Hallmark Acute monoarthritis (knee)
Mandatory Diagnostic Synovial fluid aspiration
WBC Threshold >50,000 WBC/µL (PMNs)
Urgent Intervention IV antibiotics + drainage

Diagnostic Arthrocentesis Criteria & Emergency Joint Evacuation

LO 1.03 • Clinical Presentation, Diagnosis & Treatment
Presentation & Mandatory Fluid Workup
  • Clinical Picture: Sudden onset of a painful, swollen, warm, erythematous joint with restricted range of motion, most often monoarticular (classically the knee), accompanied by fever, leukocytosis, and elevated ESR and CRP.
  • Essential Step: Immediate synovial fluid aspiration is mandatory before assuming a crystal flare, followed by Gram stain and culture.
  • Fluid Profile: Purulent, predominantly neutrophilic fluid with >50,000 WBC/µL (frequently exceeding 100,000 WBC/µL).
Dual Emergency Therapy
  • Antimicrobials: Initiate prompt IV antibiotics targeted to Gram stain/suspected host pathogens immediately after synovial fluid sampling.
  • Decompression: Combine antibiotics with immediate joint drainage via needle aspiration or surgical drainage to evacuate proteolytic pus.
  • Delayed Care Risk: Failure to drain and treat promptly results in irreversible articular cartilage destruction.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

“Acute monoarthritis should be presumed septic arthritis until proven otherwise.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Even if a patient has a history of gout or pseudogout, an acutely hot, swollen knee with fever demands synovial fluid aspiration for Gram stain and culture before initiating anti-inflammatory therapy.

TOPIC / LO 2

Mycobacterial, Lyme & Viral Arthritides

Lecturer Rule: Distinguish chronic granulomatous TB monoarthritis and Lyme effusion from acute purulent bacterial arthritis.

Chronic Infectious & Viral Arthritis Discriminator

Switch between mycobacterial, spirochetal (Lyme), and viral arthritides to compare histopathology and clinical patterns.

LO 2.01 • Mycobacterial

Tuberculous (Mycobacterial) Arthritis

Etiology Mycobacterium tuberculosis
Joint Distribution Hip > knee > ankle
Microscopic Hallmark Granulomatous synovitis
Giant Cell Type Langhans-type giant cells
Diagnostic Stain AFB stain (carbol fuchsin)

Chronic Granulomatous Monoarthritis & Acid-Fast Bacilli Staining

LO 2.01 • Mycobacterial Arthritis
Clinical Course & Routes of Spread
  • Clinical Pattern: Caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, presenting as an indolent, chronic progressive monoarthritis rather than an abrupt pyogenic flare.
  • Weight-Bearing Tropism: Most commonly affects large joints in the hierarchy hip > knee > ankle.
  • Pathogenesis: Develops via hematogenous spread from pulmonary TB or direct extension from adjacent tuberculous osteomyelitis.
Histopathology & AFB Mechanism
  • Synovial Histology: Synovial biopsy reveals granulomatous synovitis composed of epithelioid histiocytes and multinucleated Langhans-type giant cells.
  • AFB Stain Principle: Bacteria are stained with carbol fuchsin and washed with acid-alcohol; most organisms decolorize, whereas mycobacteria retain the red dye because of their waxy cell walls.
  • Diagnostic Workup: Confirmed through AFB stain, mycobacterial cultures, and synovial biopsy.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

“Chronic monoarthritis + granulomas + Langhans giant cells = think TB arthritis.”

Second-Order Board Clue

If synovial fluid Gram stain is negative in a patient with chronic hip monoarthritis and a pulmonary lesion, perform a synovial biopsy looking for epithelioid histiocytes and acid-fast bacilli.

LO 2.02 • Spirochetal

Lyme Arthritis (Borrelia burgdorferi)

Etiology & Vector Borrelia burgdorferi / Ixodes ticks
Clinical Hallmark Knee swelling >> pain
Histologic Hallmark Lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate
Diagnostic Testing ELISA → Western blot
First-Line / Refractory Doxycycline / IV ceftriaxone

Late-Manifesting Lyme Knee Effusion, Proliferative Synovitis & Serology

LO 2.02 • Lyme Arthritis
Clinical Presentation & Imaging
  • Transmission & Timing: Transmitted by Ixodes ticks carrying Borrelia burgdorferi; intermittent or persistent monoarthritis/oligoarthritis develops months after infection.
  • Joint & History Clues: Affects the knee far more often than ankle, elbow, or shoulder; may follow erythema migrans, facial nerve palsy, or AV block.
  • Imaging Profile: Radiograph and MRI demonstrate a large knee effusion, synovitis, and enlarged popliteal lymph nodes.
Pathology, Serology & Therapy
  • Synovial Pathology: Chronic proliferative synovitis characterized by papillary synovial hypertrophy, dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates, fibrin deposition, and neovascularization.
  • Diagnostic Strategy: Two-tier serologic testing (ELISA followed by Western blot); PCR may detect Borrelia DNA in synovial fluid.
  • Treatment: Oral doxycycline or amoxicillin first-line; IV ceftriaxone for refractory cases.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

“Marked knee swelling out of proportion to pain is a classic presentation of Lyme arthritis.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A hiker with painless massive unilateral knee effusion, enlarged popliteal lymph nodes, and a prior AV block or seventh nerve palsy should be evaluated with ELISA and Western blot.

LO 2.03 • Viral Mimicker

Viral Arthritis (Parvovirus B19 & Chikungunya)

Major Viruses Parvovirus B19, Chikungunya
Clinical Pattern Symmetric polyarthritis
Target Joints Small joints (hands/wrists)
Pathology & Course Transient; 1–6 weeks
Chronic Exceptions Chikungunya, HBV, HCV, B19

Immune-Mediated Symmetric Polyarthritis Mimicking Rheumatoid Arthritis

LO 2.03 • Viral Arthritis
Viruses & RA-Like Pattern
  • Etiologic Agents: Parvovirus B19, Chikungunya, HBV, HCV, Rubella, and HIV.
  • Joint Distribution: Acute or subacute symmetric polyarthritis involving the small joints of the hands and wrists, closely mimicking rheumatoid arthritis.
  • Associated Systemic Clues: Accompanied by fever, lymphadenopathy, and rash — classically the "slapped-cheek" rash of erythema infectiosum in Parvovirus B19.
Pathology, Course & Chronic Risk
  • Mechanism: Transient immune-mediated synovitis rather than purulent bacterial destruction.
  • Typical Resolution: Self-limited in most cases, resolving within 1–6 weeks; diagnosed via clinical presentation plus virus-specific serology or PCR when indicated.
  • Chronic Exceptions: May become chronic especially with Chikungunya, HBV, HCV, and Parvovirus B19.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

“Acute symmetric polyarthritis of the small joints following a viral illness can mimic rheumatoid arthritis.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A daycare worker or parent with acute symmetric hand and wrist synovitis after a child's slapped-cheek rash has Parvovirus B19, whereas persistent RA-like hand arthritis after tropical travel points to Chikungunya.

TOPIC / LO 3

Gout Pathogenesis, Clinical Stages & Tophus Morphology

Lecturer Rule: Serum uric acid may be normal during an acute gout flare; diagnosis requires polarized light microscopy of synovial fluid.

Gout Pathophysiology, Polarized Birefringence & Tophus Simulator

Step through urate balance, NLRP3 inflammasome signaling, polarized light axes, and tophus morphology.

LO 3.01 • Urate Balance

Hyperuricemia: Underexcretion vs. Overproduction

Biochemical Cutoff Uric acid >6.8 mg/dL
Major Mechanism (~90%) Decreased renal excretion
Overproduction (~10%) Tumor lysis / HGPRT deficiency
Acute Flare Triggers Alcohol, purines, diuretics

Etiologies of Hyperuricemia & Crystal Precipitants

LO 3.01 • Gout Etiology & Risk Factors
Underexcretion (~90% of Cases)
  • Solubility Threshold: Hyperuricemia is defined as serum uric acid >6.8 mg/dL, promoting monosodium urate (MSU) crystal precipitation.
  • Primary Dominance: Approximately 90% of primary gout results from decreased renal uric acid excretion.
  • Secondary Underexcretion: Driven by chronic kidney disease, diuretics, cyclosporine, and dehydration.
Overproduction (~10%) & Triggers
  • High Cell Turnover: Accounts for ~10% of cases, including leukemias and tumor lysis syndrome.
  • Enzyme Defects: HGPRT deficiency causes purine salvage failure — partial deficiency produces Kelley-Seegmiller syndrome, whereas near-complete deficiency produces Lesch-Nyhan syndrome.
  • Clinical Triggers: Alcohol, purine-rich foods, trauma, surgery, dehydration, and diuretics precipitate acute flares.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

“Although ~90% of primary gout results from decreased renal uric acid excretion, serum uric acid may be normal during an acute attack.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Link gout in a transplant recipient to cyclosporine-mediated renal underexcretion, and link juvenile severe hyperuricemia to Lesch-Nyhan syndrome (near-complete HGPRT deficiency).

LO 3.02 • Inflammasome & Optics

NLRP3 Activation & Polarized MSU Identification

Molecular Driver NLRP3 inflammasome → IL-1β
Gold Standard Test Polarized light microscopy
Crystal Shape Needle-shaped MSU crystals
Birefringence Negatively birefringent
Key Negative Clue Serum urate may be normal

Innate Immune Signaling & Compensator Axis Optics

LO 3.02 • Pathogenesis & Crystal Gold Standard
NLRP3-Mediated Joint Inflammation
  • Central Signaling Step: Deposited MSU crystals activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, driving caspase-mediated IL-1β release.
  • Leukocyte Recruitment: IL-1β recruits waves of neutrophils into the synovium, producing neutrophilic inflammation and acute cartilage damage.
  • Serum Pitfall: Because urate shifts or drops transiently during acute flares, serum uric acid may be normal during an acute attack and cannot exclude gout.
Polarized Light Gold Standard
  • Diagnostic Rule: Synovial fluid crystal analysis under polarized light microscopy is the diagnostic gold standard for all crystal-induced arthritides.
  • MSU Morphology: Crystals are slender and needle-shaped (intracellular or extracellular in synovial fluid).
  • Axis Orientation: MSU crystals exhibit negative birefringence — appearing yellow when parallel to the slow compensator axis and blue when perpendicular.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

“Needle + Negative = Gout. Monosodium urate crystals are yellow when parallel to the axis and blue when perpendicular, opposite of calcium pyrophosphate.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A normal serum urate level in a patient with an excruciatingly painful swollen 1st MTP joint does NOT rule out gout; aspirate the joint for needle-shaped, negatively birefringent crystals and recognize NLRP3 inflammasome activation.

LO 3.03 • Clinical Stages

Four Clinical Stages of Gout Progression

Stage 1 Asymptomatic hyperuricemia
Stage 2 Hallmark 1st MTP podagra
Stage 3 Intercritical gout
Stage 4 Radiology Punched-out erosions

Natural History from Silent Hyperuricemia to Chronic Tophaceous Destruction

LO 3.03 • Four Clinical Stages & Radiology
Stages 1–3: Early & Recurrent Disease
  • 1. Asymptomatic Hyperuricemia: Elevated serum uric acid without clinical arthritis symptoms.
  • 2. Acute Gouty Arthritis: Sudden onset of severe, warm, intensely painful monoarthritis — classically affecting the 1st metatarsophalangeal joint (podagra), lasting hours to weeks with complete resolution between attacks.
  • 3. Intercritical Gout: Symptom-free interval between acute flares; over time, recurrent attacks become more frequent and eventually polyarticular.
Stage 4: Chronic Tophaceous Radiology
  • 4. Chronic Tophaceous Gout: Develops after years of recurrent attacks as tophi accumulate in joints and periarticular soft tissues, causing progressive cartilage and bone destruction and deformity.
  • Radiographic Hallmark: Classic "punched-out" (rat-bite) erosions with overhanging edges adjacent to soft-tissue tophi.
  • Gross Finding: Soft-tissue tophi may ulcerate and extrude chalky-white urate material while eroding underlying bone.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

“Podagra is the classic initial presentation of gout. Chronic gout causes 'punched-out' erosions with overhanging edges and soft-tissue tophi on radiographs.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Differentiate chronic gout radiographs from rheumatoid arthritis by identifying juxta-articular rat-bite erosions with overhanging edges and eccentric soft-tissue tophi.

LO 3.04 • Histology & Rx

Tophus Morphology & Acute vs. Chronic Therapy

Tophus Center (H&E) Pale dissolved clefts
Cellular Rim Foreign-body giant cells
Processing Artifact Formalin dissolves urate
Acute Flare Rx NSAIDs, colchicine, steroids
Chronic Urate-Lowering Allopurinol, febuxostat

Tophus Zonal Architecture, Formalin Artifact & Pharmacologic Strategy

LO 3.04 • Tophus Histology & Gout Management
Zonal Histology of a Gouty Tophus
  • Central Zone: Appears as pale amorphous spaces because routine formalin processing dissolves urate crystals during tissue fixation.
  • Middle Cellular Rim: A purple inflammatory rim of macrophages and foreign-body giant cells creates a granulomatous reaction around the dissolved urate deposits.
  • Outer Zone: Surrounded by pink peripheral fibrosis replacing periarticular soft tissue.
Acute vs. Chronic Pharmacotherapy
  • Acute Attack Treatment: Rapidly suppress neutrophilic inflammation using NSAIDs, colchicine, or glucocorticoids.
  • Chronic Prevention (Urate-Lowering): Lower serum uric acid long-term with allopurinol, febuxostat, probenecid, or pegloticase.
  • Contrast with CPPD: Unlike gout, chronic CPPD has no specific crystal-lowering therapy.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

“Routine formalin processing dissolves urate crystals; therefore H&E sections often show pale deposits surrounded by foreign-body granulomatous inflammation.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A biopsy of an ear or toe nodule showing pale eosinophilic clefts ringed by foreign-body giant cells and fibrosis represents a gouty tophus whose MSU crystals dissolved in formalin.

TOPIC / LO 4

Calcium Pyrophosphate Deposition Disease (CPPD / Pseudogout)

Lecturer Rule: Rhomboid + Positive Birefringence + Knee in older adult = CPPD; screen for hemochromatosis.

CPPD vs. Gout Polarized Light & Chondrocalcinosis Workbench

Explore CPP crystal birefringence, knee meniscal chondrocalcinosis, and hemochromatosis/metabolic associations.

LO 4.01 • CPP Crystals

CPPD Pathogenesis & Polarized Crystal Signature

Deposited Crystal Calcium pyrophosphate (CPP)
Molecular Driver NLRP3 inflammasome → IL-1β
Crystal Shape Rhomboid-shaped
Birefringence Weakly positively birefringent
Parallel Axis Color Blue when parallel

Cartilage Shedding, NLRP3 Inflammation & Optical Identification

LO 4.01 • CPPD Pathogenesis & Crystal Analysis
Pathogenesis & Inflammatory Cascade
  • Primary Event: Deposition of calcium pyrophosphate (CPP) crystals within articular hyaline cartilage, fibrocartilage, and periarticular tissues.
  • Joint Shedding: CPP crystals shed from cartilage into the synovial space and activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, triggering IL-1β release and neutrophil-mediated inflammation.
  • Shared Pathway: Both gout and CPPD converge on NLRP3 → IL-1β → neutrophilic synovitis despite distinct crystals.
Polarized Light Microscopy Signature
  • Shape: Synovial fluid aspiration reveals rhomboid-shaped CPP crystals (in contrast to needle-shaped MSU).
  • Birefringence: CPP crystals are weakly positively birefringent under polarized light.
  • Compensator Color Rule: Appears blue when parallel to the slow compensator axis (opposite of MSU, which is yellow when parallel).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

“Rhomboid + Positive = CPPD. Rhomboid crystals + weak positive birefringence + knee in older adults = CPPD (pseudogout).”

Second-Order Board Clue

When a question gives synovial fluid crystals that turn blue when parallel to the slow axis, identify calcium pyrophosphate and recall that it activates NLRP3 inflammasome just like urate.

LO 4.02 • Clinical Spectrum

Acute Pseudogout vs. Chronic CPP Arthropathy

Acute Target Joint Acute monoarthritis (knee)
Acute Mimickers Gout & septic arthritis
Chronic Mimicker Resembles osteoarthritis
Acute Treatment NSAIDs, colchicine, steroids
Chronic Prevention No crystal-lowering; low-dose colchicine

Clinical Progression & Therapeutic Distinctions from Gout

LO 4.02 • Acute & Chronic CPPD Presentations
Acute Pseudogout vs. Chronic Arthropathy
  • Clinical Progression: Asymptomatic CPP crystal deposition → acute pseudogout → chronic CPP crystal arthropathy.
  • Acute CPP Crystal Arthritis (Pseudogout): Acute warm, swollen, painful monoarthritis most commonly affecting the knee, closely mimicking gout and septic arthritis.
  • Chronic CPP Arthropathy: Progressive cartilage degeneration resembling osteoarthritis, commonly involving knees, wrists, MCP joints, shoulders, and ankles.
Management Differences vs. Gout
  • Acute Flare Control: Treated identically to acute gout with NSAIDs, colchicine, or glucocorticoids.
  • Chronic Prevention: Crucially, there is no crystal-lowering therapy for CPPD (allopurinol has no effect on pyrophosphate).
  • Recurrence Prophylaxis: Frequent recurrent acute pseudogout flares may be prevented using low-dose colchicine.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

“Acute CPP crystal arthritis most commonly affects the knee and mimics gout and septic arthritis. There is no crystal-lowering therapy; recurrent attacks may be prevented with low-dose colchicine.”

Second-Order Board Clue

An older adult with degenerative arthropathy in atypical OA sites like the wrists, MCPs, or shoulders has chronic CPP arthropathy rather than primary osteoarthritis.

LO 4.03 • Radiology & H&E

Chondrocalcinosis Morphology & Imaging

Imaging Hallmark Chondrocalcinosis
Classic Anatomic Site Menisci of the knee
H&E Deposit Appearance Dark purple nodules
Surrounding Reaction Fibroblastic tissue rim

Radiographic Cartilage Calcification & Fibrocartilage Histology

LO 4.03 • Chondrocalcinosis Pathology & Radiology
Radiographic & Arthroscopic Chondrocalcinosis
  • Definition: Chondrocalcinosis is the characteristic radiographic calcification of hyaline articular cartilage and fibrocartilage.
  • Knee Menisci: Most frequently visualized within the menisci of the knee on plain radiographs as linear radiodense bands.
  • Arthroscopic View: Direct visualization shows chalky-white meniscal calcification embedded in the cartilage substance.
Histopathology of Chondrocalcinosis
  • Basophilic Nodules: On H&E sections, CPP crystal deposits appear as large dark purple nodules within cartilage (unlike formalin-dissolved pale urate clefts).
  • Matrix Background: Surrounded by pale pink fibrocartilaginous tissue.
  • Cellular Rim: Bordered by a cellular rim of chronic inflammatory and fibroblastic tissue reaction.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

“Acute knee monoarthritis + chondrocalcinosis + rhomboid, weakly positively birefringent crystals = CPPD.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Contrast H&E slides: pale empty clefts with foreign-body giant cells indicate gout, whereas large dark purple nodules embedded in fibrocartilage represent CPPD chondrocalcinosis.

LO 4.04 • Associations

Metabolic Risk Factors & Hemochromatosis Arthropathy

Major Risk Factor Advanced age (>50 years)
Classic Systemic Link Hemochromatosis
Pathogenic Mechanism Iron deposition → CPP crystals
Signature Hand Joints 2nd and 3rd MCP joints
Other Metabolic Causes Hyperparathyroidism, hypomagnesemia

Idiopathic Age Risk vs. Secondary Metabolic Screening & Hemochromatosis

LO 4.04 • Risk Factors & Hemochromatosis Association
Idiopathic & Secondary Metabolic Triggers
  • Primary Driver: Most CPPD cases are idiopathic and occur with advanced age (>50 years) or prior joint damage.
  • Endocrine/Electrolyte Links: Secondary CPPD is associated with hyperparathyroidism, hypomagnesemia, and hypothyroidism.
  • Metabolic Deposition: Also linked to ochronosis (alkaptonuria) and hereditary iron overload.
Hemochromatosis Arthropathy Signature
  • High-Yield Link: Hemochromatosis strongly predisposes to secondary CPPD.
  • Mechanism: Tissue iron deposition directly promotes calcium pyrophosphate (CPP) crystal formation in cartilage.
  • Anatomic Hallmarks: The 2nd and 3rd MCP joints are classically involved in hemochromatosis arthropathy, often accompanied by chondrocalcinosis.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

“Know this extremely well: Hemochromatosis predisposes to CPPD; iron deposition promotes CPP crystal formation; 2nd and 3rd MCP joints are classically involved in hemochromatosis arthropathy.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A relatively young patient (<50 years) presenting with chondrocalcinosis or 2nd and 3rd MCP degenerative changes should be screened for hemochromatosis, hyperparathyroidism, and hypomagnesemia.
