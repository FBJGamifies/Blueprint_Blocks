Suppurative (Septic) Arthritis & Microbial Pathogenesis

Lecturer Rule: Acute monoarthritis = septic arthritis until proven otherwise

Interactive Pathogen & Synovial Triage Explorer (LO1)

Cartilage destroyed within days without drainage

LO 1.01 • Pathogenesis

Synovial Vulnerability & Routes of Entry

Anatomic Vulnerability Synovium lacks limiting basement membrane
#1 Entry Route Hematogenous seeding
Alternative Routes Direct inoculation, contiguous, osteomyelitis
Inflammatory Exudate Purulent neutrophil-rich inflammation
Cartilage Risk Destroyed within days (poor regeneration)

Why the Joint Space Is Rapidly Destroyed in Suppurative Arthritis

LO 1.01 • Lecture 10
Synovial Barrier Defect & Seeding Routes
  • Absence of Basement Membrane: The synovium is uniquely vulnerable to circulating pathogens because it lacks a limiting basement membrane, allowing microbes to pass directly from synovial capillaries into the joint space.
  • Primary Route: Hematogenous seeding of the synovium during bacteremia is the most common route of acute joint infection.
  • Secondary Entry Routes: Bacteria may also enter via direct inoculation (trauma, surgery, or arthrocentesis), contiguous spread from adjacent soft tissue infection, or extension from adjacent osteomyelitis.
Purulent Cascade & Irreversible Chondrolysis
  • Purulent Inflammation: Microbial proliferation triggers an acute purulent neutrophil-rich inflammation within the synovial cavity.
  • Rapid Articular Damage: Because articular hyaline cartilage has poor intrinsic regenerative capacity, unchecked neutrophil-driven inflammation destroys articular cartilage within days.
  • Clinical Urgency: Any delay in diagnosis or drainage leads to permanent, irreversible joint destruction.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Alex Gasparian, PhD)

“Remember why bacteria enter the joint so easily during bacteremia: the synovium lacks a limiting basement membrane, and once neutrophils pour in, articular cartilage can be destroyed within days because of its poor regenerative capacity.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A patient develops acute knee swelling days after bacteremia or arthrocentesis; the histologic feature explaining easy synovial colonization is absence of a limiting basement membrane.

Microbial Etiologies of Infectious Arthritis Across Clinical Hosts

LO 1.02 • Lecture 10
Common Bacterial Pathogens
  • Most Common Overall: Staphylococcus aureus is the single most common cause of acute septic arthritis overall across clinical cohorts.
  • Second Most Common: Streptococcus spp. represent the second most frequent bacterial etiology of suppurative joint infection.
  • Young Sexually Active Adults: Neisseria gonorrhoeae must be suspected in sexually active young adults presenting with acute infectious arthritis.
  • Older Adults & Immunocompromised: Gram-negative bacilli, notably Escherichia coli, predominate in older adults and immunocompromised patients.
Less Common & Non-Purulent Infectious Causes
  • Mycobacterial Cause: Mycobacterium tuberculosis produces chronic granulomatous arthritis rather than acute purulent synovitis.
  • Fungal Opportunists: Candida albicans and other fungi cause infectious arthritis predominantly in immunocompromised hosts.
  • Viral Contrast: Viruses such as parvovirus B19, hepatitis B/C, and rubella cause infectious arthritis but typically do not produce purulent septic arthritis.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Alex Gasparian, PhD)

“Staphylococcus aureus is the most common cause overall, Streptococcus is second, Neisseria gonorrhoeae targets young sexually active adults, and E. coli targets the elderly and immunocompromised.”

Second-Order Board Clue

An older immunocompromised patient with acute warm knee monoarthritis yields Gram-negative rods on synovial aspirate; the classic organism taught is Escherichia coli (whereas a young sexually active adult points to Neisseria gonorrhoeae).

Diagnostic Workup & Emergency Decompression of Acute Septic Arthritis

LO 1.03 • Lecture 10
Clinical & Synovial Fluid Profile
  • Acute Monoarticular Pattern: Sudden onset of a painful, swollen, warm, erythematous joint with decreased range of motion; usually monoarticular, most often affecting the knee.
  • Systemic Signs: Fever, leukocytosis, and elevated inflammatory markers (CRP and ESR) commonly accompany the joint findings.
  • Mandatory Arthrocentesis: Synovial fluid aspiration is essential before definitive therapy, yielding purulent, neutrophil-predominant fluid with >50,000 WBC/µL (frequently >100,000 WBC/µL) plus Gram stain and culture.
Immediate Therapeutic Countermeasures
  • Antimicrobial Therapy: Initiate prompt IV antibiotics immediately after diagnostic synovial fluid sampling.
  • Mechanical Decompression: Perform urgent joint drainage via repeated needle aspiration or surgical drainage to evacuate purulent exudate.
  • Delayed Treatment Consequence: Delaying antibiotics or joint drainage causes irreversible cartilage destruction.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Alex Gasparian, PhD)

“Acute monoarthritis should be presumed septic arthritis until proven otherwise. Synovial fluid aspiration is mandatory—expect purulent, neutrophil-predominant fluid over 50,000 WBC/µL.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Even if a patient has a history of gout or pseudogout, an acutely hot, swollen knee requires synovial fluid aspiration to rule out septic arthritis (>50,000 WBC/µL) followed by IV antibiotics and joint drainage.

LO 2.01 • Granulomatous

Mycobacterial (Tuberculous) Arthritis

Etiologic Agent Mycobacterium tuberculosis
Clinical Pattern Chronic progressive monoarthritis
Weight-Bearing Predilection Hip > knee > ankle
Histologic Hallmark Granulomatous synovitis (Langhans cells)
Special Stain AFB carbol fuchsin (waxy wall)

Chronic Granulomatous Monoarthritis Caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis

LO 2.01 • Lecture 10
Spread & Clinical Course
  • Routes of Joint Infection: Usually develops via hematogenous spread from pulmonary TB or direct extension from adjacent osteomyelitis.
  • Chronic Progressive Pattern: Presents as a chronic progressive monoarthritis rather than an acute purulent emergency.
  • Weight-Bearing Joints: Classically involves large weight-bearing joints in the distribution hip > knee > ankle.
Granulomatous Histology & AFB Diagnostics
  • Synovial Biopsy Hallmark: Demonstrates granulomatous synovitis composed of epithelioid histiocytes and multinucleated Langhans-type giant cells.
  • Acid-Fast Bacillus (AFB) Mechanism: Bacteria are stained with carbol fuchsin and treated with acid-alcohol; mycobacteria retain the red dye because of their waxy cell walls.
  • Confirmatory Workup: Diagnosis relies on AFB stain, mycobacterial cultures, and synovial biopsy.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Alex Gasparian, PhD)

“Chronic monoarthritis plus granulomas and Langhans giant cells equals tuberculous arthritis. Remember the AFB principle: carbol fuchsin is retained after acid-alcohol wash because of mycobacteria's waxy cell walls.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A patient with indolent chronic hip monoarthritis undergoes synovial biopsy showing epithelioid histiocytes and Langhans-type giant cells; confirm the organism with AFB stain and mycobacterial culture.

LO 2.02 • Spirochetal

Lyme Arthritis

Pathogen & Vector Borrelia burgdorferi / Ixodes ticks
Clinical Hallmark Knee swelling out of proportion to pain
Histologic Hallmark Papillary (villous) synovial hypertrophy
Diagnostic Workup ELISA followed by Western blot
Antibiotic Regimen Doxycycline / amoxicillin; IV ceftriaxone

Chronic Proliferative Synovitis & Diagnostic Confirmation in Lyme Arthritis

LO 2.02 • Lecture 10
Transmission, Latency & Preceding Signs
  • Etiology & Timeline: Caused by Borrelia burgdorferi transmitted by Ixodes ticks; intermittent or persistent monoarthritis or oligoarthritis develops months after infection.
  • Joint Predilection: The knee is affected far more often than the ankle, elbow, or shoulder, classically with massive unilateral knee swelling out of proportion to pain.
  • Associated Prior Findings: Preceding history may include erythema migrans, facial nerve palsy, or AV block.
Synovial Histology, Imaging & Treatment
  • Chronic Proliferative Synovitis: Histology shows synovial hyperplasia with papillary (villous) synovial hypertrophy, a dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate, fibrin deposition, and neovascularization.
  • Imaging & Two-Tier Serology: MRI/radiographs show a large knee effusion, synovitis, and enlarged popliteal lymph nodes; diagnosis uses two-tier serology (ELISA followed by Western blot) or synovial fluid PCR.
  • Antimicrobial Management: Treat with oral doxycycline or amoxicillin; use IV ceftriaxone for refractory cases.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Alex Gasparian, PhD)

“Massive knee swelling out of proportion to pain is the classic Lyme arthritis clue—look for prior erythema migrans, facial palsy, or AV block, and confirm with ELISA followed by Western blot.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A hiker with painless massive unilateral knee effusion and enlarged popliteal lymph nodes on MRI has papillary (villous) synovial hypertrophy with a dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate; treat initially with doxycycline.

Symmetric Small-Joint Polyarthritis Following Viral Illness

LO 2.03 • Lecture 10
Clinical Mimicry & Viral Etiologies
  • Key Causative Viruses: Parvovirus B19, Chikungunya, HBV, HCV, Rubella, and HIV.
  • Rheumatoid Arthritis Mimic: Produces an acute or subacute symmetric polyarthritis targeting the small joints of the hands and wrists.
  • Associated Viral Prodrome: Often accompanied by fever, rash, and lymphadenopathy.
Pathology, Course & Chronic Exceptions
  • Pathology: Driven by a transient immune-mediated synovitis rather than purulent neutrophil invasion.
  • Typical Resolution: Symptoms typically resolve spontaneously within 1–6 weeks.
  • Chronicity Exceptions & Diagnosis: May become chronic especially with Chikungunya, HBV, HCV, and Parvovirus B19; diagnosed by clinical presentation plus virus-specific serology/PCR when indicated.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Alex Gasparian, PhD)

“Acute symmetric polyarthritis of the small joints of the hands and wrists following a viral illness with rash and fever can closely mimic rheumatoid arthritis—usually resolving within 1 to 6 weeks.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A teacher develops acute symmetric hand and wrist pain with fever and rash after exposure to children; distinguish this transient immune-mediated synovitis (Parvovirus B19) from rheumatoid arthritis by its self-limiting 1–6 weeks course.

TOPIC / LO 3

Gout Pathogenesis, Clinical Stages & Tophus Histopathology

Lecturer Rule: Needle + Negative = Gout; Serum uric acid may be normal in acute attacks

Gout Four-Stage Progression & Tophus / Crystal Explorer (LO3)

Hyperuricemia >6.8 mg/dL → MSU → NLRP3 → IL-1β

LO 3.01 • Etiology

Hyperuricemia: Underexcretion vs. Overproduction

Hyperuricemia Threshold Serum urate >6.8 mg/dL
Primary Mechanism (~90%) Decreased renal uric acid excretion
Overproduction (~10%) Cell turnover, leukemias, tumor lysis syndrome
HGPRT Deficiency Kelley-Seegmiller vs. Lesch-Nyhan
Crystal Overview Endogenous vs. corticosteroid / debris

Crystal Classification & Metabolic Drivers of Hyperuricemia

LO 3.01 • Lecture 10
Crystal Types & Underexcretion (~90%)
  • Endogenous vs. Exogenous Crystals: Endogenous pathogenic crystals include monosodium urate (MSU) (gout), calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate (CPP) (pseudogout), and basic calcium phosphate; exogenous crystals include corticosteroid crystals and prosthetic joint wear debris.
  • Hyperuricemia Definition: Gout arises from urate supersaturation when serum uric acid exceeds >6.8 mg/dL, leading to MSU crystal precipitation.
  • Underexcretion (~90%): Most primary gout results from decreased renal uric acid excretion, worsened by chronic kidney disease, diuretics, cyclosporine, and dehydration.
Overproduction (~10%) & Enzymatic Syndromes
  • High Cell Turnover States: Approximately 10% of cases stem from uric acid overproduction due to increased cell turnover in leukemias and tumor lysis syndrome.
  • Partial HGPRT Deficiency: Partial deficiency of HGPRT causes Kelley-Seegmiller syndrome.
  • Near-Complete HGPRT Deficiency: Near-complete deficiency of HGPRT causes Lesch-Nyhan syndrome.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Alex Gasparian, PhD)

“Approximately 90% of primary gout results from decreased renal uric acid excretion, while ~10% comes from overproduction such as tumor lysis syndrome or HGPRT deficiency.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A patient starting chemotherapy develops acute hyperuricemia and monoarthritis from tumor lysis syndrome (~10% overproduction pathway), whereas a patient on loop diuretics with CKD illustrates the ~90% renal underexcretion mechanism.

LO 3.02 • Pathogenesis

NLRP3 Inflammasome & Acute Triggers

Innate Immune Sensor NLRP3 inflammasome activation
Key Cytokine IL-1β release
Effector Leukocyte Neutrophilic inflammation
Precipitating Triggers Alcohol, purines, dehydration, trauma, diuretics
Key Negative Clue Serum uric acid may be normal in acute attack

MSU Crystal-Induced Inflammasome Signaling & Diagnostic Serum Pitfall

LO 3.02 • Lecture 10
Molecular Inflammasome Cascade
  • Most Important Pathogenetic Step: Deposited monosodium urate (MSU) crystals activate the NLRP3 inflammasome.
  • Cytokine & Neutrophil Recruitment: Inflammasome activation drives IL-1β release, triggering intense neutrophilic inflammation and acute joint pain and swelling.
  • Cartilage Consequence: Repeated crystal-driven inflammatory flares progressively damage articular cartilage.
Acute Triggers & Serum Uric Acid Pitfall
  • Classic Acute Triggers: Flares are precipitated by alcohol, a purine-rich diet, dehydration, trauma, surgery, or diuretics.
  • Critical Diagnostic Trap: Serum uric acid levels may be completely normal during an acute gout attack.
  • Confirmation Rule: Diagnosis cannot be excluded by normal serum urate and must be confirmed by identifying MSU crystals in synovial fluid.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Alex Gasparian, PhD)

“Remember the core pathway: MSU crystals activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, driving IL-1β release and neutrophilic inflammation. Never rule out gout just because serum uric acid is normal during an acute attack.”

Second-Order Board Clue

After surgery or heavy alcohol intake, a patient develops excruciating first-toe pain with a normal serum urate level; the acute inflammatory mediator directly driving neutrophil influx is IL-1β downstream of the NLRP3 inflammasome.

LO 3.03 • Clinical Course

Four Clinical Stages of Gout & Podagra

Stage 1 Asymptomatic hyperuricemia (no symptoms)
Stage 2 Hallmark 1st metatarsophalangeal joint (podagra)
Stage 3 Intercritical gout (symptom-free interval)
Stage 4 Chronic tophaceous gout
Late Joint Pattern Recurrent attacks become polyarticular

Natural History of Gout from Asymptomatic Hyperuricemia to Chronic Tophi

LO 3.03 • Lecture 10
Stages 1 & 2: Hyperuricemia & Podagra
  • 1. Asymptomatic Hyperuricemia: Elevated serum uric acid without any clinical arthritis symptoms.
  • 2. Acute Gouty Arthritis: Sudden onset of severe joint pain, usually monoarticular, most classically affecting the 1st metatarsophalangeal joint (podagra).
  • Acute Resolution: Untreated or treated acute attacks last hours to weeks with complete resolution between early attacks.
Stages 3 & 4: Intercritical to Tophaceous Disease
  • 3. Intercritical Gout: Symptom-free interval between flares; over time, recurrent attacks become more frequent and eventually become polyarticular.
  • 4. Chronic Tophaceous Gout: Develops after years of recurrent attacks as tophi accumulate in joints and soft tissues.
  • Structural Outcome: Results in progressive cartilage destruction, bone erosions, and joint deformity.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Alex Gasparian, PhD)

“Podagra—sudden, severe monoarthritis of the first metatarsophalangeal joint—is the classic initial presentation of gout, progressing through intercritical intervals into chronic tophaceous deformity if untreated.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A patient recalls episodic 1st MTP flares (podagra) with completely symptom-free intervals (intercritical gout) years ago that have now evolved into polyarticular deformity with nodular masses (chronic tophaceous gout).

LO 3.04 • Morphology

Tophus Histology & Radiographic Erosions

Gross Tophus Extruded chalky-white urate material
Formalin Artifact Routine formalin dissolves urate crystals
Central H&E Zone Pale amorphous MSU deposit spaces
Cellular Rim Macrophages & foreign-body giant cells
Radiographic Hallmark Punched-out erosions with overhanging edges

Microscopic Tophus Architecture, Formalin Artifact & Rat-Bite Erosions

LO 3.04 • Lecture 10
Gross & Microscopic Morphology of a Tophus
  • Gross & Processing Artifact: Ulcerated tophi extrude chalky-white urate material; during histology, routine formalin processing dissolves urate crystals.
  • Central Pale Area: Leaves central pale dissolved MSU crystal deposits (pale amorphous spaces on H&E).
  • Purple Cellular Rim: Surrounded by a granulomatous rim of macrophages and foreign-body giant cells.
  • Outer Pink Layer: Encased peripherally by reactive fibrosis.
Radiographic Bone & Soft-Tissue Findings
  • Periarticular Tophi: Soft-tissue tophi replace periarticular soft tissue and erode adjacent bone.
  • Characteristic Erosions: Radiographs show sharply marginated punched-out erosions (also termed rat-bite erosions) with overhanging edges.
  • Differential Radiology Contrast: Unlike CPPD (chondrocalcinosis), chronic gout is defined by soft-tissue tophi and rat-bite erosions.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Alex Gasparian, PhD)

“Routine formalin processing dissolves urate crystals—that is why H&E sections show pale dissolved spaces surrounded by macrophages, foreign-body giant cells, and outer fibrosis. On X-ray, look for soft-tissue tophi and punched-out erosions with overhanging edges.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A periarticular biopsy fixed in formalin shows pale amorphous spaces rimmed by foreign-body giant cells and fibrosis rather than intact crystals; this formalin dissolution artifact corresponds radiographically to punched-out erosions with overhanging edges.

LO 3.05 • Microscopy & Tx

Polarized Crystal ID & Gout Pharmacotherapy

Diagnostic Gold Standard Polarized light microscopy of synovial fluid
Crystal Shape Needle-shaped crystals
Birefringence & Color Negatively birefringent (yellow parallel)
Acute Countermeasures NSAIDs, colchicine, glucocorticoids
Chronic Prevention Allopurinol, febuxostat, probenecid, pegloticase

Synovial Polarized Microscopy & Acute vs. Chronic Gout Therapy

LO 3.05 • Lecture 10
Gold-Standard Polarized Microscopy
  • Gold Standard Test: Synovial fluid crystal analysis under polarized light microscopy confirms crystal-induced arthritis.
  • Morphology & Optics: MSU crystals are needle-shaped and negatively birefringent.
  • Axis Color Rule: MSU crystals appear yellow when parallel to the slow axis and blue when perpendicular—the exact opposite of calcium pyrophosphate.
Acute Flare Control vs. Urate-Lowering Prevention
  • Acute Gout Treatment: Control acute neutrophilic inflammation using NSAIDs, colchicine, or glucocorticoids.
  • Chronic Prevention (ULT): Long-term urate-lowering therapy prevents recurrent attacks and tophi using allopurinol, febuxostat, probenecid, or pegloticase.
  • CPPD Contrast: Unlike gout, CPPD has no crystal-lowering therapy and uses low-dose colchicine for flare prevention.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Alex Gasparian, PhD)

“Memorize the exam equation: Needle + Negative = Gout. MSU crystals are yellow when parallel to the slow axis and blue when perpendicular, opposite of CPPD.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Synovial fluid aspirate shows intracellular needle-shaped crystals turning yellow when parallel to the compensator axis; start NSAIDs, colchicine, or glucocorticoids acutely and allopurinol for chronic prevention.

Lecturer Rule: Rhomboid + Positive = CPPD; Screen for Hemochromatosis (2nd & 3rd MCPs)

CPPD Clinical Phenotype & Crystal Discriminator (LO4)

Rhomboid + Weakly Positive Birefringence + Chondrocalcinosis

LO 4.01 • Pathogenesis & Optics

CPP Deposition, NLRP3 & Rhomboid Crystals

Deposited Crystal Calcium pyrophosphate (CPP) in cartilage
Inflammatory Pathway NLRP3 inflammasome → IL-1β → neutrophils
Crystal Shape Rhomboid-shaped crystals
Birefringence & Color Weakly positively birefringent (blue parallel)
Key Negative Clue NOT needle-shaped or negatively birefringent

Cartilage Crystal Shedding, Inflammasome Activation & Polarized Identification

LO 4.01 • Lecture 10
Pathogenesis & Joint Shedding
  • Cartilage Deposition: Calcium pyrophosphate (CPP) crystals deposit within articular cartilage and periarticular tissues.
  • Crystal Shedding & NLRP3: When CPP crystals shed into the joint space, they activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, triggering IL-1β release and neutrophil-mediated inflammation.
  • Clinical Progression: Advances from asymptomatic CPP crystal deposition to acute pseudogout and finally chronic CPP crystal arthropathy.
Polarized Light Crystal Identification
  • Shape: Synovial fluid microscopy reveals characteristic rhomboid-shaped CPP crystals.
  • Optical Property: Crystals are weakly positively birefringent.
  • Slow-Axis Rule: CPP crystals appear blue when parallel to the slow axis, directly contrasting with yellow-parallel MSU crystals in gout.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Alex Gasparian, PhD)

“Rhomboid plus positive equals CPPD. When parallel to the slow axis, rhomboid CPP crystals are blue—weakly positively birefringent—and share the same NLRP3 inflammasome and IL-1β pathway as gout.”

Second-Order Board Clue

An older adult with acute knee swelling has synovial fluid containing rhomboid-shaped crystals that appear blue when parallel to the slow axis; the shared intracellular sensor driving IL-1β release is the NLRP3 inflammasome.

LO 4.02 • Clinical & Imaging

Acute Pseudogout, Chronic Arthropathy & Chondrocalcinosis

Acute Presentation Acute monoarthritis of the knee
Clinical Mimics Mimics gout and septic arthritis
Chronic Pattern Resembles osteoarthritis (knees, wrists, MCPs)
Radiographic Hallmark Chondrocalcinosis (menisci / cartilage)

CPPD Clinical Phenotypes, Chondrocalcinosis & Cartilage Histopathology

LO 4.02 • Lecture 10
Acute Pseudogout vs. Chronic CPP Arthropathy
  • Acute CPP Crystal Arthritis (Pseudogout): Presents as an acute warm, swollen, painful monoarthritis with the knee most commonly affected, closely mimicking gout and septic arthritis.
  • Chronic CPP Crystal Arthropathy: Progressive cartilage degeneration produces chronic degenerative arthritis resembling osteoarthritis.
  • Chronic Joint Distribution: Commonly involves the knees, wrists, MCP joints, shoulders, and ankles.
Chondrocalcinosis Imaging & Histology
  • Chondrocalcinosis on X-ray & Arthroscopy: Calcification of hyaline cartilage and fibrocartilage, frequently involving the menisci of the knee.
  • Microscopic Morphology: Histology shows large dark purple nodules (CPP crystal deposits) embedded in pale pink fibrocartilaginous tissue.
  • Cellular Reaction: Nodules are bordered by a rim of chronic inflammatory and fibroblastic tissue reaction.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Alex Gasparian, PhD)

“Acute knee monoarthritis plus chondrocalcinosis—especially meniscal calcification—plus rhomboid, weakly positively birefringent crystals equals CPPD. Chronically, it mimics osteoarthritis in the knees, wrists, and MCP joints.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A knee radiograph shows linear meniscal calcification (chondrocalcinosis) and arthroscopy confirms meniscal deposits; biopsy of the fibrocartilage would show large dark purple nodules rimmed by fibroblastic reaction.

Secondary Metabolic Associations & Management

Major Risk Factor Advanced age (>50 years, idiopathic)
Classic Association Hemochromatosis (iron promotes CPP)
Hemochromatosis Joints Classic 2nd and 3rd MCP joints
Other Metabolic Risks Hyperparathyroidism, hypomagnesemia, hypothyroidism
Chronic Prevention Rule No crystal-lowering therapy (low-dose colchicine)

Secondary Metabolic Triggers, Hemochromatosis Arthropathy & CPPD Treatment

LO 4.03 • Lecture 10
Age, Hemochromatosis & Endocrine Associations
  • Idiopathic Age Predilection: Advanced age (>50 years) is the most common risk factor, with most cases being idiopathic.
  • Hemochromatosis Link: Hemochromatosis strongly predisposes to CPPD because iron deposition promotes CPP crystal formation; classically involves the 2nd and 3rd MCP joints.
  • Other Associated Conditions: Screen for hyperparathyroidism, hypomagnesemia, hypothyroidism, ochronosis, and prior joint damage.
Acute Flare Relief & Prophylaxis Limitation
  • Acute CPPD Treatment: Managed similarly to acute gout with NSAIDs, colchicine, or glucocorticoids.
  • No Crystal-Lowering Agent: Unlike urate-lowering therapy in gout, there is no crystal-lowering therapy for CPPD.
  • Recurrent Attack Prophylaxis: Frequent recurrent pseudogout attacks may be prevented using low-dose colchicine.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Alex Gasparian, PhD)

“Know this extremely well: hemochromatosis predisposes to CPPD because iron deposition promotes CPP crystal formation, and the 2nd and 3rd MCP joints are classically involved.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A patient under age 50 presents with CPPD arthropathy affecting the 2nd and 3rd MCP joints; suspect underlying hemochromatosis (where iron deposition promotes CPP nucleation) or screen for hyperparathyroidism and hypomagnesemia.

CPPD (Pseudogout) Crystal Arthropathy CPP deposition in hyaline & fibrocartilage; crystal shedding activates NLRP3 inflammasome → IL-1β → neutrophils; dark purple nodules in fibrocartilage. Acute knee monoarthritis mimicking gout/septic arthritis; chronic arthropathy mimicking OA (knees, wrists, MCPs); risks: age >50, hemochromatosis, hyperparathyroidism, hypomagnesemia. Polarized microscopy: rhomboid, weakly positively birefringent (blue parallel); X-ray/arthroscopy: chondrocalcinosis (menisci); acute NSAIDs/colchicine/steroids; no crystal-lowering drug. “Rhomboid + Positive = CPPD; iron promotes CPP in hemochromatosis (2nd & 3rd MCPs).” OA-like arthropathy in 2nd and 3rd MCP joints with meniscal chondrocalcinosis → evaluate for hemochromatosis.
