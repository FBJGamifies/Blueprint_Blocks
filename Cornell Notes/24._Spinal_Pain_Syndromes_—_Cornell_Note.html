Terminology Precision & Anatomical Orientation (Head → Down)

LO1 • Core Vocabulary

Spondyl- & Structural Definitions

  • Spondylosis denotes osteoarthritis of the spinal joints (with or without nerve compression), commonly driven by degenerative disc disease (DDD).
  • Spondylitis specifically means active inflammation of the spine or spinal joint (e.g., axial spondyloarthropathy).
  • Spondylolysis is a structural stress fracture/defect in the pars interarticularis, whereas spondylolisthesis is forward slippage of one vertebra over the one below it.
  • Spinal stenosis is narrowing of the canal where the spinal cord dwells; radiculopathy is a pinched nerve root causing dermatomal pain, tingling, numbness, or weakness.

Transition Zones & Head-to-Toe Flow

  • Transition zones (C7–T1 and L5–S1) link highly mobile cervical/lumbar curves to relatively rigid thoracic/sacral segments, concentrating mechanical shear and degeneration.
  • Dr. Dodson frames differential diagnosis from head down: cervicogenic headache → cervicalgia → cervical radiculopathy → compression fracture → spondylosis → spinal stenosis → spondylolisthesis → lumbar strain → facet syndrome → spondyloarthropathies.
  • Course Scope Boundary: Primary spinal tumors are covered in separate oncology/neuro courses; this lecture focuses on common mechanical, inflammatory, and structural pain syndromes.

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Daniel Dodson, DO)

This is a high-yield COMLEX topic. Always separate radiological labels ("spondylosis" on a report) from inflammatory disease ("spondylitis") and structural instability ("spondylolisthesis").

Second-Order Board Clue

Young hyperextension athlete with intact alignment = spondylolysis; same patient presenting 5 years later with anterior vertebral translation = spondylolisthesis.

Spinal Emergencies vs. Benign Mechanical Pain

Urgent Triage

Neural Compression Emergencies

  • Cauda equina syndrome reflects acute compression of the lowest lumbosacral nerve roots: look for saddle anesthesia and acute bowel/bladder sphincter dysfunction — an immediate surgical emergency.
  • Myelopathy indicates direct pressure on the spinal cord itself (upper motor neuron signs), distinguishing it from isolated nerve root compression (radiculitis/radiculopathy).
  • Herniated disc causes disc-related pressure on neural elements with or without neuroforaminal stenosis; central bulge can precipitate spinal canal stenosis.

Space-Occupying & Infectious Red Flags

  • Discitis (disc space infection), osteomyelitis (vertebral bone infection), and paraspinal abscess (pus around the cord) require prompt infectious workup and imaging.
  • Epidural spinal hematoma (bleeding outside the cord inside the canal) and metastatic malignancy produce progressive, non-remitting pain and neurological decline.
  • Uncomplicated facet syndrome or lumbar strain lacks fever, saddle anesthesia, bowel/bladder loss, and progressive motor weakness.

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Daniel Dodson, DO)

Cauda equina syndrome is explicitly flagged with an emergency arrow in the slide table — never delay decompression or send a patient with saddle anesthesia and urinary retention to routine PT.

Second-Order Board Clue

Low back pain + dermatomal shooting pain + positive straight leg raise = radiculopathy; low back pain + saddle numbness + urinary retention = cauda equina emergency.

Posterior Cartilage Wear & Case 1 vs. Case 2 Discriminator

LO2 • Clinical Reasoning

Pathogenesis & Provocation Mechanics

  • Pathology: Osteoarthritis/degeneration of the hyaline cartilage lining the posterior zygapophyseal (facet) joints, driven by increased age, excess weight, and decreased physical activity.
  • Localization: Most frequently affects L4–L5 and L5–S1 in the lumbar spine, as well as the cervical spine.
  • Postural Signature: Spinal extension presses the facet joint surfaces together (provoking pain), whereas flexion opens the joint space and relieves discomfort.

Slide Case Comparison (Case 1 vs. Case 2)

  • Case 1 (Facet Arthropathy): 55-year-old man with sharp localized low back pain radiating to the buttocks, worse with extension, relieved by flexion, tenderness over L4–L5, and negative straight leg raise bilaterally.
  • Case 2 (Lumbar Disc Herniation): 55-year-old man with chronic back pain radiating down into his right leg, worsened by prolonged sitting/standing, with a positive straight leg raise on the right.
  • Key Distinction: Facet pain refers locally to the buttocks/thigh without true dermatomal radiculopathy or nerve tension signs.

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Daniel Dodson, DO)

Extension presses the posterior joints together; flexion relieves them. Remember the overlap: patients can have degenerative disc disease and facet syndrome simultaneously.

Second-Order Board Clue

55M + sharp LBP radiating only to buttocks + worse with extension + negative SLR = Facet joint arthropathy (NOT lumbar disc herniation).

Radiographic Sclerosis & Clinical-Imaging Discordance

LO2 • Imaging & Treatment

X-Ray Findings & Diagnostic Rule

  • Plain X-rays demonstrate joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis (increased whiteness from increased bone density/hardening in the joint), osteophytes (bone spurs), and occasional small erosions.
  • Clinical-Radiographic Discordance: Many asymptomatic patients have marked facet arthropathy incidentally noted on X-rays obtained for unrelated reasons (e.g., shoulder/chest workups), while patients with classic clinical facet syndrome may have normal X-rays.

Conservative Management & OMT

  • First-line countermeasures include anti-inflammatory medications (Rx), heat/ice, physical therapy, core strengthening exercises, and OMT.
  • Chronic, refractory symptoms that fail conservative care may occasionally require surgical intervention.

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Daniel Dodson, DO)

"You don't diagnose these things from incidental findings on X-rays." Always correlate the clinical extension-pain syndrome with the patient in front of you.

Second-Order Board Clue

Asymptomatic patient told they have "facet arthropathy" on an incidental film → reassure and treat clinically rather than ordering aggressive interventions.

Degenerative Disc Disease & Cervical Spondylosis

LO3 • Continuum Model

Etiology & Structural Progression

  • Spondylosis = Spinal Osteoarthritis: Used clinically alongside DDD; aging is the single most common cause, compounded by excess body weight, prior spinal trauma, and repetitive occupational neck/back stress.
  • Degenerative Cascade: Disc dehydration and collapse increase bone-on-bone friction across vertebral endplates, triggering later-stage osteophyte (bone spur) formation on X-ray.

Clinical Presentation

  • Most common in the cervical and lumbar spine.
  • Patients present with axial neck or low back pain, morning stiffness, and reduced range of motion (ROM).
  • If osteophytes or disc bulges narrow the neural foramina or central canal, patients can develop radiculopathy or cervical myelopathy.

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Daniel Dodson, DO)

Osteoarthritis, spondylosis, and DDD are used interchangeably in clinic — in radiology it is simply a descriptive continuum of disc space loss and osteophyte spurs.

Second-Order Board Clue

Older laborer with chronic neck stiffness, ↓ cervical ROM, and X-ray showing narrowed disc spaces + anterior/posterior osteophytes = Cervical spondylosis.

Conservative Rehabilitation vs. Surgical Risks

LO3 • Management Decision

Why Conservative Care Comes First

  • Prioritize physical therapy, proper abdominal/core muscle activation, anti-inflammatory medications, weight/ergonomic modification, and OMT before considering surgery.
  • Without neurologic emergencies (e.g., cauda equina, progressive myelopathy), non-operative management avoids irreversible structural alteration.

Dr. Dodson's Surgical Caveats

  • Rule of Threes: Roughly 1/3 of spine surgery patients get better, 1/3 stay the same, and 1/3 get worse.
  • Laminectomy Instability: Removing posterior lamina/elements or fusing a segment transfers mechanical loads, destabilizing adjacent vertebral levels and accelerating adjacent-level degeneration.

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Daniel Dodson, DO)

Always counsel patients honestly using the Rule of Threes before elective back surgery — teaching correct abdominal/core mechanics is foundational to long-term relief.

Second-Order Board Clue

Patient with chronic axial spondylosis without motor deficit or cauda equina asks for immediate laminectomy → next best step is structured conservative therapy & core rehab.

Inflammatory Back Pain & Multisystem Features

LO4 • HLA-B27 & Clinical Pattern

Demographics & Inflammatory Criteria

  • Age & Onset: Chronic back pain almost always starting before age 40–45 with an insidious onset.
  • Mechanical Contrast: Pain improves with exercise, does NOT improve with rest, wakes patients at night (improves upon arising), and causes prominent morning stiffness.
  • Genetics: Strongly linked to HLA-B27; axial disease involves spine and bilateral sacroiliac (SI) joints, plus hips, shoulders, and costovertebral/sternoclavicular joints.

Subtypes & Extra-Spinal Features

  • r-axSpA (Ankylosing Spondylitis): Radiographic sacroiliitis on X-ray. nr-axSpA: Active SI inflammation on MRI (10–40% progress to radiographic AS within 2–10 years).
  • Articular/Periarticular: Peripheral arthritis, synovitis, enthesitis (tendon/ligament insertion inflammation), and dactylitis.
  • Non-Articular: Anterior uveitis, psoriasis, and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Daniel Dodson, DO)

"Younger person with sacroiliitis and HLA-B27 testing — that is one of my exam questions!" Remember the distinction between axial (spine/SI) and peripheral spondyloarthropathies.

Second-Order Board Clue

28-year-old with morning back stiffness that eases after a morning jog + painful red eye (uveitis) = test for HLA-B27 & SI joint imaging.

Rigid-Spine Fragility Fractures & Psychosocial Vigilance

LO4 • Complications

SI Joint Progression & Fragility Fractures

  • Bilateral SI joint disease evolves from active inflammation (MRI) → pseudo-widening → subchondral sclerosis & erosions → bony ankylosis (fusion).
  • Vertebral Fractures: Fragility fractures from trauma are at least twice as common (2x) in radiographic axSpA due to combined low bone mineral density (BMD) and spinal rigidity.
  • Most commonly involves the lower cervical spine, and of extension, flexion, and compression fractures, extension fracture is the most common.

Mandatory Mental Health Screening

  • Chronic disabling inflammatory pain, progressive stiffness, and systemic fatigue carry substantial psychiatric morbidity.
  • Perform routine depression and suicide screening in patients living with ankylosing spondylitis / axSpA alongside physical/pharmacologic disease control.

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Daniel Dodson, DO)

Ankylosed spines behave like a single rigid long bone with low BMD — even minor trauma in a patient with AS requires high suspicion for an unstable lower cervical extension fracture.

Second-Order Board Clue

Patient with long-standing AS sustains minor hyperextension fall and develops new neck pain → suspect lower cervical extension fracture (2x baseline fragility risk).

Repetitive Hyperextension & Oblique Radiography

LO5 • Pars Stress Injury

Pathogenesis & Clinical Presentation

  • Mechanism: Stress fracture in the pars interarticularis caused by repetitive hyperextension of the lumbar spine (classic in gymnasts, football linemen, and weightlifters).
  • Most Common Site: L5 is most frequently involved.
  • Symptoms: Many patients are asymptomatic; symptomatic patients have low back pain worse with activity and relieved with rest, localized tenderness, and paraspinal muscle spasms.

Oblique X-Ray Scotty Dog Anatomy

  • On an oblique lumbar X-ray, the posterior elements outline a "Scotty dog": Nose = transverse process, Eye = pedicle, Ear = superior articular process, Front leg = inferior articular process.
  • The collar around the Scotty dog's neck represents the radiolucent pars interarticularis fracture (defect of structural integrity).

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Daniel Dodson, DO)

"Something I like to ask on exams is: what is the defect that led to this?" Repetitive hyperextension in young athletes fractures the pars interarticularis — giving the Scotty dog its collar.

Second-Order Board Clue

16-year-old female gymnast with activity-induced LBP relieved by rest → order oblique lumbar X-rays looking for a Scotty dog collar at L5.

Anterior Slippage Pathways & Instability Precautions

LO5 • Slippage & Rehab

Etiology & Grading

  • Definition: One vertebra slips forward relative to the vertebra beneath it (most common in lumbar spine).
  • Pathways: (1) Spondylolysis eliminates posterior structural integrity so a young athlete presents years later with slippage; (2) Degenerative changes in aging discs cause segmental instability.
  • Meyerding Grading (1–5): Grade I (0–25%), Grade II (25–50%), Grade III (50–75%), Grade IV (75–100%), Grade V (>100% spondyloptosis).

Management & OMT Precautions

  • Conservative Care: Rest from hyperextension activities, bracing to stabilize the pars/segment, core stabilization physical therapy, and indirect OMT.
  • Safety Contraindication: Avoid high-velocity, low-amplitude (HVLA) thrust directly over an unstable spondylolisthesis segment.

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Daniel Dodson, DO)

Spondylolysis is often the precursor: "This is the case of the person who, five years later, presents with spondylolisthesis" because the pars fracture removed structural integrity.

Second-Order Board Clue

Anterior slippage on lateral X-ray in a 22-year-old former gymnast = isthmic spondylolisthesis secondary to prior L5 pars defect; treat initially with bracing & indirect OMT.

Wedge Mechanics, PPI Osteopenia & Malignancy Exclusion

LO6 • Fracture Pathology

Etiology & Pain Characteristics

  • Bone Density Loss: Osteopenia/osteoporosis weakens anterior vertebral trabeculae; Dr. Dodson highlights chronic proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy as an underrecognized driver of osteopenia.
  • Pain Character: Causes sharp, constant pain that does not wax and wane ("it just hurts"), localized to the fractured segment without initial neurologic symptoms because it is isolated bone failure.

Height-Loss Threshold & Workup

  • 50% Height Loss Rule: When anterior vertebral wedge collapse reaches ≥ 50% height loss, concern rises sharply for posterior/anterior central canal compression against the spinal cord.
  • Diagnostic Imaging: X-ray detects wedge deformity; obtain an urgent MRI to evaluate spinal cord compromise, acuity, and rule out pathologic fracture from metastatic malignancy.

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Daniel Dodson, DO)

Dr. Dodson shares his own T12 compression fracture sustained while lifting a motorcycle after developing osteopenia from long-term PPI use — always audit chronic PPIs and screen for bone loss!

Second-Order Board Clue

Patient on chronic omeprazole develops constant, non-remitting mid-back pain after lifting; X-ray shows 55% T12 height loss → order urgent MRI to assess canal compromise and exclude malignancy.

Hyperextension Bracing, OMM Contraindications & Disability

LO6 • Treatment & OMM

Bracing & Bone Health Restoration

  • Treat acute thoracic/thoracolumbar wedge compression fractures with a rigid hyperextension orthosis brace to unload the compressed anterior vertebral body during healing.
  • Address underlying bone fragility (deprescribe unnecessary long-term PPIs, evaluate bone mineral density).

Osteopathic Principles & Disability Assessments

  • Safe OMT: Use gentle indirect OMT to ease paraspinal guarding in acute compression fractures or spinal instability.
  • Absolute Contraindication: Strictly avoid HVLA over acute compression fractures, unstable spondylolisthesis, osteomyelitis/discitis, or metastatic lesions.
  • Disability Pitfall: Base disability evaluations on objective functional capacity and clinical exam rather than incidental X-ray arthritis alone.

Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Daniel Dodson, DO)

Never apply forceful thrust (HVLA) to an acute compression fracture or unstable slip — indirect osteopathic techniques respect tissue fragility while restoring comfort.

Second-Order Board Clue

Osteopenic patient with acute T12 wedge fracture and severe paraspinal spasm requests OMM → perform gentle indirect OMT and fit a rigid hyperextension orthosis.
