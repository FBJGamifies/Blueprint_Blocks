Osteoarthritis: Degenerative Joint Disease & OA vs. RA Foundation

Lecturer Rule: OA = Wear and Tear (Asymmetric, <30 min stiffness, spares MCPs)

Interactive OA Structural & Diagnostic Workbench

Inspect the structural progression from chondrocyte matrix failure to eburnation, osteophytes, and non-inflammatory synovial profile.

LO 1.01 • Pathogenesis

OA Etiology & Chondrocyte Injury

Epidemiology Most common adult joint disease
Top Risk Factors Age (most important) & obesity
Molecular Loss Type II collagen & proteoglycans
Key Negative Clue Degenerative; low-grade inflammation only

Mechanical Stress, Matrix Degradation & Repair Failure

LO 1.01 • OA Risk & Pathogenesis
Initiation & Risk Profile
  • Prevalence & Core Identity: Osteoarthritis is the most common joint disease in adults and is fundamentally a degenerative disorder caused by progressive loss of articular cartilage with secondary subchondral bone and synovial changes.
  • Primary & Mechanical Drivers: Age is the single most important risk factor because cartilage regenerative capacity declines over time; obesity adds both mechanical weight overload and metabolic burden.
  • Additional Predispositions: Joint injury or repetitive overuse (even in younger professional athletes), joint malalignment, female sex (hormonal changes), and genetic predisposition all accelerate wear.
Matrix Breakdown Cascade
  • Chondrocyte Stress: Mechanical stress combined with aging injures chondrocytes, triggering breakdown of the extracellular matrix.
  • Depleted Structural Components: Progressive loss of type II collagen and proteoglycans causes cartilage degeneration to exceed the joint's limited capacity to repair itself.
  • Secondary Mild Inflammation: Although non-inflammatory compared with autoimmune arthritis, OA develops a secondary low-grade inflammation as cartilage debris enters the joint space.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

"Even in younger individuals—specifically professional athletes—repetitive joint use and mechanical malalignment can make osteoarthritis evident early because cartilage degeneration exceeds repair."

Second-Order Board Clue

An older patient or athlete with chronic knee pain worsened by use has early matrix depletion characterized by loss of type II collagen and proteoglycans from injured chondrocytes.

OA Cartilage, Bone & Radiographic Signs

End-Stage Surface Eburnation (polished exposed bone)
Radiographic Quartet Osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis, cysts, narrowing
Detached Fragments Loose bodies ("joint mice")

Microscopic Progression & Classic Radiographic Findings

LO 1.02 • OA Morphology & Imaging
Histologic & Gross Evolution
  • Early Repair Attempt: Chondrocytes initially proliferate and form chondrocyte clusters attempting to repair damaged matrix.
  • Surface Breakdown: Loss of collagen and proteoglycans produces articular cartilage fibrillation and vertical fissuring.
  • Bone Exposure & Debris: Complete cartilage loss leads to eburnation (polished, ivory-like exposed subchondral bone), while sloughed cartilage/bone fragments form intra-articular loose bodies ("joint mice").
Radiographic Hallmarks
  • Joint Space & Spurs: Asymmetric joint-space narrowing from cartilage loss paired with marginal osteophytes (bone spurs) at joint edges.
  • Subchondral Sclerosis: Reactive thickening and increased bone density directly beneath the worn surface, appearing white on X-ray.
  • Subchondral Cysts: Fluid-filled cavities beneath the joint plate appearing as dark lucencies on radiographs.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

"On X-ray, remember subchondral sclerosis is increased bone density—white—while subchondral cysts are dark lucencies, accompanied by joint space narrowing and osteophytes."

Second-Order Board Clue

A resected femoral head showing a smooth, shiny, ivory-polished articular surface (eburnation) with adjacent subchondral cysts confirms advanced osteoarthritis rather than inflammatory pannus.

OA Clinical Pattern & Synovial Fluid

Pain & Stiffness Stiffness < 30 minutes; worse with activity
Hand Nodes Heberden (DIP) & Bouchard (PIP)
Key Negative Clue MCP joints spared; no systemic symptoms
Synovial Fluid Non-inflammatory: < 2,000 leukocytes/µL
Physical Sign Crepitus & limited range of motion

Wear-and-Tear Symptomatology, Distribution & Fluid Analysis

LO 1.03 • Clinical Presentation & Labs
Symptoms & Joint Distribution
  • Mechanical Stiffness & Pain: Morning stiffness lasts < 30 minutes (transient immobility reduces synovial fluid circulation and lubrication), while pain worsens with joint use and improves with rest (persistent only in advanced disease).
  • Physical Findings: Irregular articular surfaces produce crepitus (grinding/crackling sensation) and limited range of motion, typically with no systemic symptoms.
  • Asymmetric Pattern: Involves knees, hips, cervical/lumbar spine, 1st carpometacarpal (thumb) joint, DIP, and PIP joints; Heberden nodes (DIP osteophytes) and Bouchard nodes (PIP osteophytes) are classic, while MCP joints are spared.
Synovial Fluid & Serology
  • Non-Inflammatory Profile: Normal synovial fluid holds ~200 leukocytes/µL; in OA, leukocytes remain < 2,000/µL (mild rise reflecting secondary low-grade inflammation).
  • Negative Autoimmune Markers: Both RF and anti-CCP are negative, and acute-phase reactants are not prominently elevated.
  • Board Mnemonic: OA = Wear and Tear (older age, weight-bearing joints, asymmetric, pain worse with activity).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

"Board pearls in the orange box: Heberden nodes at DIP, Bouchard nodes at PIP, MCPs spared, and typically asymmetric. Please remember these—they are heavily tested."

Second-Order Board Clue

A 68-year-old woman with asymmetric knee pain relieved by rest, bony enlargements at the DIP (Heberden nodes) and PIP (Bouchard nodes) joints, and normal MCP joints will have synovial fluid WBC < 2,000/µL.

TOPIC / LO 2

Rheumatoid Arthritis & Extra-Articular Rheumatoid Nodules

Lecturer Rule: RA = Morning Flare (>1 hr stiffness, symmetric MCP/PIP/wrist, spares DIPs)

Interactive RA Immune Cascade & Structural Pathway Circuit

Step through the pathogenesis of rheumatoid arthritis from HLA-DR4 priming and pannus invasion to radiographic erosions and extra-articular nodules.

LO 2.01 • Genetics & Immunology

RA Autoimmune Initiation & Cytokines

Sex & Genetics Women ≈ 3:1; HLA-DR4 (HLA-DRB1)
Trigger & Antigen Smoking → citrullinated peptides
Autoantibodies Anti-CCP (ACPA) & RF (IgM vs IgG Fc)
Key Cytokines TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, IL-17
Bone Erosion Driver RANKL activates osteoclasts

Genetic Susceptibility, Immune Complexes & Osteoclast Activation

LO 2.01 • RA Pathogenesis
Tolerance Breakdown & Complexes
  • Host & Environment: Predominantly affects women (3:1) carrying HLA-DR4 (HLA-DRB1 MHC class II) alleles exposed to environmental triggers such as smoking.
  • Citrullination & ACPA: Environmental injury induces citrullination of self-proteins, driving formation of ACPA (anti-CCP) antibodies, which are more specific for RA than RF.
  • Rheumatoid Factor & Deposition: Rheumatoid factor (RF) is usually an IgM antibody against the Fc portion of IgG; ACPA and RF form immune complexes that ignite synovial inflammation.
T-Cell Activation & Cytokine Cascade
  • CD4+ T-Cell Subsets: Synovial antigen presentation activates CD4+ Th1 and Th17 cells, amplifying chronic synovitis.
  • Major Inflammatory Mediators: Key cytokines include TNF-α (a major therapeutic target), IL-1, IL-6, and IL-17.
  • Mechanism of Bone Erosion: Inflammatory synovium upregulates RANKL, which activates osteoclasts to resorb bone at joint margins.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

"Remember from this slide: anti-CCP antibodies are highly specific for RA, TNF-α is a major therapeutic target, RANKL-mediated osteoclast activation causes bone erosion, and pannus is the hallmark destructive lesion."

Second-Order Board Clue

A smoker carrying HLA-DR4 develops symmetric wrist pain and marginal bone erosions; the bone destruction is mediated by RANKL-driven osteoclast activation downstream of TNF-α and Th1/Th17 cytokines.

LO 2.02 • Synovial Histology

Pannus Formation & Synovial Pathology

Hallmark Lesion Pannus (proliferative synovium)
Low-Power Histology Synovial hyperplasia with villous projections
High-Power Infiltrate Lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate & germinal centers
Synovial Fluid 5,000–50,000 leukocytes/µL; ↑CRP, ↑ESR

Synovial Hyperplasia, Villous Projections & Inflammatory Synovitis

LO 2.02 • Morphologic Changes in RA
Histologic Architecture
  • Pannus Definition: Pannus is an inflamed, proliferative mass of synovium, granulation tissue, and inflammatory cells that grows over and destroys articular cartilage and underlying bone.
  • Low-Magnification View: Marked synovial hyperplasia (hypertrophy) forming prominent villous projections into the joint cavity, accompanied by synovial hypervascularity.
  • High-Magnification View: Dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate with organized germinal center formation and abundant plasma cells actively secreting autoantibodies locally.
Inflammatory Fluid & Labs
  • Prominent Synovitis: Synovial fluid aspiration reveals 5,000–50,000 leukocytes/µL (markedly inflammatory, unlike OA's < 2,000/µL).
  • Systemic Inflammatory Markers: Elevated CRP and ESR parallel active synovial inflammation and systemic symptoms (fatigue, fever, weight loss).
  • Overnight Exudate Mechanism: Synovial inflammation causes overnight accumulation of inflammatory exudates and edema, producing prolonged morning stiffness that eases once joint movement clears fluid.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

"At low magnification you see marked synovial hyperplasia with villous projections—this is pannus. At high magnification you see dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates with germinal centers and abundant plasma cells producing autoantibodies."

Second-Order Board Clue

A synovial biopsy showing villous projections, lymphoid germinal centers, and cartilage erosion by proliferative granulation tissue identifies pannus in a patient with synovial WBC 5,000–50,000/µL.

Key Negative Clue DIP joints spared; ~20% seronegative
Classic Deformities Ulnar deviation, swan-neck, boutonnière

Symmetric Small-Joint Polyarthritis, Serology & Radiographic Progression

LO 2.03 • Clinical, Serologic & X-Ray Features
Clinical Presentation & Deformities
  • Morning Flare Pattern: Warm, swollen, painful symmetric polyarthritis with morning stiffness > 1 hour that worsens after inactivity and improves with activity, plus systemic fatigue, fever, and weight loss.
  • Target vs. Spared Joints: Primarily affects MCP, PIP, wrists, and feet, while DIP joints are usually spared (critical distinction from OA and psoriatic arthritis).
  • Chronic Deformities: Progressive joint and tendon damage produces ulnar deviation, swan-neck deformity, and boutonnière deformity, and advanced disease may progress to ankylosis.
Serology & Radiographic Findings
  • Serologic Caveat: Anti-CCP (ACPA) is more specific than RF; however, approximately 20% of RA patients are seronegative, making clinical and radiographic features essential for diagnosis.
  • Early X-Ray Event: Periarticular osteopenia (loss of bone density immediately adjacent to the inflamed joint) appears early.
  • Erosive Triad: Followed by marginal erosions (bone destruction at joint margins) and symmetric joint-space narrowing.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

"Approximately 20% of patients are seronegative; in these cases we rely on clinical and radiographic findings—that's why knowing periarticular osteopenia, marginal erosions, and symmetric narrowing is so important."

Second-Order Board Clue

A 42-year-old woman with negative RF/CCP still meets diagnostic criteria if she exhibits symmetric MCP and PIP synovitis with DIP sparing, stiffness > 1 hour, and marginal erosions with periarticular osteopenia.

Multisystem Extra-Articular Involvement & Nodule Histopathology

LO 2.04 • Extra-Articular RA
Rheumatoid Nodule Pathology
  • Clinical Presentation: Rheumatoid nodules are the most common extra-articular manifestation of RA—firm, painless subcutaneous nodules forming over extensor pressure points (such as the forearm/elbow).
  • Seropositive Link: Strongly associated with seropositive RA (RF and/or anti-CCP positive).
  • Characteristic Histology: A zone of central fibrinoid necrosis rimmed by palisaded macrophages (histiocytes) and an outer cuff of lymphocytes and plasma cells.
Systemic & Ocular Manifestations
  • Cardiopulmonary Involvement: Pleuritis, pericarditis, and interstitial lung disease (ILD) reflect systemic autoimmune inflammation.
  • Vascular Inflammation: Systemic vasculitis can complicate severe seropositive RA.
  • Ophthalmic Disease: Episcleritis/scleritis and keratoconjunctivitis sicca (dry eyes) are key extra-articular clues.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

"Rheumatoid arthritis is a multisystem autoimmune disease—arthritis may be just one manifestation. Remember rheumatoid nodules show central fibrinoid necrosis rimmed by palisaded macrophages."

Second-Order Board Clue

Biopsy of a firm, painless subcutaneous elbow nodule in a patient with symmetric wrist synovitis reveals central fibrinoid necrosis surrounded by palisading macrophages, confirming seropositive rheumatoid arthritis.

Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (Formerly Juvenile RA)

Lecturer Rule: Age <16, ≥6 wks, oligoarthritis/knees, ANA+, NO nodules, chronic anterior uveitis

LO 3.01 • Criteria & Clinical

JIA Criteria, Pathogenesis & Joint Pattern

Diagnostic Threshold Age < 16 years; duration ≥ 6 weeks
Joint Predilection Oligoarthritis; large joints (knees)
Systemic Features Fever, rash, lymphadenopathy
Genetics & Cytokines HLA & PTPN22; IL-1, IL-17, TNF-α

Diagnostic Definition, Susceptibility Variants & Pediatric Pattern

LO 3.01 • JIA Overview
Definition & Clinical Presentation
  • Definition & Epidemiology: Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (JIA) (formerly juvenile RA) is a heterogeneous group of disorders of unknown cause beginning before age 16 years and lasting ≥ 6 weeks; it is the most common chronic rheumatic disease of childhood.
  • Large-Joint Oligoarthritis: Unlike adult RA's symmetric small-joint polyarthritis, JIA more commonly presents as oligoarthritis affecting large joints (especially knees).
  • Systemic Manifestations: Systemic presentations feature fever, rash, and lymphadenopathy more prominently than adult RA.
Shared & Distinct Pathogenesis
  • Genetic Susceptibility: Pathogenesis parallels adult RA with HLA associations and PTPN22 susceptibility variants.
  • Effector Pathways: Driven by Th1 and Th17 activation with elevated IL-1, IL-17, and TNF-α.
  • Contrast with Adult RA: Pediatric presentation shifts toward fewer, larger weight-bearing joints and prominent systemic inflammatory signs.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

"Remember the two defining numbers for JIA: arthritis beginning before age 16 years and lasting 6 weeks or longer, with oligoarthritis and large joints like the knees."

Second-Order Board Clue

A 7-year-old child with persistent unilateral knee swelling for 8 weeks, fever, and rash carries HLA and PTPN22 risk variants driving Th1/Th17 cytokine (IL-1, IL-17, TNF-α) synovitis.

LO 3.02 • Serology & Complication

JIA Serology, Negative Clues & Uveitis

Serologic Hallmarks ANA positivity is common
Key Negative Clue Rheumatoid nodules usually absent
Major Complication Chronic anterior uveitis
Clinical Priority Monitor eyes for uveitis

ANA Positivity, Absence of Nodules & Ocular Complication

LO 3.02 • JIA Labs & Complications
Laboratory & Pathologic Distinctions
  • ANA Positivity: Antinuclear antibodies (ANA) are commonly positive in JIA—especially important for stratifying ocular risk.
  • Absence of Adult RA Hallmarks: Unlike seropositive adult RA, rheumatoid nodules are usually absent in JIA.
  • Diagnostic Differentiation: Combining oligoarthritis, ANA positivity, and absent rheumatoid nodules cleanly separates JIA from adult RA.
Ocular Complication Vigilance
  • Signature Extra-Articular Risk: An important complication of JIA is chronic anterior uveitis.
  • Clinical Vigilance: Because anterior chamber inflammation can progress without obvious articular correlation, recognizing the link between pediatric oligoarthritis, ANA positivity, and chronic anterior uveitis is critical to prevent vision loss.
  • Board Contrast: JIA causes chronic anterior uveitis, whereas adult seronegative spondyloarthropathies (like AS) classically present with acute anterior uveitis.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

"In JIA, antinuclear antibodies (ANA) are pretty common, rheumatoid nodules are usually absent, and an important complication to remember is chronic anterior uveitis."

Second-Order Board Clue

A young girl with ANA-positive knee oligoarthritis lasting ≥ 6 weeks and no rheumatoid nodules must be evaluated for chronic anterior uveitis.

Seronegative Spondyloarthropathies (SpA): AS, Reactive, Psoriatic & IBD

Lecturer Rule: Think HLA-B27 whenever you see sacroiliitis, enthesitis, or uveitis

Interactive Seronegative Spondyloarthropathy Discriminator Workbench

Select each RF-negative, HLA-B27–associated disorder to compare its axial/peripheral pattern, extra-articular clues, and radiographic hallmarks.

LO 4.01 • Axial SpA Prototype

Ankylosing Spondylitis (AS)

Genetics & Onset ~90% HLA-B27+; young adults (2nd–3rd decade)
Clinical Hallmark Inflammatory back pain improves with activity
Earliest X-Ray Sign Bilateral symmetric sacroiliitis
Late Spine Sign Bamboo spine & enthesitis
Extra-Articular Risk Acute anterior uveitis & aortic regurgitation

Bilateral Sacroiliitis, Bamboo Spine & Systemic Associations

LO 4.01 • Ankylosing Spondylitis
Shared SpA & Axial Mechanics
  • Seronegative Group Identity: All seronegative spondyloarthropathies are negative for rheumatoid factor (RF), share HLA-B27 association, target the axial skeleton (sacroiliac joints), and feature enthesitis and uveitis.
  • Ankylosing Spondylitis Profile: Begins in young adults (2nd–3rd decades) with ~90% HLA-B27 positivity, involving sacroiliac joints and vertebral apophyseal joints.
  • Pain & Extra-Articular Clues: Morning stiffness and inflammatory low back pain improve with activity; extra-articular associations include acute anterior uveitis and aortic regurgitation.
Classic Radiographic Progression
  • Bilateral Symmetric Sacroiliitis: Earliest and hallmark radiographic finding—bilateral symmetric erosions with iliac-side subchondral sclerosis and progressive joint-space narrowing leading to ankylosis.
  • Enthesitis & Fusion: Chronic inflammation at tendon/ligament insertions (enthesitis) leads to progressive bony ankylosis and loss of spinal mobility.
  • Bamboo Spine: Advanced vertebral fusion produces a rigid, straight spinal column termed bamboo spine on radiograph.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

"Board pearl: think HLA-B27 whenever you see sacroiliitis, enthesitis, or uveitis. For ankylosing spondylitis, remember the classic pair: bamboo spine plus bilateral symmetric sacroiliitis."

Second-Order Board Clue

A 25-year-old man with morning low back stiffness relieved by exercise, acute anterior uveitis, and an early diastolic murmur (aortic regurgitation) will show bilateral symmetric sacroiliitis with iliac-side sclerosis and HLA-B27 positivity.

LO 4.02 • Post-Infectious SpA

Reactive Arthritis (Reiter Syndrome)

Mechanism & Timing Sterile immune reaction 1–4 wks post-infection
Bacterial Triggers Chlamydia trachomatis; Salmonella/Shigella/Yersinia/Campylobacter
Classic Triad Arthritis, urethritis, conjunctivitis/uveitis
Mucocutaneous Keratoderma blennorrhagicum & oral ulcers
Genetics & Tx ~80% HLA-B27+; NSAIDs (DMARDs/biologics)

Sterile Post-Infectious Immune Arthritis, Triad & Skin Lesions

LO 4.02 • Reactive Arthritis
Sterile Immune Pathogenesis & Triggers
  • Sterile Synovitis: Acute sterile seronegative spondyloarthropathy—no microorganisms reside inside the joint; rather, an immune reaction provoked 1–4 weeks after an infection drives joint inflammation.
  • GU & GI Triggers: Preceded by genitourinary Chlamydia trachomatis or enteric infections (Salmonella, Shigella, Yersinia, Campylobacter).
  • Genetics & Distribution: Approximately 80% are HLA-B27 positive; joint involvement is typically an asymmetric oligoarthritis of knees, ankles, and feet, with variable/asymmetric sacroiliitis.
Classic Triad, Skin Signs & Therapy
  • Classic Mnemonic Triad: "Can't see, can't pee, can't climb a tree" → conjunctivitis/uveitis, urethritis/cervicitis, and arthritis.
  • Extra-Articular Hallmarks: Enthesitis, oral mucosal ulcers, and keratoderma blennorrhagicum (characteristic hyperkeratotic skin lesion).
  • Management: First-line therapy uses NSAIDs; persistent disease may require DMARDs or biologic therapy.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

"Sterile means there are no microorganisms in the joint—it is an immune reaction provoked 1 to 4 weeks after a GU or GI infection. Remember: 'Can't see, can't pee, can't climb a tree.'"

Second-Order Board Clue

A young adult with asymmetric knee/ankle swelling, dysuria, conjunctivitis, and keratoderma blennorrhagicum 2 weeks after diarrheal illness has sterile synovial fluid (negative joint culture) driven by HLA-B27 cross-reactivity.

LO 4.03 • Cutaneous SpA

Psoriatic Arthritis (PsA)

Preceding Condition Psoriasis of skin or nails
Digit Hallmarks DIP involvement & dactylitis ("sausage digits")
Radiographic Sign "Pencil-in-cup" deformity
Key Negative Clue No rheumatoid nodules; RF negative
Axial Genetics HLA-B27 strongest in axial disease

DIP Targeting, Sausage Digits & Pencil-in-Cup Radiology

LO 4.03 • Psoriatic Arthritis
Clinical & Genetic Features
  • Cutaneous Association: Chronic inflammatory arthritis that usually follows psoriasis of the skin or nails.
  • Signature Digit Findings: Prominent DIP joint involvement (unlike RA which spares DIPs), dactylitis ("sausage digits") from diffuse digit swelling, and enthesitis.
  • Axial & Negative Markers: Common, often asymmetric sacroiliitis and axial disease (where HLA-B27 association is strongest), with no rheumatoid nodules.
Radiographic & Differential Clues
  • Pathognomonic X-Ray Sign: Distal interphalangeal erosion and phalangeal tapering produce the classic "pencil-in-cup" deformity.
  • Differentiating from OA: Both PsA and OA affect DIP joints, but PsA shows inflammatory synovitis, dactylitis, skin/nail psoriasis, and pencil-in-cup erosions rather than osteophytes.
  • Differentiating from RA: PsA targets DIP joints, lacks RF and rheumatoid nodules, and frequently exhibits dactylitis and asymmetric sacroiliitis.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

"Classic clue for psoriatic arthritis: psoriasis plus DIP involvement and dactylitis ('sausage digits'), with pencil-in-cup deformity on X-ray and no rheumatoid nodules."

Second-Order Board Clue

A patient with nail pitting and swollen, tender "sausage digits" (dactylitis) involving the DIP joints will demonstrate "pencil-in-cup" erosive changes on hand radiographs and negative RF.

LO 4.04 • Gut-Associated SpA

Enteropathic (IBD-Associated) Arthritis

Underlying Disease Crohn disease & ulcerative colitis
Incidence in IBD Occurs in ~20% of IBD patients
Peripheral Targets Knees and ankles, enthesitis, dactylitis
Axial Pattern Sacroiliitis/spondylitis (HLA-B27 axial)
Treatment Strategy Control IBD, cautious NSAIDs, anti-TNF

Peripheral & Axial Joint Manifestations in Inflammatory Bowel Disease

LO 4.04 • Enteropathic Arthritis
Dual Articular Phenotypes
  • IBD Prevalence: Chronic inflammatory arthritis occurring in approximately 20% of patients with Crohn disease or ulcerative colitis, accompanied by GI symptoms (abdominal pain, diarrhea, weight loss).
  • Peripheral Manifestation: Arthritis predominantly involving large lower-extremity joints (knees and ankles), alongside enthesitis and dactylitis.
  • Axial Manifestation: Sacroiliitis and spondylitis closely resembling ankylosing spondylitis; HLA-B27 is primarily associated with this axial disease subset.
Management Considerations
  • Primary Strategy: Treatment centers on control of the underlying IBD to dampen gut-driven systemic inflammation.
  • NSAID Caution: NSAIDs must be used with caution in enteropathic arthritis because of underlying intestinal mucosal disease.
  • Biologic Therapy: Anti-TNF therapy effectively treats both active inflammatory bowel disease and associated spondyloarthropathy.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian)

"Enteropathic arthritis occurs in about 20% of IBD patients and has two major parts: peripheral arthritis (especially knees and ankles) and axial sacroiliitis/spondylitis, where HLA-B27 is primarily linked."

Second-Order Board Clue

A patient with chronic diarrhea, weight loss, and knee/ankle swelling plus sacroiliitis requires control of underlying IBD and anti-TNF therapy rather than unrestricted NSAIDs.
