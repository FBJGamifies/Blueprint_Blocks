Osteoarthritis (OA) — Degenerative Joint Disease

Lecturer Rule: "Wear and Tear" — pain worsens with activity, stiffness <30 min, MCP spared

Osteoarthritis Joint Architecture & Nodule Discriminator Workbench

Inspect structural cartilage progression, hand osteophyte distribution, and synovial fluid thresholds.

LO 1.01 • Pathogenesis

Mechanical Wear-and-Tear & Risk Factors

Most Important Risk Advancing age
Matrix Depletion Type II collagen & proteoglycans
Cellular Target Chondrocyte injury
Inflammatory Tone Secondary low-grade inflammation

Pathogenesis of Degenerative Joint Disease

LO 1.01 • Slides 3–4
Etiology & Risk Profile
  • Prevalence & Core Definition: Osteoarthritis is the most common joint disease in adults, defined as a degenerative joint disease caused by progressive loss of articular cartilage with secondary changes in subchondral bone and synovium.
  • Primary Drivers: Age is the single most important risk factor, amplified by obesity, joint injury or repetitive use, joint malalignment, female sex, and genetic predisposition.
Chondrocyte & Matrix Cascade
  • Biomechanical Trigger: Mechanical stress combined with aging induces chondrocyte injury, disrupting normal cartilage homeostasis.
  • Net Cartilage Loss: Injured chondrocytes fail to sustain the extracellular matrix, resulting in loss of type II collagen and proteoglycans so cartilage degeneration exceeds repair, triggering secondary low-grade inflammation.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian) "Osteoarthritis is fundamentally a degenerative cartilage disorder driven by mechanical stress and aging—not a primary autoimmune synovitis. Any inflammation present is secondary and low-grade."
Second-Order Board Clue An older obese patient with chronic knee pain undergoes joint fluid sampling showing minimal leukocytes; the earliest biochemical defect driving cartilage softening is depletion of type II collagen and proteoglycans following chondrocyte injury.
LO 1.02 • Clinical Profile

Clinical Manifestations & Joint Distribution

Morning Stiffness Less than 30 minutes
Pain Pattern Worse with use, relieved by rest
Physical Exam Sign Crepitus + Heberden nodes
Key Negative Clue MCP joints usually spared

Asymmetric Mechanics, Hand Nodes & Synovial Fluid

LO 1.02 • Slides 4–5
Symptom & Fluid Dynamics
  • Mechanical Stiffness (<30 min): Transient joint immobility reduces synovial fluid circulation and lubrication of damaged cartilage, producing brief stiffness lasting less than 30 minutes that improves with initial activity.
  • Activity-Related Pain & Crepitus: Pain worsens with joint use and improves with rest (though advanced disease may cause persistent pain); irregular articular surfaces cause crepitus and limited range of motion without systemic symptoms.
  • Non-Inflammatory Synovial Fluid: Arthrocentesis yields less than 2,000 leukocytes/μL.
Joint Distribution & Nodes
  • Asymmetric Load-Bearing Pattern: Typically asymmetric, targeting knees, hips, cervical and lumbar spine, 1st carpometacarpal joint (thumb base), and DIP/PIP joints.
  • Hand Osteophytes: Heberden nodes are osteophytes at the distal interphalangeal joints (DIP), whereas Bouchard nodes are osteophytes at the proximal interphalangeal joints (PIP).
  • Critical Exclusion: Unlike Rheumatoid Arthritis, MCP joints are usually spared.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian) "Memorize the hand mapping: Heberden nodes = DIP, Bouchard nodes = PIP, and MCP joints are spared in OA. Morning stiffness is mechanical and lasts under 30 minutes."
Second-Order Board Clue A 68-year-old seamstress presents with bony enlargement at the distal finger joints and pain at the base of the thumb that worsens by evening; arthrocentesis will demonstrate non-inflammatory synovial fluid with less than 2,000 leukocytes/μL.
LO 1.03 • Morphology

OA Morphology & Radiographic Hallmarks

Early Histology Cartilage fibrillation & fissuring
Polished Bone Eburnation

Intra-Articular Fragments Loose bodies (joint mice)

From Chondrocyte Clustering to Eburnation & Radiographs

LO 1.03 • Slides 5–7
Microscopic Progression
  • Early Microscopic Changes: Compensatory chondrocyte proliferation and clustering occur alongside loss of type II collagen and proteoglycans, causing vertical and horizontal cartilage fibrillation and fissuring.
  • Advanced Bone Remodeling: Full-thickness cartilage erosion leaves exposed, polished subchondral bone known as eburnation, accompanied by subchondral cysts and marginal osteophytes.
  • Free Fragments: Dislodged cartilage and bone fragments inside the joint space form loose bodies, classically termed joint mice.
Radiographic Signs
  • Joint-Space Narrowing: Asymmetric loss of radiolucent articular cartilage width across the weight-bearing compartment.
  • Subchondral Sclerosis: Increased subchondral bone density appearing bright white immediately beneath the articular surface.
  • Subchondral Cysts & Osteophytes: Dark radiolucent subchondral cysts beneath the cortex and bony osteophytes (bone spurs) at joint margins.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian) "Know the four classic radiographic hallmarks of OA: joint-space narrowing, osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis (white), and subchondral cysts (dark lucencies)."
Second-Order Board Clue Gross inspection of a resected femoral head from a patient with severe hip pain reveals an ivory-like, smooth, glistening articular surface where cartilage is completely absent; this finding is termed eburnation.
LO 1.04 • Management

Stepped Osteoarthritis Management

First-Line Non-Pharm Weight loss + exercise
Topical Analgesia Topical NSAIDs (diclofenac)
Intra-Articular Option Corticosteroids / hyaluronic acid
Definitive End-Stage Arthroplasty

From Non-Pharmacologic Core to Definitive Arthroplasty

LO 1.04 • Slide 8
First-Line & Symptomatic Care
  • Core Non-Pharmacologic Therapy: Weight loss (if overweight) combined with exercise and physical therapy focused on strengthening muscles supporting the joint and range-of-motion exercises.
  • Symptomatic Medications: Acetaminophen, topical NSAIDs (such as diclofenac gel), and topical capsaicin.
Advanced & End-Stage Options
  • Intra-Articular Interventions: Intra-articular corticosteroid injections or viscosupplementation using hyaluronic acid ("synovial fluid" injections).
  • Definitive Surgical Cure: For severe symptomatic end-stage OA, definitive treatment is arthroplasty (joint replacement).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian) "First-line management is always weight loss, exercise, and topical NSAIDs—do not jump to surgery. Definitive treatment for end-stage OA is arthroplasty."
Second-Order Board Clue A 71-year-old with advanced tricompartmental knee OA and refractory pain despite physical therapy, topical diclofenac gel, and intra-articular injections requires definitive arthroplasty.

Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA) & Extra-Articular Rheumatoid Nodules

Lecturer Rule: "Morning Flare" — symmetric MCP/PIP (DIP spared), stiffness >1 hr, pannus + anti-CCP

Autoimmune Synovitis & Pannus Destruction Circuit

Step through the immunopathogenic cascade from HLA-DR4 citrullination to pannus erosion, deformities, and rheumatoid nodules.

LO 2.01 • Immunopathogenesis

HLA-DR4, Autoantibodies, Cytokines & Pannus

Genetic + Trigger HLA-DR4 + smoking
Specific Antibody Anti-CCP antibodies (ACPA)
Rheumatoid Factor IgM against Fc of IgG
Erosive Mediator TNF-α + RANKL
Destructive Lesion Pannus formation

Autoimmune Synovitis & Pannus-Mediated Joint Destruction

LO 2.01 • Slides 9–11
Tolerance Breakdown & Serology
  • Susceptibility & Citrullination: HLA-DR4 (HLA-DRB1 alleles) combined with environmental triggers such as smoking promotes citrullination of self-proteins, driving anti-CCP antibodies (ACPA), which are highly specific for RA.
  • Rheumatoid Factor & Immune Complexes: Rheumatoid Factor (usually an IgM antibody against the Fc portion of IgG) and ACPA form immune complexes that perpetuate synovial inflammation.
Cytokine Network & Pannus Histology
  • T-Cell & Cytokine Drivers: Activated CD4+ Th1 and Th17 cells release TNF-α (major cytokine and therapeutic target), IL-1, and IL-6 (synovitis & cartilage destruction), IL-17 (leukocyte recruitment), IFN-γ (macrophage activation), and RANKL (osteoclast activation and bone erosion).
  • Synovial Pathology: Marked synovial hyperplasia with villous projections, dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate, and germinal center formation create pannus—inflamed proliferating synovium that destroys cartilage and bone, progressing to fibrous and bony ankylosis.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian) "Pannus is the hallmark destructive lesion of RA. Anti-CCP antibodies are more specific than RF, TNF-α is a major therapeutic target, and RANKL-mediated osteoclast activation causes bone erosion."
Second-Order Board Clue A synovial biopsy showing villous hypertrophy and lymphoid follicles with germinal centers belongs to a smoker carrying HLA-DR4; the molecule directly stimulating osteoclast differentiation at the bone margin is RANKL.

Morning Flare Mechanics, Hand Deformities & Subcutaneous Nodules

LO 2.02 • Slides 9, 13–17
Clinical Joint & Hand Findings
  • Inflammatory Rhythm: Warm, swollen, painful joints with morning stiffness lasting greater than 1 hour due to overnight accumulation of inflammatory exudates and edema; unlike OA, pain worsens after inactivity and improves with activity.
  • Symmetric Small-Joint Pattern: Affects women (~3:1) in MCP, PIP, wrists, and feet while DIP joints are usually spared; ~20% of patients are seronegative.
  • Classic Deformities: Ulnar deviation at the MCP joints, swan-neck deformity (DIP flexion, PIP hyperextension), and boutonniere deformity (DIP hyperextension, PIP flexion).
Extra-Articular & Rheumatoid Nodules
  • Most Common Extra-Articular Lesion: Rheumatoid nodules are firm, painless, nonmobile subcutaneous nodules over pressure points (elbows, extensor forearm) strongly linked to seropositive RF and severe systemic disease (occasionally in internal organs).
  • Pathognomonic Biopsy: Central fibrinoid necrosis rimmed by palisading macrophages, surrounded by lymphocytes and plasma cells.
  • Multisystem Involvement: Pleuritis, pericarditis, interstitial lung disease, vasculitis, episcleritis/scleritis, and keratoconjunctivitis sicca, plus systemic fatigue, fever, and weight loss.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian) "Rheumatoid nodule = central fibrinoid necrosis surrounded by palisading macrophages in a patient with seropositive RA. Remember swan-neck = DIP flexion / PIP hyperextension, whereas boutonniere = DIP hyperextension / PIP flexion."
Second-Order Board Clue Biopsy of a firm, painless nodule on the extensor forearm of a patient with bilateral wrist swelling and morning stiffness >90 minutes reveals central fibrinoid necrosis surrounded by palisading macrophages, predicting RF positivity.
LO 2.03 • Radiology & Contrast

RA Radiographs & "Wear and Tear vs. Morning Flare"

Early X-Ray Event Periarticular osteopenia
Bone Destruction Marginal erosions
Synovial Leukocytes 5,000–50,000 leukocytes/μL
Course Mnemonic Wear and Tear vs Morning Flare

Radiologic Hallmarks & Direct OA vs. RA Comparison

LO 2.03 • Slides 12, 18, 20–21
Radiologic & Inflammatory Profile
  • Radiographic Triad: Early periarticular osteopenia (bone density loss adjacent to the joint), marginal erosions (bone destruction at joint margins), and symmetric joint-space narrowing; late disease leads to deformity and ankylosis.
  • Prominent Synovitis: Synovial fluid contains 5,000–50,000 leukocytes/μL with elevated CRP and ESR (vs. OA ≤2,000 leukocytes/μL).
"Wear and Tear vs. Morning Flare"
  • OA ("Wear and Tear"): Gradual onset, older age, asymmetric weight-bearing + DIP/PIP (MCP spared), pain worse with activity, morning stiffness <30 min, osteophytes + subchondral sclerosis, RF−/anti-CCP−.
  • RA ("Morning Flare"): Insidious onset, HLA-DR4/smoking/female sex, symmetric MCP/PIP/wrists (DIP spared), morning stiffness >1 hour improving with activity, systemic fatigue/fever/weight loss, erosions + osteopenia, RF+/anti-CCP+.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian) "Contrast the X-rays directly: OA builds extra bone (osteophytes and white subchondral sclerosis), whereas RA destroys bone (periarticular osteopenia and marginal erosions)."
Second-Order Board Clue Hand radiographs in early symmetrical MCP/PIP synovitis before frank cortical destruction has occurred will most likely reveal periarticular osteopenia rather than subchondral sclerosis or osteophytes.
LO 2.04 • Pharmacotherapy

DMARDs, Biologics & Opportunistic TB Risk

First-Line DMARD Methotrexate (MTX)
Symptom Bridge Only Glucocorticoids & NSAIDs
Add-On Biologics TNF-α inhibitors
Critical Biologic Risk Reactivation of tuberculosis

Slowing Disease Progression vs. Symptom Control

LO 2.04 • Slide 19
Disease-Modifying Strategy
  • Anchor DMARD: Methotrexate (MTX) is the first-line disease-modifying antirheumatic drug to slow joint destruction.
  • Symptom-Only Agents: Glucocorticoids and NSAIDs provide rapid symptom relief but do not prevent disease progression.
TNF-α Blockade & Infection Risk
  • Biologic Escalation: TNF-α inhibitors (infliximab, adalimumab, etanercept) are added for inadequate response to MTX; other biologics targeting T- and B-cell responses are used in refractory cases.
  • Opportunistic Infection Risk: Anti-TNF therapy increases opportunistic infections, particularly reactivation of tuberculosis.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian) "Remember two key rules for RA therapy: Methotrexate is the first-line DMARD, and TNF-α inhibitors increase the risk of tuberculosis reactivation."
Second-Order Board Clue A patient with erosive RA refractory to Methotrexate is scheduled to start infliximab or etanercept; prior to initiation, screening is essential to prevent reactivation of tuberculosis.

Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (JIA)

Lecturer Rule: Age <16 yrs, ≥6 weeks, large joints (knees), ANA+, NO nodules, chronic anterior uveitis

JIA Pediatric Diagnostic & Systemic Matrix

Explore the diagnostic age/duration gates, adult RA contrasts, and ocular complication mechanisms in childhood arthritis.

LO 3.01 • Criteria & Phenotype

Pediatric Criteria & Joint/Systemic Profile

Age & Duration Gate Before age 16; ≥ 6 weeks
Joint Pattern Oligoarthritis; large joints (knees)
Systemic Features Fever, rash, lymphadenopathy
Susceptibility Genes HLA & PTPN22 variants

Diagnostic Thresholds, Shared Genetics & Pediatric Presentation

LO 3.01 • Slide 22
Definition & Pediatric Contrast
  • Definition: Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (formerly Juvenile RA) is a heterogeneous group of disorders of unknown cause with arthritis beginning before age 16 years and lasting ≥ 6 weeks; it is the most common chronic rheumatic disease of childhood.
  • Clinical Pattern vs. Adult RA: More commonly presents with oligoarthritis, large-joint involvement (especially knees), and prominent systemic manifestations (fever, rash, lymphadenopathy).
Immunopathogenesis
  • Genetic Susceptibility: Similar to adult RA, involving HLA and PTPN22 susceptibility variants.
  • Cytokine Axis: Driven by Th1/Th17 activation with elevated IL-1, IL-17, and TNF-α.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian) "JIA is NOT simply adult RA in a child: compared to adult RA, JIA favors oligoarthritis, large joints like the knees, and systemic signs like fever, rash, and lymphadenopathy."
Second-Order Board Clue A 9-year-old girl with a swollen left knee for 8 weeks, spiking fever, and lymphadenopathy fits JIA because onset occurred before age 16 years for ≥ 6 weeks, mediated by Th1/Th17 activation and IL-1/IL-17/TNF-α.
LO 3.02 • Labs & Complication

ANA Positivity, Absent Nodules & Anterior Uveitis

Serology Profile ANA positivity common
Key Negative Clue Rheumatoid nodules usually absent
Ocular Complication Chronic anterior uveitis
Predominant Joint Knees (large joints)

Serologic Distinctions & Ocular Screening Imperative

LO 3.02 • Slide 22
Serologic & Exam Discriminators
  • Autoantibody Profile: Antinuclear antibodies (ANA) positivity is common in JIA rather than adult-type RF/anti-CCP dominance.
  • Absence of Subcutaneous Nodules: Unlike seropositive adult RA, rheumatoid nodules are usually absent in JIA.
Critical Complication
  • Chronic Anterior Uveitis: A major extra-articular complication of JIA is chronic anterior uveitis, making ophthalmologic monitoring critical to prevent vision loss.
  • Clinical Contrast with SpA: Note that JIA causes chronic anterior uveitis, whereas HLA-B27 spondyloarthropathies characteristically cause acute anterior uveitis.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian) "In JIA, ANA positivity is common, rheumatoid nodules are usually absent, and the classic complication you must not miss is chronic anterior uveitis."
Second-Order Board Clue A 7-year-old child with persistent knee oligoarthritis and positive ANA without subcutaneous nodules requires regular eye evaluation to detect asymptomatic chronic anterior uveitis.

Seronegative Spondyloarthropathies (SpA)

Lecturer Rule: Think HLA-B27 whenever you see sacroiliitis, enthesitis, or acute anterior uveitis

Seronegative Spondyloarthropathy Clinical & Radiographic Discriminator Workbench

Compare the four HLA-B27-associated disorders across target joints, radiographs, extra-articular clues, and triggers.

LO 4.01 • Shared Features

Shared Spondyloarthropathy Pathophysiology

Genetic Driver HLA-B27 association
Key Negative Clue Seronegative for RF
Anatomic Target Sacroiliac joints + enthesitis
Ocular Hallmark Acute anterior uveitis

Unifying Features Across All Four SpA Entities

LO 4.01 • Slide 23
Five Core Shared Hallmarks
  • Four Family Members: Ankylosing Spondylitis, Reactive Arthritis, Psoriatic Arthritis, and Enteropathic (IBD-Associated) Arthritis.
  • Shared Signature: All are seronegative for rheumatoid factor (RF−), strongly associated with HLA-B27, and feature axial skeleton involvement (especially sacroiliac joints), enthesitis (inflammation at tendon and ligament insertions), and acute anterior uveitis.
Immune Mechanism
  • T-Cell Trigger: Pathogenesis likely involves T-cell-mediated immune responses triggered by environmental or microbial antigens in genetically susceptible (HLA-B27) individuals.
  • Site Specificity: Unlike RA (which targets synovial lining of small peripheral joints), SpA characteristically targets tendon/ligament bony insertions (enthesitis) and the axial sacroiliac joints.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian) "Think HLA-B27 whenever you see sacroiliitis, enthesitis, or acute anterior uveitis in a patient who is negative for rheumatoid factor."
Second-Order Board Clue Achilles tendon insertion pain (enthesitis) paired with unilateral red, painful eye (acute anterior uveitis) and negative RF points directly to the HLA-B27 seronegative spondyloarthropathy spectrum.
LO 4.02 • Axial SpA

Ankylosing Spondylitis & Bamboo Spine

HLA-B27 Rate Approximately 90% positive
Clinical Hallmark Inflammatory low back pain
Earliest X-Ray Bilateral symmetric sacroiliitis
Late Spine X-Ray Bamboo spine
Cardiac Complication Aortic regurgitation

Bilateral Sacroiliitis, Spinal Fusion & Systemic Associations

LO 4.02 • Slides 24–25
Clinical Presentation & Extra-Articular
  • Target Joints & Progression: Chronic inflammatory arthritis of the sacroiliac joints and vertebral apophyseal joints causing enthesitis, progressive bony ankylosis, and loss of spinal mobility.
  • Typical Patient: Onset in the 2nd–3rd decades (young adulthood) with inflammatory low back pain and morning stiffness improving with activity; ~90% are HLA-B27 positive.
  • Associated Findings: Acute anterior uveitis and aortic regurgitation.
Radiographic Evolution
  • Earliest & Hallmark Finding: Bilateral, symmetric sacroiliitis characterized by bilateral symmetric sacroiliac joint erosions, iliac-side subchondral sclerosis, progressive joint-space narrowing, and eventual ankylosis.
  • Late Spinal Appearance: Bamboo spine—the vertebral column appears rigid and straight like a bamboo stalk on X-ray.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian) "Young patient + inflammatory back pain improving with activity + bilateral symmetric sacroiliitis + HLA-B27 (~90%) = Ankylosing Spondylitis. Bilateral symmetric sacroiliitis is the earliest hallmark radiographic finding."
Second-Order Board Clue A 25-year-old man with morning low back stiffness relieved by exercise, a diastolic decrescendo murmur (aortic regurgitation), and iliac-side subchondral sclerosis on pelvic X-ray will progress to bamboo spine.
LO 4.03 • Post-Infectious SpA

Reactive Arthritis & Post-Infectious Triad

Trigger Pathogens Chlamydia / GI enterics
Classic Triad Uveitis + urethritis + arthritis
Skin Hallmark Keratoderma blennorrhagicum
HLA-B27 & Joint Fluid ~80% HLA-B27; sterile joint

Sterile Post-Infectious Arthritis & Mucocutaneous Clues

LO 4.03 • Slides 26–27
Triggers & Classic Triad
  • Definition & Timing: Acute sterile seronegative spondyloarthropathy developing 1–4 weeks after a preceding genitourinary or gastrointestinal infection (~80% HLA-B27 positive).
  • Common Triggers: Chlamydia trachomatis (GU) and enteric pathogens (Salmonella, Shigella, Yersinia, Campylobacter).
  • Triad Presentation: Asymmetric oligoarthritis (knees, ankles, feet), urethritis/cervicitis, and conjunctivitis or uveitis.
Mucocutaneous Findings & Therapy
  • Skin & Mucosal Signs: Keratoderma blennorrhagicum (characteristic hyperkeratotic skin lesion), oral mucosal ulcers, and enthesitis.
  • Mnemonic: "Can't see, can't pee, can't climb a tree" (Conjunctivitis/Uveitis → Urethritis → Arthritis).
  • Treatment: NSAIDs first-line; persistent disease may require DMARDs or biologic therapy.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian) "Reactive arthritis (formerly Reiter syndrome) is an acute STERILE arthritis following Chlamydia or enteric GI infection—meaning synovial fluid cultures are negative."
Second-Order Board Clue A 26-year-old presents 2 weeks after infectious diarrhea (Shigella or Campylobacter) with an acutely swollen knee, conjunctivitis, dysuria, and waxy plantar papules (keratoderma blennorrhagicum); joint aspiration will yield sterile synovial fluid.
LO 4.04 • Psoriasis-Linked SpA

Psoriatic Arthritis & Pencil-in-Cup Deformity

Predominant Hand Joint DIP joint involvement
Digit Swelling Dactylitis (sausage digits)
Radiographic Sign Pencil-in-cup deformity
Key Negative Clue No rheumatoid nodules
Targeted Biologics TNF & IL-17 inhibitors

Cutaneous Psoriasis, Sausage Digits & Eroded Phalanges

LO 4.04 • Slide 28
Clinical & Radiographic Hallmarks
  • Cutaneous Association: Chronic inflammatory arthritis usually following psoriasis of the skin or nails; notably has no rheumatoid nodules.
  • Classic Digit Findings: DIP joint involvement (unlike RA), dactylitis ("sausage digits"), enthesitis, and axial/often asymmetric sacroiliitis (HLA-B27 association is strongest in axial disease).
  • Distinctive X-Ray: Pencil-in-cup deformity resulting from simultaneous bone erosion and new bone formation.
Treatment Options
  • Initial & DMARD Therapy: NSAIDs and methotrexate.
  • Biologic Therapy: TNF inhibitors and IL-17 inhibitors.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian) "Four keywords for Psoriatic Arthritis: Psoriasis (skin/nails), DIP involvement, Dactylitis ('sausage digits'), and Pencil-in-Cup deformity on X-ray."
Second-Order Board Clue A patient with nail pitting and silvery extensor plaques develops diffuse swelling of an entire toe (dactylitis) and DIP pain; hand radiography shows central phalangeal tapering into a flared base, termed pencil-in-cup deformity.
LO 4.05 • GI-Linked SpA

Enteropathic (IBD-Associated) Arthritis

Associated GI Disease Crohn disease & UC (~20%)
Peripheral Pattern Knees and ankles + enthesitis
Axial Pattern Sacroiliitis & spondylitis
HLA-B27 Link Primarily in axial disease
Management Caution Control IBD; NSAIDs with caution

Peripheral vs. Axial Manifestations in Crohn Disease & Ulcerative Colitis

LO 4.05 • Slide 29
Two Major Clinical Patterns
  • Epidemiology & GI Context: Occurs in approximately 20% of patients with inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn disease and ulcerative colitis), alongside abdominal pain, diarrhea, and weight loss.
  • Peripheral Manifestations: Peripheral arthritis (usually knees and ankles), enthesitis, and dactylitis.
  • Axial Manifestations: Sacroiliitis and spondylitis (resembling AS), where HLA-B27 is primarily associated.
Therapeutic Strategy
  • Primary Anchor: Control of underlying IBD is foundational, especially for peripheral joint flares.
  • Medication Rules: Use NSAIDs with caution (due to underlying bowel inflammation), and utilize anti-TNF therapy to address both bowel and joint/axial disease.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gasparian) "IBD + Peripheral Arthritis ± Sacroiliitis/Spondylitis = Enteropathic Arthritis. Remember that HLA-B27 is primarily tied to the axial disease component, and NSAIDs must be used with caution."
Second-Order Board Clue A patient with chronic bloody diarrhea (ulcerative colitis) develops acute knee swelling and Achilles enthesitis; rather than high-dose NSAIDs, definitive management prioritizes control of underlying IBD or anti-TNF therapy.

Enteropathic (IBD) Arthritis
Seronegative SpA 		RF−; ~20% of Crohn disease & ulcerative colitis; HLA-B27 associated primarily with axial pattern. Peripheral (knees/ankles, enthesitis, dactylitis) ± axial (sacroiliitis, spondylitis) + abdominal pain, diarrhea, weight loss. Diagnostics: IBD correlation ± axial sacroiliitis. Tx: control underlying IBD, NSAIDs with caution, anti-TNF therapy. IBD + Peripheral Arthritis ± Sacroiliitis/Spondylitis; use NSAIDs with caution in bowel inflammation. In IBD-associated arthritis, HLA-B27 predicts axial sacroiliitis/spondylitis rather than peripheral flares.
