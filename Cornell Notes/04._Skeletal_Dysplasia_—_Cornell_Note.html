How Skeletal Dysplasias Present Across Age & Radiographic Principles

LO 1.01 • Lecture 04a Slides 3–7
Meaning & Clinical Spectrum
  • Abnormal Skeleton: Skeletal dysplasia refers to a malformed skeleton, meaning abnormal growth or disorganization of the skeleton. Hundreds of genetic causes exist, whereas rare phenocopies result from maternal medications such as thalidomide.
  • Prenatal Detection: Presents in pregnancy with an abnormal 20-week anatomy ultrasound or decreased growth in the 3rd trimester labeled as intra-uterine growth restriction, which must be separated from true intra-uterine growth restriction caused by insufficient placental nutrients.
  • Delivery Room & Childhood: Neonates may present with respiratory distress or as a small for dates infant whose growth parameters fail to match known pregnancy dates; children present with fractures or short stature.
  • Adult Complications: Adults develop mechanical sequelae from abnormal bone architecture, such as a small foramen magnum in achondroplasia or cranial nerve foramina impingement in osteopetrosis.
Radiographic Interpretation Rules
  • Cartilage Transparency: Because cartilage is transparent on routine X-rays, the apparent skeletal outline changes dramatically with developmental maturity.
  • Fetal vs. Child vs. Adult: In a fetus, the entire epiphysis and the anterior ribs are still cartilage; children have open growth plates, while adults have closed growth plates.
  • Thorax-to-Abdomen Ratio: In lethal skeletal dysplasia, the small thorax appears disproportionately narrow relative to the abdomen in a fetus or neonate; clinically, thoracic circumference is measured and compared to normal.
  • Molecular Confirmation: X-rays are a major component of assessment, and most skeletal dysplasia diagnoses are confirmed with genetic sequencing.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Remember that in this context, dysplasia means abnormal growth or disorganization of the skeleton—not pre-neoplastic cellular atypia. When reading fetal or neonatal X-rays, always recall that the unossified epiphyses and anterior ribs are invisible cartilage.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A fetus flagged at the 20-week anatomy ultrasound with shortened long bones and a small thorax relative to the abdomen represents skeletal dysplasia rather than true intra-uterine growth restriction from placental insufficiency.

LO 1.02 • Genetics & OB Terms

Gain vs. Loss of Function & Obstetric GP(TPAL) Terminology

Gain of Function Rule Usually autosomal dominant
Loss of Function Rule Usually autosomal recessive
Gravida & Para Total pregnancies vs. live births
Developmental Stages Fetus, neonate, and infant

Molecular Inheritance Logic & Clinical Vignette Vocabulary

LO 1.02 • Lecture 04a Slides 14–17
Gain vs. Loss of Function Mutations
  • Gain-of-Function Mechanism: A DNA mutation causes the corresponding protein to acquire an abnormal function that interferes with the normal allele gene product, making it usually autosomal dominant (as in FGFR3 disorders).
  • Loss-of-Function Mechanism: Typically causes loss of enzymatic activity so the remaining normal gene product can compensate, making these conditions generally autosomal recessive (as in lysosomal storage disorders and Carbonic Anhydrase 2 deficiency).
  • Dominant Negative Contrast: In Osteogenesis Imperfecta, a mutated collagen chain interferes with assembly of normal chains into the triple helix—a dominant negative effect.
Obstetric GP(TPAL) & Pediatric Ages
  • Gravida (G) & Para (P): Gravida counts total pregnancies regardless of outcome (live births, miscarriages, abortions, stillbirths), whereas Para counts total live births (e.g., G3P2 = three gestations, two live births).
  • TPAL Breakdown: Lists Term births, Premature births, Abortions before 20 weeks (spontaneous or therapeutic), and Living children.
  • Age Definitions: An embryo is before full development; a fetus is before birth (almost completely developed, growing in size); a neonate is 0–1 month after delivery; an infant is 0–12 months after delivery.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Every skeletal dysplasia clinical vignette uses obstetric shorthand like G1P0 at 18 or 38 weeks. Know immediately that a fetus is prenatal, a neonate is 0–1 month old, and enzyme deficiencies are typically autosomal recessive while gain-of-function receptor mutations are autosomal dominant.”

Second-Order Board Clue

When a single mutant allele encodes an overactive receptor or structural chain that poisons the wild-type product, inheritance is autosomal dominant, whereas loss of a soluble enzyme like Carbonic Anhydrase 2 is autosomal recessive.

LO 1.03 • FGFR3 Non-Lethal

Achondroplasia: FGFR3 Activation & Clinical Phenotype

Molecular Driver FGFR3 gain-of-function (paternal allele)
Growth Plate Defect No lysosomal degradation; always ON
Classic Limb & Hand Proximal short bones & trident hands
Craniofacial Hallmark Macrocephaly, bulging forehead, depressed nose
Life-Threatening Risk Narrow foramen magnum (brainstem)

Endochondral Ossification Arrest & Systemic Features of Achondroplasia

LO 1.03 • Lecture 04a Slides 8–13
FGFR3 Receptor & Growth Plate Mechanism
  • Epidemiology & Germline Origin: Most common skeletal dysplasia (height well below 3rd percentile), inherited as autosomal dominant; most cases are de novo mutations on the paternal allele linked to older fathers and germline stem cell competition.
  • Constitutive Signaling: Mutation in the membrane portion prevents normal processing and lysosomal degradation of FGFR3, keeping the receptor constitutively active ("always ON") rather than turning on and off.
  • Endochondral Arrest: Continuous signaling forces growth plate chondroblasts to differentiate prematurely into chondrocytes; because chondroblasts fail to proliferate, long bones growing by endochondral ossification become short.
Clinical & Craniospinal Hallmarks
  • Disproportionate Skeleton: Short long bones (more severe proximally) paired with a trunk of relatively normal length and lumbar lordosis.
  • Trident Hands: Fingers are short, of equal length, and spread apart like a three-pronged fish spear (trident hands).
  • Skull & Brain: Macrocephaly (both brain and skull enlarged due to signaling abnormality elsewhere), enlarged head with bulging forehead, and depressed root of the nose.
  • Foramen Magnum Compression: A narrow foramen magnum may compress the brainstem and cause hydrocephalus or central sleep apnea.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Normally, FGF binds FGFR3 and the receptor is degraded in the lysosome so the signal turns OFF and chondroblasts can proliferate. In achondroplasia, mutated FGFR3 is not degraded—the signal stays always ON, halting proliferation at the growth plate.”

Second-Order Board Clue

An infant with short proximal long bones, trident hands, and macrocephaly develops central sleep apnea or hydrocephalus secondary to brainstem compression at a narrow foramen magnum.

Lethal FGFR3 Dysplasia, Fetal Breathing & Lung Development

LO 1.04 • Lecture 04a Slides 18–21
Severe Phenotype & Radiology
  • Distinct FGFR3 Mutation: Caused by a different FGFR3 gain-of-function mutation than achondroplasia, producing a much more severe, perinatal lethal skeletal phenotype.
  • Telephone Receiver Femur: Radiographs show severely shortened, thick, and curved femurs resembling an old telephone receiver, paired with a relatively large head and bell-shaped abdomen.
  • Delivery Room Vignette: A neonate delivered at 38 weeks by C-section develops immediate respiratory distress and dies after 45 minutes of resuscitation; X-ray confirms a tiny thorax and telephone receiver femurs.
Fetal Breathing & Pulmonary Hypoplasia
  • Why Small Thorax Causes Tiny Lungs: During pregnancy, fetuses practice breathing amniotic fluid in and out (fetal breathing), which is essential for normal lung growth.
  • Pulmonary Hypoplasia: A tiny thorax prevents adequate fetal breathing movements, leaving the lungs severely underdeveloped (pulmonary hypoplasia) while the heart remains normal in size; postnatally the lungs cannot grow fast enough to sustain life.
  • Key Differential Mechanism: Lethal skeletal dysplasia is one cause of pulmonary hypoplasia; bilateral renal agenesis with resulting anhydramnios is another classic cause.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Thanatophoric dysplasia has a thorax too small to support life because fetal breathing of amniotic fluid is required for lung growth. On autopsy, compare the tiny lungs inside the tiny thorax against a heart of normal size.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A neonate with a telephone receiver femur and tiny thorax dies shortly after birth from pulmonary hypoplasia—sharing the same fatal fetal-breathing mechanism as bilateral renal agenesis with anhydramnios.

LO 2.01 • Collagen Mechanism

Osteogenesis Imperfecta Overview & 4 Clinical Subtypes

Biochemical Defect Deficiency of Type I collagen synthesis
Molecular Interference Dominant negative effect on triple helix
Extra-Skeletal Triad Blue sclerae, hearing loss, abnormal teeth
Subtype Sclera Discriminator OI Type 4 has normal sclerae

Triple Helix Disruption & Subtype Spectrum (Over 800 Mutations)

LO 2.01 • Lecture 04a Slides 26–27
Dominant Negative Collagen Pathogenesis
  • Type I Collagen Deficiency: Over 800 mutations cause defective synthesis of Type I collagen, leading to abnormal bone mineralization so bone becomes fragile and fractures easily.
  • Dominant Negative Effect: In autosomal dominant OI, the mutated collagen chain physically interferes with the normal gene product during assembly of collagen triple helix chains.
  • Extra-Skeletal Manifestations: Because Type I collagen is vital outside bone, patients develop blue sclerae, hearing impairment, and dentinogenesis imperfecta (abnormal teeth).
Four Major Clinical Subtypes
  • OI Type 1: Childhood fractures that decrease after puberty, blue sclerae, hearing impairment, and dentinogenesis imperfecta.
  • OI Type 2: Perinatal lethal phenotype with extreme skeletal fragility and crumpled intra-uterine fractures.
  • OI Type 3: Multiple fractures (severe surviving form), blue sclerae, hearing impairment, and dentinogenesis imperfecta.
  • OI Type 4: Moderate skeletal fragility, short stature, sometimes dentinogenesis imperfecta, and crucially normal sclerae.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Clinical interpretation of childhood fractures is complex—you must keep Osteogenesis Imperfecta in your differential diagnosis. Many pediatric hospitals have dedicated Care Teams that investigate childhood fractures.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Unlike enzyme deficiencies where 50% activity suffices, a structural Type I collagen mutation exerts a dominant negative effect by poisoning collagen triple helix assembly.

LO 2.02 • Perinatal Lethal

Osteogenesis Imperfecta Type 2: Crumpled Bones & Procollagen Failure

Inheritance Pattern Most autosomal recessive; some parental mosaicism
Molecular Example Glycine to Alanine blocks triple helix
Radiographic Hallmark Crumpled long bones & crumpled ribs
Cause of Death Tiny thorax → respiratory insufficiency

OI Type 2: Intra-Uterine Fractures & Lethal Thoracic Collapse

LO 2.02 • Lecture 04a Slides 23–29
Genetics & Procollagen Assembly Defect
  • Genetics: Most OI Type 2 cases are autosomal recessive, while some are autosomal dominant arising from parental mosaicism with a mutation in the gonads.
  • Glycine Substitution Model: Simplified teaching example shows a Glycine to Alanine substitution in procollagen that prevents triple helix formation.
  • Osteoblast Failure: Because no intact triple helix forms, osteoblasts do not deposit Type I collagen, leaving severely insufficient bone tissue.
Radiographs & Vignette Correlation
  • Intra-Uterine Fractures: Extreme skeletal fragility causes fractures in utero; long bones (femur, humerus) appear short and crumpled on X-ray.
  • Rib Fractures & Lethality: Individual ribs also appear crumpled from intra-uterine fractures, collapsing the chest into a tiny thorax; respiratory insufficiency is the cause of death (same final mechanism as thanatophoric dysplasia).
  • Clinical Vignette (04a): A 26-year-old G1P0 woman delivers prematurely at 21 weeks (non-surviving infant); close-up X-rays show crumpled left ribs and a crumpled left femur.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Compare the femurs of our two perinatal lethal dysplasias: Thanatophoric dysplasia has a smooth, thick, curved telephone-receiver femur, whereas OI Type 2 has short, crumpled long bones and crumpled ribs caused by multiple intra-uterine fractures.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Recurrence of perinatal lethal OI Type 2 in phenotypically normal parents with an autosomal dominant mutation is explained by parental mosaicism in the gonads.

LO 2.03 • Pediatric Survival

Osteogenesis Imperfecta Type 1: Childhood Fractures & Osteopenia

Genetics & Prognosis Autosomal dominant; normal life span
Fracture Timeline Childhood fractures decrease after puberty
Radiographic Bone Severe osteopenia (faint/translucent bone)
Exam Triad Blue sclerae, hearing loss, gray-brown teeth

Non-Lethal Quantitative Collagen Deficiency in Children & Adults

LO 2.03 • Lecture 04a Slides 31–33
Clinical Profile & Natural History
  • Quantitative Collagen Deficit: Autosomal dominant disorder with decreased amount of Type I collagen, allowing a normal life span.
  • Pubertal Improvement: Patients sustain recurrent childhood fractures (often from minor playground trauma) that decrease after puberty.
  • Classic Vignette (04a): A 5-year-old boy presents after a right leg fracture on the school playground; he has a history of hearing loss, blue sclerae, and abnormal dentition, with a right femur fracture on X-ray.
Radiology & Physical Confirmation
  • Severe Osteopenia: On X-ray, bones appear faint and more translucent compared to normal due to severe osteopenia; adult X-rays show persistent osteopenia and prior surgical stabilization hardware.
  • Dental & Ocular Signs: Physical examination reveals blue sclerae and dentinogenesis imperfecta presenting as abnormal gray-brown primary teeth.
  • Diagnostic Workup: Clinical diagnosis combining fracture history, X-ray osteopenia, and physical signs is confirmed with DNA studies.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“OI Type 1 students learn to integrate the clinical vignette and physical exam images—hearing loss, blue sclera, and gray-brown teeth—with faint osteopenic bone on X-ray, then confirm with genetic studies.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A school-aged child with a playground long-bone fracture, translucent severe osteopenia on X-ray, and blue sclerae has OI Type 1, whose fracture frequency characteristically decreases after puberty.

Osteoclast Resorption Defects & Limb Aplasia: Osteopetrosis, Radial Aplasia & Thalidomide

Lecturer Rule: Too much sclerotic bone—but abnormally remodeled and fragile

Osteoclast Remodeling Pit, Erlenmeyer Flask & Limb Aplasia Workbench

Toggle between normal bone remodeling, Osteopetrosis (CA2 deficiency), Radial Aplasia, and Thalidomide Embryopathy.

LO 3.01 • Osteoclast Defect

Osteopetrosis: Diffuse Skeletal Sclerosis & Paradoxical Fragility

Cellular Defect Osteoclast defect (poor remodeling)
Primary Pit Failure Failed acidification of resorption pit
Distal Femur Sign Erlenmeyer flask deformity
Diaphyseal X-Ray No cortex-medulla distinction
Mechanical Paradox Sclerotic bone fractures easily

Osteoblast–Osteoclast Imbalance & Radiographic Architecture

LO 3.01 • Lecture 04a Slides 35–38
Remodeling Balance & Resorption Pit Failure
  • Coupled Bone Cells: Normal bone formation requires osteoblasts (which deposit Type I collagen to form bone) and osteoclasts (which break down bone to remodel it and regulate calcium) to be active simultaneously and tightly balanced.
  • Acidification Defect: Osteoclast regulation is complex; most mutations causing osteopetrosis interfere with acidification of the osteoclast resorption pit.
  • Paradoxical Fragility: Without osteoclast remodeling, there is too much bone (diffuse skeletal sclerosis), yet the bone is abnormally formed, weak, and fractures easily.
Radiographic Hallmarks (Femur)
  • Erlenmeyer Flask Deformity: Failure of metaphyseal remodeling produces broad flaring of the distal femur known as the Erlenmeyer flask deformity.
  • Loss of Medullary Cavity: The diaphysis is diffusely sclerotic with poor demarcation—no distinct boundary—between cortex and medulla (unlike a normal pediatric femur).
  • Clinical Scenario (04a): A 2-year-old boy evaluated for multiple fractures shows an unfractured left femur with an Erlenmeyer flask deformity and absent cortex–medulla distinction.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Contrast OI and Osteopetrosis on X-ray: OI has faint, translucent osteopenic bone, whereas Osteopetrosis has dense, white sclerotic bone with loss of the medullary cavity and an Erlenmeyer flask distal femur—yet both present with multiple fractures.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A child with multiple fractures and dense femoral sclerosis lacking cortex-medulla distinction has impaired acidification of the osteoclast resorption pit rather than a collagen synthesis defect.

LO 3.02 • CA2 & Complications

Carbonic Anhydrase 2 Deficiency & Systemic Complications

Exemplar Gene Defect Homozygous Carbonic Anhydrase 2 mutation
Inheritance Autosomal recessive (enzyme LoF)
Renal Complication Renal tubular acidosis
Neurologic Sequela Cranial nerve defects (foramina)
Hematologic Sequela Infections from leukopenia

Multi-Organ Carbonic Anhydrase 2 Deficiency & Survival Complications

LO 3.02 • Lecture 04a Slides 37–40 & 04c Q7
Carbonic Anhydrase 2 (CA2) Syndrome
  • Autosomal Recessive Loss of Function: While many genes can cause osteopetrosis, a classic example is a homozygous mutation in the Carbonic Anhydrase 2 gene causing enzyme loss of function.
  • Bone Effect: Osteoclasts cannot generate protons to acidify the pit, so they fail to remodel bone correctly, producing fragile sclerotic bone.
  • Kidney Effect: Loss of Carbonic Anhydrase 2 in renal tubules simultaneously causes renal tubular acidosis.
Marrow Obliteration & Foramina Impingement
  • Infantile Mortality: Severe forms of osteopetrosis may result in death during infancy.
  • Cranial Nerve Impingement: Survivors develop cranial nerve defects as unremodeled sclerotic bone encroaches into cranial nerve foramina.
  • Leukopenia & Infections: Progressive sclerosis decreases marrow space, preventing normal hematopoiesis and causing leukopenia and recurrent infections.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Carbonic Anhydrase 2 deficiency is our clean prototype for how one enzyme loss-of-function mutation causes both osteopetrosis in bone and renal tubular acidosis in the kidneys.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A surviving child with sclerotic femurs, recurrent bacterial infections from leukopenia, cranial nerve defects, and renal tubular acidosis has an autosomal recessive Carbonic Anhydrase 2 mutation.

LO 3.03 • Aplasia & Teratogen

Radial Aplasia & Thalidomide Embryopathy

Radial Aplasia Anatomy Absent radius & absent thumb
Radial Aplasia Etiology Unilateral/bilateral; sporadic or syndromic
Thalidomide Class Maternal medication phenocopy
Historical Exposure Late 1950s for morning sickness

Focal Bone Aplasia & Teratogenic Limb Phenotypes

LO 3.03 • Lecture 04a Slides 42–43
Radial Aplasia (Bone Aplasia Example)
  • Defining Anatomy: Presented as the prototype of aplasia of a bone, characterized by an absent radius paired with an absent thumb.
  • Laterality & Causes: May occur unilaterally or bilaterally, and may be sporadic or part of a genetic syndrome.
  • Radiographic View: Neonatal and fetal radiographs demonstrate absence of the radial forearm bone and first digit ray, causing radial deviation of the hand.
Thalidomide Embryopathy
  • Non-Genetic Phenocopy: Illustrates how a skeletal dysplasia limb phenotype can rarely result from maternal medications rather than an inherited mutation.
  • Historical Context: Prescribed in the late 1950s to treat morning sickness (largely in Canada and other countries outside the United States).
  • Limb Phenotype: Causes severe limb reduction malformations (shortened or absent limb segments) in exposed embryos during early development.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“While hundreds of skeletal dysplasias are genetic, never forget that maternal medication exposure—classically thalidomide used for morning sickness in the late 1950s—can produce a dramatic limb malformation phenotype.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Radiographs showing an absent radius and absent thumb represent radial aplasia (either sporadic or syndromic), whereas bilateral severe limb reduction after 1950s morning sickness therapy points to thalidomide.

Keep OI in differential for childhood fractures; pediatric hospitals use Care Teams to evaluate fractures. Playground fracture + faint osteopenic tibia + blue sclerae + dentinogenesis imperfecta (note: Type 4 has normal sclerae).
Osteogenesis Imperfecta Type 2 Collagen I Lethal Most autosomal recessive (some AD via parental mosaicism); e.g., Glycine to Alanine blocks triple helix → osteoblasts do not deposit Type I collagen. Extreme fragility with intra-uterine fractures; very small thorax; perinatal death from respiratory insufficiency. Fetal/neonatal X-rays show short crumpled long bones (femur, humerus) and crumpled ribs; thoracic measurements. Shares lethal respiratory insufficiency from tiny thorax with thanatophoric dysplasia, but bones are crumpled rather than smoothly curved. Recurrent perinatal lethal OI Type 2 in unaffected parents with AD mutation points to gonadal parental mosaicism.
Osteopetrosis Osteoclast Defect Osteoclast dysfunction (most impair acidification of resorption pit); e.g., autosomal recessive Carbonic Anhydrase 2 loss of function. Multiple fractures despite dense bone; severe forms die in infancy; survivors get cranial nerve defects, infections (leukopenia), and renal tubular acidosis. Femur X-ray shows diffuse sclerosis, Erlenmeyer flask deformity, and poor cortex-medulla demarcation; genetic confirmation. Too much bone on X-ray, yet abnormally remodeled and weak; contrast against translucent osteopenia in OI. Carbonic Anhydrase 2 deficiency links dense fragile bones with renal tubular acidosis and marrow obliteration.
Radial Aplasia & Thalidomide Aplasia / Teratogen Focal bone aplasia (sporadic or genetic syndrome) vs. rare non-genetic phenocopy from maternal thalidomide medication exposure. Radial aplasia: absent radius and absent thumb (unilateral or bilateral); Thalidomide: severe limb reduction phenotype. Prenatal ultrasound and limb radiographs; careful maternal medication history (late 1950s morning sickness drug). Rarely, a skeletal dysplasia phenotype is non-genetic and caused by a maternal medication rather than a gene mutation. 1950s Canadian/international morning sickness drug exposure causing limb defects = thalidomide embryopathy.
