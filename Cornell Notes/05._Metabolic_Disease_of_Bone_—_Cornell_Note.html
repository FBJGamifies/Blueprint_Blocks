Radiologic Differentiation of Compact vs. Trabecular Bone & Diagnostic Strategy

LO 1.01 • Lecture Slides & Case 1 Q1
Adult vs. Child Femur Anatomy (Case 1 Q1)
  • Three Structural Regions: Every long bone such as the femur is divided into the proximal and distal epiphysis, the flared transitional metaphysis, and the central cylindrical shaft or diaphysis.
  • Two Radiologic Bone Types: On plain X-rays, compact bone forms the dense, thick, radiopaque outer cortex along the shaft, whereas trabecular bone appears as an interconnected latticework of spongy struts concentrated within the metaphysis and epiphysis.
  • Adult vs. Pediatric Discriminator: Comparing adult femur radiographs to a child femur radiograph demonstrates fusion and closure of the epiphyseal growth plate in adults, whereas children retain a distinct radiolucent cartilaginous growth plate (physis) separating the epiphysis from the metaphysis.
Clinical Workup & Biopsy Protocol
  • Non-Invasive Diagnostic Pillars: Metabolic bone disorders are evaluated clinically with radiology (X-rays) and laboratory medicine panels measuring calcium, phosphate, vitamin D, and parathyroid hormone levels.
  • Why Distinguish Entities: Separating entities such as osteoporosis, osteomalacia, hyperparathyroidism, and Paget disease is essential because each requires different targeted therapies.
  • Bone Biopsy Rule: Bone biopsy is rarely performed in metabolic bone disease (microscopic images are largely for educational understanding), with mass lesions—specifically brown tumor and Paget disease—serving as the major exceptions usually biopsied.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Bone biopsy is rarely done in metabolic bone disease, and images today are mostly for learning purposes—the key exceptions are mass lesions such as brown tumor and Paget disease, which are usually biopsied.”

Second-Order Board Clue

When a vignette asks for the initial workup of suspected diffuse metabolic bone loss without a focal mass, choose radiographs and serum Ca, PO₄, Vitamin D, and PTH rather than an invasive bone biopsy.

Quantitative Bone Mineral Density Thresholds & Fragility Hip Fracture Vignette

LO 1.02 • Robbins Ch 26 & Dr. Tano Vignette
DEXA Scan Quantification
  • Osteopenia Threshold: Represents decreased bone mass measured at 1 to 2.5 standard deviations below peak bone mass in young adults on a DEXA scan.
  • Osteoporosis Threshold: Represents decreased bone mass severe enough to increase fracture risk, defined as 2.5 standard deviations or more below peak bone mass in young adults.
  • DEXA Technique & Sites: Dual Energy X-ray Absorptiometry is a radiologic technique used to evaluate bone density, generally performed on the femur and vertebrae.
Clinical Scenario: Elderly Steroid-Induced Hip Fracture
  • Patient Presentation: An 83-year-old woman with autoimmune lung disease on chronic steroids (BMI 18 kg/m², thin and frail) trips over a rug and lands on her left hip, developing acute hip pain radiating to the left groin and complete inability to bear weight.
  • Classic Physical Exam: Inspection reveals the left leg is shortened and externally rotated with an 8 cm lateral hip ecchymosis.
  • Clinical Diagnosis: Hip fracture due to osteoporosis, illustrating how minimal trauma produces devastating fractures when bone mass drops below the fracture threshold.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Osteopenia is simply decreased bone mass, whereas osteoporosis is decreased bone mass severe enough to increase the risk of fracture—measured at the femur and vertebrae by DEXA.”

Second-Order Board Clue

An elderly, low-BMI patient on chronic corticosteroids who sustains a shortened, externally rotated leg after a minor ground-level fall points to an osteoporotic femoral neck/hip fracture with a DEXA T-score ≤ -2.5 standard deviations.

Autopsy Rib Histomorphometry & Cellular Balance of Bone Loss

LO 1.03 • Case 2 Q2–Q3, Rosai sem57a-case10 & Robbins Fig 26.8
Case 2 Autopsy Rib Pathology (Q2–Q3)
  • Case 2 Scenario: An 88-year-old female falls in her kitchen, sustains a right hip fracture, and dies of repair complications 1 month later; autopsy rib section (Rosai sem57a-case10) is compared to a normal rib.
  • Diagnostic Rib Appearance: Summarized as “not enough bone”—showing a markedly thin cortex and thin, sparse, disconnected trabecular bone surrounded by abundant marrow space, contrasting with the normal rib’s thick, abundant, connected trabeculae.
  • Antemortem DEXA Correlation (Q3): Had a DEXA scan been performed on her femur and vertebrae while alive, it would show bone mineral density ≥2.5 SD below young adult peak bone mass.
Robbins Fig 26.8 Pathogenesis & Fracture Prevention
  • Age-Related (Senile) Decline: Advanced age and reduced physical activity lower osteoblast replicative and synthetic activity while blunting matrix response to mechanical loading.
  • Postmenopausal Estrogen Loss: Estrogen deficiency elevates inflammatory cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNF), increasing RANKL and M-CSF expression while decreasing osteoprotegerin (OPG), driving heightened osteoclast activity and bone resorption.
  • Prevention & Fracture Risk Reduction: Lecture-cited measures include weight-bearing exercise, appropriate calcium and vitamin D, and pharmacologic agents.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Think of osteoporosis in one phrase: not enough bone—thin cortex and thin, sparse trabeculae—whereas the remaining bone present is normally mineralized.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Postmenopausal bone resorption accelerates because low estrogen upregulates inflammatory cytokines that raise RANKL and M-CSF while suppressing the decoy receptor OPG, tipping remodeling toward osteoclasts.

Epiphyseal Growth Plate Disorganization & Skeletal Deformities in Rickets

LO 2.01 • Robbins Ch 9 & Dr. Tano Vignette
Pathogenesis & 3 Growth Plate Hallmarks
  • Mineralization Failure: Vitamin D normally drives mineralization of osteoid matrix and epiphyseal cartilage; deficiency leads to overgrowth of epiphyseal cartilage, deposition of osteoid onto inadequately mineralized cartilage, and disrupted cartilage-to-osteoid replacement.
  • Microscopic Triad (vs. Orderly Normal Plate): 1) No columnar organization of chondrocytes, 2) a disorganized hypertrophic zone, and 3) irregular demarcation between proliferative and hypertrophic zones.
Clinical Scenario & Gross Skeletal Signs
  • Clinical Vignette (7-Year-Old Girl): Cognitive delay and feeding difficulty (diet limited to chicken nuggets and juice, no vitamins/supplements) → 3 months of bone pain, muscle weakness, difficulty walking, short stature, thickened ankles and wrists, and genu varum.
  • Radiologic & Thoracic Signs: X-ray of a 6-year-old boy demonstrates bowed femurs, tibias, and fibulas, while chest imaging reveals a rachitic rosary—enlarged costochondral junctions along the ribs.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Unlike the very orderly columns of a normal growth plate, Rickets shows no columnar organization of chondrocytes, a disorganized hypertrophic zone, and irregular demarcation between proliferative and hypertrophic zones.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A child with restricted dietary intake, genu varum, thickened wrists/ankles, and beaded ribs (rachitic rosary) has open growth plates failing to mineralize prior to epiphyseal fusion.

Unmineralized Osteoid Accumulation & Post-Bariatric Clinical Presentation

LO 2.02 • Robbins Ch 9, RGB Trichrome & Dr. Tano Vignette
Osteoid Biology & Trichrome Histopathology
  • What Is Osteoid: Extracellular matrix produced by osteoblasts containing collagen type I and other molecules that normally undergoes mineralization and hardens into bone.
  • Adult Pathophysiology (“Soft Bones”): Much less common than osteoporosis, osteomalacia features increased unmineralized osteoid that weakens bone and increases fracture susceptibility.
  • RGB Trichrome Visualization: Osteoid stains pink and mineralized bone stains green; normal bone shows scant pink osteoid and abundant green mineralized bone, whereas osteomalacia shows prominent, thickened pink osteoid seams (histologic appearance varies by stain and decalcification).
Post-Bariatric Vignette, Labs & Treatment
  • Clinical Vignette (70-Year-Old Woman): 1 year after weight-loss (gastric bypass) surgery with intermittent nutritional supplement compliance → 3 months of fatigue and dull aching bone pain over bilateral shoulders, lower back, hips, and thighs with a normal physical exam.
  • Laboratory Findings: Alkaline phosphatase elevated alongside either low calcium or low phosphorus.
  • Diagnostic Imperative: Distinguishing osteomalacia from osteoporosis matters because osteomalacia requires treatment directed specifically at vitamin D and calcium levels.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“On RGB Trichrome stain, osteoid is pink and mineralized bone is green—osteomalacia shows way too much pink unmineralized osteoid, and its treatment is completely different from osteoporosis.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A patient status-post gastric bypass surgery with diffuse axial/proximal bone pain, elevated alkaline phosphatase, and low calcium or phosphorus has osteomalacia rather than primary osteoporosis.

Parathyroid Hormone Physiology & Skeletal Effects of Primary Hyperparathyroidism

LO 3.01 • Robbins Ch 26 & Endocrine Schematic
PTH Mechanisms & Gland Regulation
  • Normal vs. Adenoma Regulation: Normal parathyroid glands sense serum calcium levels and secrete PTH proportionally; a parathyroid adenoma produces autonomous, unregulated PTH while the remaining three normal parathyroid glands sit suppressed.
  • Four Core PTH Effects: PTH 1) activates osteoclasts to increase bone resorption, 2) increases calcium reabsorption by the kidney, 3) increases phosphate excretion by the kidney, and 4) increases Vitamin D synthesis by the kidney.
Systemic & Skeletal Consequences
  • Net Biochemical Shift: Combined bone resorption, renal calcium retention, and enhanced Vitamin D synthesis steadily elevate serum calcium (hypercalcemia).
  • Skeletal Manifestations: Primary hyperparathyroidism produces generalized osteoporosis, localized brown tumors, and—in severe hyperparathyroidism—osteitis fibrosa cystica.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“A parathyroid adenoma secretes unregulated PTH regardless of serum calcium—activating osteoclasts, conserving renal calcium, wasting renal phosphate, and boosting renal Vitamin D synthesis.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Severe primary hyperparathyroidism causing cystic bone resorption and marrow fibrosis is termed osteitis fibrosa cystica, paired biochemically with elevated PTH, hypercalcemia, and renal phosphate wasting.

Case 3 Synthesis: Radial Brown Tumor Secondary to Parathyroid Adenoma

LO 3.02 • Case 3 Q4–Q7 & Dr. Oviedo Photomicrographs
Case 3 Radiology & Tissue Diagnosis (Q4–Q6)
  • Arm Mass & X-Ray (Q4): A 42-year-old female presents with left arm pain and a radial mass; plain X-ray reveals an expansile lytic lesion of the radius.
  • Bone Biopsy (Q5): Histology shows reactive tissue composed of multinucleated osteoclasts, hemosiderin-laden macrophages, and fibroblasts driven by elevated PTH—diagnostic of a brown tumor.
  • Neck Mass Excision (Q6): Subsequent neck examination identifies a cervical mass whose resection histology confirms a parathyroid adenoma.
Endocrine Causation & Reactive Behavior (Q7)
  • Bone–Neck Relationship (Q7): Unregulated PTH secreted by the parathyroid adenoma stimulated focal osteoclastic bone resorption and reactive hemorrhage/fibrosis, producing the radial brown tumor.
  • Meaning of “Reactive”: Dr. Oviedo emphasizes that “reactive” indicates the lesion is not neoplastic—it is reacting to excessive systemic PTH rather than growing autonomously.
  • Clinical Outcome: Because it is reactive, the brown tumor goes away when the parathyroid adenoma is removed.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Reactive is a general word implying something is NOT neoplastic—it is reacting to some other process. The brown tumor will go away once the parathyroid adenoma is removed.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A lytic bone mass packed with osteoclasts, hemosiderin-laden macrophages, and fibroblasts should prompt a neck and serum calcium/PTH evaluation to uncover a parathyroid adenoma before treating it as a neoplasm.

Multifactorial Skeletal Pathology in Chronic Kidney Disease & Dialysis

LO 3.03 • Robbins Ch 26 & Dr. Tano Vignette
Pathogenesis & Composite Bone Lesions
  • Renal Dysfunction Triad: Pathogenesis stems from decreased tubular, glomerular, and hormonal renal functions in chronic kidney disease.
  • Four Skeletal Changes: Renal osteodystrophy encompasses osteopenia/osteoporosis, osteomalacia, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and—in children—growth retardation.
  • Clinical Interpretation: Rather than routine bone biopsy, skeletal changes are largely interpreted through a clinical history of long-standing kidney disease and laboratory medicine studies assessing renal function.
Clinical Scenario: Dialysis Hairline Fracture
  • Patient Presentation: A 55-year-old man with end-stage renal disease on dialysis presents with 3 days of right leg pain after merely opening a car door against his leg (denying falls or overt trauma).
  • Exam & Imaging: Right hip area appears bruised and swollen; hip X-ray reveals a hairline fracture of the right femur.
  • Underlying Cause: Renal osteodystrophy weakened the bone via combined osteomalacia, osteopenia, and secondary hyperparathyroidism.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Renal osteodystrophy is interpreted through clinical history of long-standing kidney disease and lab studies assessing kidney function—combining osteopenia, osteomalacia, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and growth retardation.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A dialysis patient developing a hairline femur fracture from trivial mechanical contact demonstrates skeletal fragility driven by secondary hyperparathyroidism and impaired renal hormonal/tubular function.

Paget Disease of Bone (Osteitis Deformans)

Lecturer Rule: Abundant but weak bone; mosaic cement lines, isolated high ALP, normal Ca/PO₄

Typical Age & Sites Adults over 40; femur, pelvis, axial

Genetics, Skeletal Distribution & Three-Stage Remodeling Progression

LO 4.01 • Robbins Ch 26, Fig 26.12 & OMIM 601530
Clinical Distribution & SQSTM1 Pathogenesis
  • Demographics & Anatomy: Paget disease (Osteitis Deformans) affects adults over 40 years old, favoring the proximal femur, pelvis, and axial skeleton. It may involve one bone (monostotic) or multiple bones (polyostotic) and can be asymptomatic or present with pain.
  • Genetic & Environmental Factors: Mutations in SQSTM1 (Sequestosome 1) enhance osteoclast activity and are found in 50% of familial cases and 10% of sporadic cases, alongside environmental contributors.
Disordered Remodeling & 3 Sequential Phases
  • Uncoupled Architecture: Increased osteoclast bone resorption followed by secondary abnormally increased osteoblast activity generates abundant bone that is structurally abnormal and weak.
  • Three Sequential Phases: 1) Initial osteolytic stage, 2) mixed osteoclastic-osteoblastic stage, and 3) final burned-out, quiescent osteosclerotic stage.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Unlike osteoporosis which has too little bone, Paget disease produces increased bone—but it is abnormally remodeled, structurally weak, and prone to bowing and fracture.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Inherited susceptibility to Paget disease most commonly involves a mutation in SQSTM1 (50% familial, 10% sporadic), which aberrantly enhances osteoclast activity.

Biochemical Discriminators, Malignant Potential & Anti-Resorptive Therapy

LO 4.03 • Robbins Ch 26 & ExpertPath
Laboratory Signature & Complication Risk
  • Diagnostic Lab Pattern: Laboratory medicine demonstrates elevated alkaline phosphatase paired with normal serum calcium and phosphate—separating Paget disease from hyperparathyroidism and osteomalacia.
  • Malignant Transformation: Rarely, Paget disease progresses to produce a secondary osteosarcoma or fibrosarcoma.
Targeted Pharmacologic Therapy
  • First-Line Agents: Paget disease therapy relies on calcitonin and bisphosphonates to restrain hyperactive osteoclastic remodeling.
  • Fracture Susceptibility: Even in late sclerotic bone, disordered lamellar architecture leaves the bone susceptible to fracture.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“Expect elevated alkaline phosphatase with normal calcium and phosphate; treat with calcitonin and bisphosphonates, and remember the rare risk of osteosarcoma or fibrosarcoma.”

Second-Order Board Clue

An adult over 40 with isolated elevated alkaline phosphatase and normal calcium and phosphate who develops a rapidly enlarging painful mass in a pagetic bone signals secondary osteosarcoma or fibrosarcoma.

Adolescent Epiphyseal Slippage, Frog-Leg Radiographs & Delayed-Repair Sequelae

Risk Profile & Clinical Vignettes (Case 5 Q10)

  • Dr. Tano Vignette (15-Year-Old Boy): Presents with left hip pain radiating to the left knee and a limp; physical exam reveals a shortened, externally rotated left leg.

  • Vascular Compromise: If not repaired promptly, displacement across the physis disrupts femoral head blood supply and progresses to avascular necrosis.
  • Chronic Disability: Persistent deformity leaves the femoral head distorted, impairing gait/ability to walk and causing chronic hip pain.

An adolescent with obesity presenting with limp and hip pain radiating to the knee with an externally rotated, shortened leg requires frog-leg hip radiographs to catch posterior epiphyseal slip before AVN develops.

Progression of Bone Fracture Repair & Lytic Pathologic Fractures

LO 5.03 • Robbins Fig 26.14 & Radiopaedia rID 75119
Fracture Healing Progression
  • Sequential Repair (Robbins Fig 26.14): Bone healing progresses in an orderly continuum from acute fracture hematoma and inflammatory cell recruitment to fibrocartilaginous soft callus, woven bony callus formation, and final remodeling into mature lamellar bone.
  • Metabolic Context: Throughout this lecture, traumatic and minimal-trauma fractures arise clinically when underlying metabolic or structural weakening compromises bone integrity.
Pathologic Fracture Due to Metastatic Tumor
  • Core Definition: A pathologic fracture is a fracture that occurs due to some underlying cause weakening the bone rather than normal bone failing under extreme force.
  • Lecture Case Example: Plain X-ray demonstrates a pathologic fracture of the humerus secondary to metastatic renal cell carcinoma; bone adjacent to the fracture appears lytic (more transparent) due to tumor-mediated osteolysis.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Oviedo)

“A pathologic fracture is simply a fracture that occurs due to some underlying cause—notice on the humerus X-ray how the bone adjacent to the fracture is lytic and more transparent from metastatic renal cell carcinoma.”

Second-Order Board Clue

When a humeral fracture shows lytic, more transparent bone bordering the fracture line, suspect a pathologic fracture due to metastatic tumor (such as renal cell carcinoma) rather than simple trauma.

Primary Hyperparathyroidism / Brown Tumor Endocrine Parathyroid adenoma secretes unregulated PTH → activates osteoclasts, ↑renal Ca reabsorption, ↑PO₄ excretion, ↑Vit D synthesis. Elevated PTH, hypercalcemia, renal phosphate wasting. Generalized osteoporosis, osteitis fibrosa cystica, and focal expansile lytic lesion (Case 3 radius). Reactive (non-neoplastic) mass of osteoclasts, hemosiderin macrophages, fibroblasts; usually biopsied; resolves after adenoma excision. 42yo with lytic radial mass + neck nodule; removal of parathyroid adenoma cures the bone lesion.
Renal Osteodystrophy Chronic Kidney Chronic renal disease impairing tubular, glomerular, and hormonal renal functions. Interpreted via long-standing renal disease history and renal function / mineral labs. Composite of osteopenia, osteomalacia, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and pediatric growth retardation; hairline femur fracture. Biopsy rarely done; bone changes reflect overlapping resorption, unmineralized osteoid, and low mass. 55yo ESRD dialysis patient with hairline femur fracture after bumping car door without a fall.
Paget Disease of Bone Remodeling Increased resorption + secondary abnormal osteoblast activity; SQSTM1 mutations (50% familial, 10% sporadic); 3 phases: osteolytic → mixed → osteosclerotic. Elevated alkaline phosphatase with normal calcium and phosphate. Adults >40 (femur, pelvis, axial; mono/polyostotic); thickened cortex, thickened trabeculae, bowed femur (Case 4). Mosaic pattern of lamellar bone, irregular cement lines (vs. smooth normal), giant osteoclasts with excess nuclei; rare osteosarcoma; treat with calcitonin/bisphosphonates. One of two routinely biopsied lesions; isolated ↑ALP with bowed thick femur and jigsaw cement lines.
Slipped Capital Femoral Epiphysis Pediatric Physis Mechanical slip across an open growth plate, typically in teenagers with obese-range BMI (Case 5 Q10). Clinical and radiographic diagnosis; no metabolic lab hallmark. Frog-leg radiograph shows posterior displacement of capital femoral epiphysis; shortened externally rotated leg. If unrepaired, progresses to distorted femoral head, chronic pain/gait impairment, and avascular necrosis (Case 5 Q11). Obese adolescent (e.g., BMI 33) with limp and hip pain radiating to knee.
Avascular Necrosis Osteonecrosis Bone infarction from femoral neck fracture, unrepaired SCFE, corticosteroids, or sickle cell crisis occluding hypercellular marrow vessels. Clinical/MRI/pathology diagnosis (e.g., HbSS history). Subchondral wedge-shaped infarction of femoral head; sagittal T1 MRI shows multifocal femur/femoral head/tibia infarcts in HbSS. Necrotic subchondral trabeculae beneath intact cartilage (Case 5 Q11 femoral head resection, Rosai sem57a-case9). 22yo HbSS post-URI with acute hot painful knee or teenager lost to follow-up 8 months after SCFE.
