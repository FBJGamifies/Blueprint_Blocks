Cellular Organization of the Neuromuscular Junction

LO 1

Structural Tripartite Components

  • Motor Neuron & Presynaptic Terminal: Distal motor nerve ending innervates skeletal muscle fibers to mediate both reflex and voluntary contractions, storing acetylcholine (ACh) inside synaptic vesicles.
  • Synaptic Cleft: Extracellular gap separating the motor nerve terminal from the muscle membrane across which released ACh rapidly diffuses.
  • Postsynaptic Motor Endplate: Specialized sarcolemmal region arranged in deep junctional folds whose high-density crests concentrate ligand-gated nicotinic ACh receptors (nAChRs).

Signal Fidelity & Receptor Coupling

  • High receptor density along folded junctional crests ensures reliable safety factor transmission from every presynaptic nerve impulse to sarcolemmal depolarization.
  • Binding of two ACh molecules to a postsynaptic nAChR opens its cation pore, permitting rapid Na+ influx into the muscle cell to depolarize the motor endplate.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

Dr. Ontiveros emphasizes that the junctional architecture of the motor endplate directly governs transmission efficiency: any loss or flattening of those high-density receptor crests sharply drops the muscle cell’s ability to depolarize.

Second-Order Board Clue

Presynaptic vesicle release -> synaptic cleft diffusion -> 2 ACh molecules bind postsynaptic nAChR -> Na+ influx -> endplate depolarization. Distinguish presynaptic release defects from postsynaptic nAChR blockade.

Excitation-Contraction Coupling & Calcium Homeostasis

LO 2

Mechanical DHPR–RyR1 Activation & Contraction

  • Depolarization Wave: Sarcolemmal depolarization sweeps down transverse tubules (T-tubules) and activates the voltage-sensing L-type Ca2+ channel, DHPR.
  • Direct Mechanical Pull: Conformational movement of DHPR physically pulls open RyR1 channels embedded in the terminal cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR).
  • Contraction Initiation: Massive SR Ca2+ efflux floods the cytosol, binds troponin C, shifts tropomyosin off actin binding sites, and drives actin-myosin cross-bridge cycling.

SERCA Reuptake & Mitochondrial Buffering

  • SR Re-Sequestration: Muscle relaxation depends on active ATP-driven transport of cytosolic Ca2+ back into the SR lumen via the SERCA pump.
  • Mitochondrial Buffering: Adjacent mitochondria take up and buffer transient cytosolic Ca2+ elevations to support oxidative ATP production while preventing toxic Ca2+ overload.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

Skeletal muscle DHPR functions primarily as a mechanical voltage sensor tethered to RyR1. If the physical linkage between DHPR and RyR1 is lost, depolarization cannot pull RyR1 open even when SR calcium stores are intact.

Second-Order Board Clue

T-tubule depolarization -> DHPR conformational pull -> RyR1 opening -> SR Ca2+ release -> Troponin C -> cross-bridge cycling. Relaxation failure or cytosolic overload implicates SERCA or leaky RyR1.

Autoimmune Disruption of Neuromuscular Transmission

LO 3

Autoantibody Blockade & Fold Simplification

  • Competitive Blockade: Acquired autoantibodies bind postsynaptic nAChRs, physically blocking ACh attachment and preventing Na+ entry and membrane depolarization.
  • Receptor Destruction: Cross-linked autoantibodies accelerate receptor internalization and destruction, simplifying and flattening postsynaptic junctional folds.
  • Downstream Failure: Reduced endplate potential fails to reliably activate T-tubule DHPR and SR RyR1 Ca2+ release, decreasing contractile force.

Clinical Course, Diagnosis & Treatment

  • Hallmark Pattern: Fatigable skeletal muscle weakness that worsens with activity (as presynaptic ACh stores naturally taper during repeated firing) and improves with rest, progressing from mild to severe over weeks or months with exacerbations and remissions.
  • Diagnosis: Detection of circulating anti-AChR autoantibodies in patient serum.
  • Management: Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitors such as pyridostigmine prevent ACh breakdown in the synaptic cleft, increasing ACh dwell time to outcompete autoantibodies at remaining nAChRs.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

When a vignette describes muscle weakness that progressively worsens across the day or with repeated exertion and visibly rebounds after rest, think Myasthenia Gravis immediately—autoantibodies both competitively block nAChRs and drive receptor loss.

Second-Order Board Clue

Fatigable weakness + serum anti-AChR antibodies -> flattened postsynaptic junctional folds -> treat with pyridostigmine (AChE inhibitor) to elevate synaptic cleft ACh and restore endplate depolarization.

Central Core Disease: RyR1 Calcium Paradox & Core Histology

LO 4

Two Divergent RyR1 Mechanisms ("Calcium Paradox")

  • Autosomal Dominant Basis: Congenital myopathy presenting from birth through childhood; mutations in RyR1 account for ~57–67% (~60%) of CCD cases.
  • Leak-Inducing Mutations: RyR1 leaks continuous excessive Ca2+ into the cytosol -> mitochondrial Ca2+ overload, ROS generation, cellular stress, and SR depletion.
  • Defective DHPR–RyR1 Uncoupling: DHPR and RyR1 fail to link properly -> inadequate Ca2+ release upon stimulation -> weak contraction and reduced mitochondrial activity.

Biopsy Cores & Congenital Phenotype

  • Histology: Skeletal muscle biopsy demonstrates well-demarcated central pale areas ("cores") running along the fiber axis characterized by reduced mitochondrial content and absent oxidative/cellular activity.
  • Clinical Features: Delayed motor milestones, congenital muscle hypotonia (decreased muscle tone), and symmetric proximal weakness predominantly affecting the hip girdle and quadriceps.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

Both excessive cytosolic Ca2+ leakage (causing mitochondrial overload/stress) and uncoupled DHPR–RyR1 failure (causing insufficient Ca2+ release) converge on the same clinical result: weak skeletal muscle contraction and central mitochondria-depleted pale cores.

Second-Order Board Clue

Infant/child with hypotonia, delayed motor milestones, and hip girdle/quadriceps weakness + biopsy showing central pale cores lacking mitochondria -> autosomal dominant RyR1 mutation.

Malignant Hyperthermia Susceptibility & Emergency Countermeasure

CRITICAL ALERT • LO 4

Anesthetic-Induced Hypermetabolic Crisis

  • Shared RyR1 Susceptibility: Patients harboring certain RyR1 mutations—including those with Central Core Disease—are exquisitely sensitive to volatile inhalational anesthetics during surgery.
  • Pathophysiologic Cascade: Anesthetic exposure locks mutant RyR1 open, triggering massive, uncontrolled SR Ca2+ dumping into the cytosol -> sustained cross-bridge muscle rigidity, relentless ATP consumption by SERCA and myosin ATPase, severe hypermetabolism, and life-threatening hyperthermia/fever.

Surgical Vigilance & Dantrolene Rescue

  • Preoperative Precaution: Dr. Ontiveros emphasizes that surgeons and the entire anesthesia team must exercise extreme caution and avoid triggering anesthetics in any patient with known or suspected RyR1 channelopathy.
  • Acute Countermeasure: Immediate cessation of volatile anesthetic and administration of dantrolene, which directly inhibits RyR1 Ca2+ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum to halt muscle rigidity and hypermetabolism.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

Never treat Central Core Disease as isolated childhood weakness: always connect RyR1 mutations to perioperative Malignant Hyperthermia susceptibility—failure to screen before anesthesia can be fatal.

Second-Order Board Clue

Child with congenital hypotonia (or family history of CCD) develops sudden jaw/body rigidity and spiking temperature under general anesthesia -> RyR1 uncontrolled Ca2+ efflux -> immediate dantrolene.

Sarcolemma Strength, Reading-Frame Rule & Necrotic Cascade

LO 5

Structural Bridge & Reading-Frame Genetics

  • Mechanical Anchoring: Desmin anchors sarcomeric Z-disks to the sarcolemma, while dystrophin links cytoplasmic F-actin to the transmembrane dystroglycan and sarcoglycan complexes (DAPC), which bind extracellular laminin-2.
  • DMD vs. Becker (BMD): Both are X-linked recessive (`DMD` gene). DMD stems from out-of-frame frameshift or nonsense mutations causing complete loss of function; BMD stems from in-frame deletions yielding partially functional truncated dystrophin and milder disease.

Delta Lesions & Myonecrosis Cascade

  • Sarcolemmal Tearing: Without dystrophin, contraction stresses rip physical tears (delta lesions) in the fragile sarcolemma.
  • Calcium-Driven Destruction: Unregulated extracellular Ca2+ floods the fiber -> mitochondrial Ca2+ overload & ROS cycle of dysfunction + activation of Ca2+-dependent calpain proteases -> myonecrosis and fibrofatty replacement.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

DMD represents a near-total loss-of-function of dystrophin: once mechanical delta lesions form, calcium entry triggers a self-amplifying mitochondrial ROS and calpain protease cycle that kills myofibers.

Second-Order Board Clue

Young boy with X-linked recessive weakness -> out-of-frame DMD deletion -> absent dystrophin -> sarcolemma delta lesions -> Ca2+ influx activates calpains and mitochondrial ROS.

LO5: DMD Clinical & CRISPR

Clinical Hallmarks Gowers sign, pseudohypertrophy
Serum Biomarkers High CK; miR-1, 133, 206
Standard Medications Corticosteroids, ACEi, beta-blockers
Gene-Editing Strategy CRISPR/Cas9 exon 51 skipping

DMD Presentation, Early Biomarkers & CRISPR/Cas9 Frame Rescue

LO 5

Clinical Progression & Biomarkers

  • Classic Exam Findings: Proximal pelvic/limb-girdle weakness in young boys manifests as Gowers sign (walking hands up the thighs to stand), calf pseudohypertrophy (fibrofatty infiltration), and eventual wheelchair dependence.
  • Early Biomarkers: Massive sarcolemmal leakage elevates serum creatine kinase (CK) alongside circulating muscle-specific microRNAs (miR-1, miR-133, miR-206) used for early detection.

Supportive Regimen & CRISPR/Cas9 Restoration

  • Current Therapy: Corticosteroids slow muscle deterioration; ACE inhibitors and beta-blockers protect against cardiomyopathy, paired with physical mobility aids.
  • CRISPR/Cas9 Application: A guide RNA scaffold directs Cas9 molecular scissors to cut DNA flanking mutated regions (e.g., exon 51 skipping), restoring the reading frame to convert a severe null DMD phenotype into a functional truncated Becker-like dystrophin (validated in canines).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Ontiveros)

CRISPR/Cas9 does not need to rebuild the entire giant dystrophin gene—restoring the reading frame by excising/skipping a disruptive exon (such as exon 51) restores partially functional dystrophin protein.

Second-Order Board Clue

Boy using hands to climb legs (Gowers sign) + calf pseudohypertrophy + marked CK elevation -> treat with corticosteroids (+ ACEi/beta-blocker); experimental CRISPR/Cas9 restores the reading frame.

Autosomal Dominant Repeat Expansion & RNA Gain-of-Function Toxicity

LO 6

DM1 vs. DM2 Architecture & Repeat Thresholds

  • DM1 (Most Common, 1 in 8,000): Autosomal dominant CTG trinucleotide repeat expansion in the non-coding 3' UTR of DMPK (dystrophia myotonica protein kinase) on chromosome 19q. Wild-type = 5–30 repeats; full-blown DM1 = 100 to several thousand repeats.
  • DM2 Contrast: Autosomal dominant on chromosome 3q caused by a four-nucleotide (tetranucleotide) repeat with only mild anticipation.

Nuclear CUG Foci & MBNL1 Mis-Splicing

  • RNA Toxicity (Not Protein Loss): Expanded CUG-repeat DMPK mRNA gets trapped inside the nucleus, folding into hairpin foci that bind and sequester the splicing regulator MBNL1.
  • Downstream Mis-Splicing: Depletion of functional MBNL1 disrupts alternative splicing of CLCN1 (muscle chloride channel -> hyperexcitability & myotonia) and the insulin receptor (insulin resistance).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gardner)

Never confuse Myotonic Dystrophy with Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy—different genes, different inheritance, and different mechanism. Remember Dr. Gardner’s mnemonic: repeats begin with C and end with G, and myoTonic puts a T in the middle (CTG).

Second-Order Board Clue

AD 19q 3' UTR CTG expansion -> nuclear CUG mRNA foci trap MBNL1 -> defective splicing of CLCN1 chloride channel -> delayed muscle relaxation (myotonia).

Multisystem Manifestations, Anticipation & Congenital DM1

LO 6

Classic Multisystem Presentation

  • Neuromuscular & Cardiac: Grip myotonia (inability to promptly release a handshake), progressive muscle loss/weakness, and potentially fatal cardiac conduction arrhythmias.
  • Ocular & Endocrine: Early-onset cataracts, insulin resistance, and in affected men prominent frontal balding and testicular atrophy.

Anticipation & Maternal Congenital Form

  • Genetic Anticipation: Two defining features down a pedigree: earlier age of onset and more severe expression as CTG repeats further expand across generations.
  • Transmission & Congenital DM1: Either parent can expand and transmit the DM1 repeat, but massive expansion (>1,000 repeats) almost exclusively follows maternal transmission, producing severe congenital DM1 with neonatal hypotonia and respiratory failure.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gardner)

Anticipation requires two things on board pedigrees: earlier onset AND worsening severity in successive generations—unlike DM2 (mild anticipation), DM1 exhibits dramatic anticipation culminating in maternal congenital DM1.

Second-Order Board Clue

Adult with grip myotonia, cataracts, arrhythmia, and frontal balding whose newborn grandchild has severe neonatal hypotonia and respiratory failure -> maternal CTG repeat expansion >1,000.

Opposite Molecular Mechanisms at the Same FMR1 Locus

LO 7

FXTAS Premutation (RNA Toxicity)

  • Repeat Range (55–199 CGG): Located in the 5' UTR of the X-linked FMR1 gene; exhibits increased transcription (marked excess of FMR1 mRNA plus elevated antisense RNA).
  • Protein Paradox: Despite elevated mRNA, FMRP protein is NOT increased (normal amounts); excess CGG-containing mRNA is toxic inside the nucleus, just like CUG mRNA in DM1.

Fragile X Full Mutation (Loss of Function)

  • Repeat Range (>200 CGG): Triggers promoter hypermethylation and complete gene silencing—zero FMR1 mRNA and zero FMRP protein are produced.
  • Normal FMRP Function: Expressed strongly in brain; regulates synaptic plasticity and inhibits translation by interacting with eIF4E at the mRNA 5' cap. Loss of FMRP causes childhood intellectual disability in Fragile X.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gardner)

Even though Fragile X is in the name, FXTAS is a completely different disease from Fragile X Syndrome! Fragile X shuts FMR1 OFF (no RNA, no FMRP), whereas FXTAS turns FMR1 UP (excess toxic mRNA with normal FMRP).

Second-Order Board Clue

55–199 CGG repeats in FMR1 -> increased transcription + toxic nuclear mRNA (and antisense RNA) rather than promoter methylation.

Intranuclear Inclusions, Penetrance & Late-Onset Neurodegeneration

LO 7

Histopathology & X-Linked Penetrance

  • Pathologic Hallmark: Nuclear accumulation of toxic FMR1 mRNA produces characteristic eosinophilic ubiquitin-positive intranuclear inclusions within both neurons and astrocytes.
  • Demographics & Penetrance: X-linked inheritance with age-dependent penetrance—approximately 30% of adult premutation males develop FXTAS (frequency rises with age >50 years); heterozygous carrier women show very low penetrance.

Clinical Triad & Crucial Exclusion

  • Movement & Cognition: Progressive intention tremor, cerebellar gait ataxia, parkinsonism, executive function deficits, cognitive decline, and dementia.
  • Youth Baseline: Unlike full-mutation Fragile X boys, FXTAS premutation carriers are intellectually normal in youth without childhood intellectual disability.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gardner)

Look for the classic family pedigree clue: an intellectually normal grandfather over 50 develops intention tremor, gait ataxia, and executive decline—while his grandson has childhood Fragile X intellectual disability.

Second-Order Board Clue

Male >50 years with intention tremor, cerebellar ataxia, parkinsonism, and no history of childhood intellectual disability -> biopsy shows eosinophilic intranuclear inclusions in neurons/astrocytes.

D4Z4 Epigenetic Contraction & Permissive pLAM Polyadenylation

LO 8

Hypomethylation & Ectopic DUX4 Activation

  • Normal vs. Contracted Array: Normal individuals have 10–100 D4Z4 macrosatellite repeats on chromosome 4q35.2, keeping chromatin hypermethylated and silent in adult muscle.
  • FSHD1 Contraction: Reduction to 1–10 D4Z4 repeats causes chromatin hypomethylation and ectopic activation of the double-homeobox transcription factor DUX4 in adult skeletal muscle.
  • Repeat-Severity Rule: Fewer D4Z4 repeats correlate with earlier onset and more rapid disease progression.

The Permissive 4qA (pLAM) Requirement

  • Cis Polyadenylation Signal: Contraction alone does NOT cause FSHD unless the telomeric functional DUX4 gene is linked to a permissive 4qA allele carrying a functional pLAM sequence.
  • 3' UTR Stabilization: pLAM supplies the 3' UTR polyadenylation signal essential for stabilizing DUX4 mRNA in non-embryonic muscle; non-permissive 4qB alleles lack pLAM, preventing toxic DUX4 expression.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gardner)

Dr. Gardner highlights the "additional genetic complexity" of FSHD: both D4Z4 contraction (1–10 repeats causing hypomethylation) AND a downstream pLAM polyadenylation signal (4qA) are mandatory—without pLAM, toxic DUX4 mRNA cannot be stabilized.

Second-Order Board Clue

Why does a patient with only 4 D4Z4 repeats on 4q35.2 remain asymptomatic? The contracted array sits on a non-permissive 4qB allele lacking the pLAM 3' UTR polyadenylation signal.

Asymmetric Facioscapulohumeral Weakness & Deltoid Sparing

LO 8

Facial & Scapular Findings

  • Facial Muscle Weakness: Prominent orbicularis oris/oculi weakness yields a characteristic "sucking on a straw" look, transverse smile, and difficulty whistling or tightly closing eyelids.
  • Scapular Winging: Weakness of periscapular stabilizers (serratus anterior and trapezius) causes asymmetric protruding shoulder blades when raising the arms.

Humeral Pattern & Characteristic Sparing

  • Asymmetry: Unlike symmetric limb-girdle dystrophies, FSHD characteristically presents with pronounced right-vs-left asymmetry.
  • Humeral Involvement with Spared Deltoid: Upper arm muscles (biceps/triceps) weaken noticeably while the deltoid muscle is classically spared, creating a distinct shoulder-to-arm contour step-off.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Gardner)

Spot FSHD visually on clinical exam questions: asymmetric scapular winging + pursed "sucking on a straw" facial appearance + upper arm weakness with preserved deltoid bulk.

Second-Order Board Clue

Asymmetric facial weakness + protruding scapulae (serratus anterior/trapezius) + humeral wasting with spared deltoid -> autosomal dominant 4q35.2 D4Z4 contraction on 4qA (pLAM+).

FXTAS
FMR1 5' UTR Premutation (X-linked)		 55–199 CGG repeats -> increased transcription (excess toxic mRNA & antisense RNA) with normal FMRP (opposite of Fragile X >200 methylation silencing). Eosinophilic intranuclear inclusions in neurons and astrocytes; unmethylated promoter. Males >50y (~30% penetrance): intention tremor, cerebellar gait ataxia, parkinsonism, executive decline; normal youth intellect. Same gene as Fragile X, completely different disease & mechanism—no childhood intellectual disability. Grandfather >50y with intention tremor + ataxia + Fragile X grandson -> 55–199 CGG toxic mRNA.
FSHD1
Chr 4q35.2 D4Z4 / DUX4 (AD)		 Contraction from 10–100 to 1–10 D4Z4 repeats -> hypomethylation + ectopic DUX4 expression ONLY on permissive 4qA allele (pLAM+) stabilizing 3' UTR mRNA. 4q35.2 D4Z4 contraction (1–10 repeats) paired with cis 4qA pLAM polyadenylation signal (4qB lacks pLAM). Asymmetric facial weakness ("sucking on a straw"), scapular winging (serratus anterior/trapezius), humeral wasting with spared deltoid. Fewer repeats = earlier, faster disease; contraction on 4qB (no pLAM) is non-pathogenic. Asymmetric scapular winging + spared deltoid + facial weakness -> 1–10 D4Z4 on 4qA (pLAM).
