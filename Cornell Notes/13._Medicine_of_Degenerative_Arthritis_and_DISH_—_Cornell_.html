LO 1.01 • Taxonomy

Arthritis Umbrella & Primary OA

Umbrella Hierarchy Degenerative Joint Disease
Primary OA Caveat Must be idiopathic
Core Drivers Genetic predisposition, age, wear
Inflammatory Link Leads to secondary OA

Diagnostic Hierarchy: Arthritis → DJD → Primary vs. Secondary OA

LO 1.01 • Slides 2–3, 13
Conceptual Umbrella
  • Etymology & Broadest Term: Arthro- (joint) + -itis (inflammation) forms the broadest umbrella covering both Degenerative Joint Disease (any structural joint deterioration) and inflammatory arthritides.
  • Primary OA Definition: The “garden variety” degenerative joint disease of older adults affecting bones and cartilage, driven by genetic predisposition, age, and wear.
  • Strict Exclusion Rule: Primary OA carries a mandatory caveat—it cannot be due to any known trauma, misalignment, metabolic, or inflammatory insult and must remain strictly idiopathic.
Inflammatory Conversion
  • Inflammatory Arthritis Branches: Encompasses autoimmune (RA, PsA, Ankylosing Spondylitis, SLE), crystalline (gout, pseudogout), reactive (post-GI/STI), and septic (Neisseria gonorrhoeae) etiologies.
  • Convergence Rule: Any type of inflammatory arthritis damages articular architecture over time and can subsequently lead to secondary OA.
  • Pre-existing Insult Distinction: Unlike primary OA, secondary OA develops from a documented mechanical, structural, metabolic, or inflammatory insult to articular cartilage and subchondral bone.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson)

“Primary OA is the garden variety form older people get, but the caveat is huge: it cannot be due to any secondary cause—it must be strictly idiopathic. And remember, any inflammatory arthritis can eventually burn out into secondary OA.”

Second-Order Board Clue

When a vignette describes chronic joint degeneration in a young patient or an atypical joint (ankle, shoulder, wrist), do not label it Primary OA—hunt for prior trauma, CPPD, hemochromatosis, or prior septic arthritis driving secondary OA.

Trauma Cases (#1 vs. #2) & Six Secondary OA Pathomechanics

LO 1.02 • Slides 4–13
Clinical Trauma Comparison
  • Case #1 (Dashboard Knee → Acetabulum): High-speed restrained MVC driving the knee into the dashboard causes an intra-articular fracture, leading to direct chondrocyte injury, focal contact stress, and inevitable post-traumatic arthritis.
  • Case #2 (40yo State Officer): Struck by a car at high speed with an extra-articular femur fracture fixed via intramedullary nail; 8-month films healed cleanly and he returned to full duty at 9 months because it was not an intra-articular fracture.
  • Metabolic Syndrome-Associated OA: Listed as a dedicated secondary OA phenotype alongside anatomical malalignment (genu varum / valgum) and ligament/meniscal tears (ACL/PCL).
Systemic & Structural Categories
  • Anatomical & Vascular: DDH, FAI, SCFE alter geometry causing asymmetric wear; AVN / osteonecrosis and Legg-Calve-Perthes disease cause subchondral collapse and loss of cartilage support.
  • Metabolic & Crystal: CPPD, gout, hemochromatosis, and ochronosis (alkaptonuria) trigger crystal-mediated inflammation and matrix degeneration.
  • Neuropathic & Infectious: Diabetic neuropathy, syringomyelia, and tabes dorsalis remove protective sensation causing repetitive microtrauma (Charcot); prior septic arthritis directly destroys cartilage.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson)

“Which injury would you choose? Not intra-articular fractures! Our state officer had a severe high-speed shaft fracture but returned to full duty with zero restrictions at 9 months because his joint surface was spared.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A patient with darkened sclera/ear cartilage (ochronosis), bronze diabetes (hemochromatosis), or painless destroyed midfoot joints (diabetic neuropathy) develops premature degenerative joint disease via distinct secondary OA pathomechanics.

Cartilage Fibrillation, Subchondral Remodeling & Imaging Indications

LO 2.01 • Slides 15–19
Articular Pathologic Cascade
  • Extracellular Matrix Breakdown: Normal articular cartilage relies on proteoglycans and collagen; altered chondrocyte function initiates cartilage fibrillation, progressive loss, and joint space narrowing (JSN).
  • Bone Remodeling Triad: Exposed bone undergoes subchondral bone sclerosis (eburnation/thickening), subchondral cysts (fluid-filled pseudocysts), and peripheral marginal osteophytes (spurs).
  • Secondary Synovial Reaction: Cartilage wear debris provokes only a mild reactive synovitis (non-erosive), and loose fragments can form intra-articular bodies (“joint mice”).
Radiographic & Axis Rules
  • Gold-Standard Initial Film: Always obtain weightbearing radiographs to reveal true asymmetric joint space narrowing under physiologic load.
  • MRI Choosing Wisely: There is no indication for MRI in advanced OA with classic deformity (e.g., genu valgus on plain film) unless specifically concerned about a tumor.
  • Limb Mechanical Axis: Line A represents normal alignment; genu valgus (more common in females with OA and in inflammatory arthritides) shifts load laterally, while varus deformity (more common in men) overloads the medial compartment.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson)

“Always order weightbearing radiographs—supine films underestimate cartilage loss! And if you already see classic OA with genu valgus on X-ray, there is zero indication for an MRI unless you are worried about a tumor.”

Second-Order Board Clue

An older man with chronic knee pain and bow-legged alignment demonstrates medial compartment narrowing (genu varus), subchondral sclerosis, and osteophytes without systemic inflammatory markers.

Heberden vs. Bouchard Nodes, CMC Involvement & Mechanical Symptoms

LO 2.02 • Slides 15–16, 32
Hand OA Anatomical Signature
  • Heberden vs. Bouchard Nodes: Hard, bony osteophytic enlargements at the distal interphalangeal (DIP) joints are Heberden nodes, whereas those at the proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joints are Bouchard nodes.
  • Base of Thumb & Heritability: Hand OA is strongly heritable and classically targets the 1st CMC (trapeziometacarpal) joint, producing squaring at the thumb base.
  • Critical Negative Discriminator: Primary OA usually spares the MCP joints—a hallmark contrast against rheumatoid arthritis.
Symptoms & Physical Exam Signs
  • Pain & Gelling Pattern: Insidious onset of deep, achy joint pain aggravated by movement and weightbearing (“Ouch! Please don’t make me jump!”), accompanied by minimal morning/inactivity gelling lasting < 30 minutes that improves with motion.
  • Palpation & Motion Signs: Joint line tenderness, limited ROM, palpable/audible crepitus, and spasm or atrophy of adjacent muscles.
  • Effusions & Late Deformity: Non-inflammatory cool effusions (no warmth or erythema) and late genu valgus or varus malalignment.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson)

“Think of the patient saying ‘Ouch! Please don’t make me jump!’—OA is mechanical pain on loading, stiffness under 30 minutes, cool effusions, crepitus, and Heberden/Bouchard nodes while sparing the MCPs.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A 64-year-old seamstress with hard, non-tender bony nodules at the DIP and 1st CMC joints, 15 minutes of morning stiffness, and spared MCP joints requires plain radiographs rather than RF/anti-CCP screening.

Asymmetric Wear vs. Symmetric Autoimmune Synovitis

LO 3.01 • Slides 2, 26, 29–31
Symmetry & Hand Mapping
  • RA Systemic Autoimmunity: The most common autoimmune arthritis; attacks the synovial lining of small joints symmetrically in the hands (MCPs and PIPs), wrists, and feet.
  • Opposite DIP/MCP Rule: RA spares the DIP joints (unlike OA, which classically targets DIPs and 1st CMC while sparing MCPs).
  • Classic Hand Deformities: Chronic RA synovitis produces swan neck deformities, boutonniere deformities, and ulnar drift of the MCPs; OA produces bony Heberden/Bouchard nodes.
Temporal Rhythm & Systemic Burden
  • Pain Timing Contrast: OA pain worsens with use and peaks at the end of the day with < 30 min gelling; RA causes prolonged morning stiffness (> 60 min) that improves as the day progresses.
  • Joint Palpation: OA exhibits hard, bony, cool effusions; RA presents with soft, warm, boggy synovial swelling.
  • Systemic Organ Involvement: OA has zero systemic organ manifestations; RA is a systemic illness capable of affecting internal organs such as the lungs and eyes.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson)

“Memorize the hand flip: RA hits the MCPs and wrists with ulnar drift and swan-neck deformities and SPARES the DIPs—the exact opposite of OA, which loves the DIPs and 1st CMC and spares the MCPs.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A middle-aged woman with symmetric boggy swelling of bilateral MCP and wrist joints, 90 minutes of morning stiffness, and dry eyes/interstitial lung findings has Rheumatoid Arthritis, not degenerative OA.

Interpreting Inflammatory Serologies, “Seronegative” Arthropathies & Arthrocentesis

LO 3.02 • Slides 24, 27–28, 30
Serologic & Synovial Profiles
  • Meaning of “Seronegative”: Because RA is the most common autoimmune arthritis, “seronegative” specifically denotes the absence of rheumatoid factor (RF) and anti-CCP (cyclic citrullinated peptide) antibodies in blood tests (classic in PsA, AS, Reactive).
  • Inflammatory Markers: RA elevates ESR and CRP alongside RF/anti-CCP; primary OA demonstrates normal ESR/CRP and negative autoantibodies.
  • Synovial Fluid Cell Count: OA yields non-inflammatory fluid (< 2,000 WBC/µL), whereas RA yields inflammatory synovial fluid (2,000–50,000 WBC/µL).
Radiographic Joint-Line Contrast
  • RA Bare-Area Attack: Synovial pannus erodes bone at the capsular bare areas producing marginal erosions, symmetric joint space loss, and periarticular osteopenia.
  • Primary OA Preservation: Primary OA shows asymmetric joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis, and osteophytes with NO erosions and normal bone mineralization.
  • HLA-B27 Seronegative Spine Link: Ankylosing Spondylitis is a classic seronegative spondyloarthropathy targeting the spine and SI joints with HLA-B27 positivity.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson)

“KNOW what ‘seronegative’ actually means: it simply means RA autoantibodies—RF and anti-CCP—do not show up in the blood test. And remember, Primary OA has NO erosions!”

Second-Order Board Clue

A patient with sausage digits (dactylitis), nail pitting, and negative RF and anti-CCP falls into the seronegative spondyloarthropathy group (Psoriatic Arthritis), distinct from both seropositive RA and non-erosive OA.

Erosive Hand OA vs. Classic Erosion Patterns & Crystal Associations

LO 4.01 • Slides 19, 22–23
Erosive Hand OA Phenotype
  • Aggressive Subset: Unlike primary OA (which typically has NO erosions), erosive hand OA is a rapidly progressing, severely painful inflammatory subset with strong genetic predisposition in peri/menopausal women.
  • Gull-Wing / Saw-Tooth Appearance: Subchondral central erosions collapse the central joint surface while marginal osteophytes flare outward, creating a “gull-wing” or “saw-tooth” profile that can progress to ankylosis (bony fusion/stiffness).
  • Crystal Relationship: Osteoarthritic cartilage degeneration can coexist with or trigger secondary crystal deposition of CPPD and uric acid microcrystals, amplifying local synovial inflammation and erosive progression.
6 Salient Joint Erosion Patterns
  • RA vs. Erosive OA: RA creates marginal erosions at capsular bare areas, whereas Erosive OA creates central erosions (“gull-wing” deformity).
  • Gout vs. PsA: Gout with tophus (uric acid crystals) produces juxta-articular “rat bite” erosions with overhanging margins; Psoriatic Arthritis produces the “pencil-in-cup” deformity.
  • Septic Arthritis & Scleroderma: Septic arthritis causes ill-defined, rapidly progressive loss of the subchondral “white line”; scleroderma causes distal tuft resorption (acro-osteolysis).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson)

“Know all erosion signatures cold: Primary OA has NO erosions! Erosive OA has central gull-wing erosions, RA has marginal erosions, Gout has rat-bite erosions, PsA has pencil-in-cup, and Septic arthritis wipes out the white line rapidly.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A 53-year-old perimenopausal woman develops acute flares of severe DIP pain with radiographs showing central “gull-wing” erosions and early interphalangeal ankylosis—distinguish this aggressive Erosive Hand OA from RA (which spares DIPs and causes marginal erosions).

Cervical/Lumbar Spondylosis, Degenerative Disc Disease & Postural Shift

LO 4.02 • Slides 19–21
Spinal OA (Spondylosis) & DDD
  • Predominant Spinal Regions: OA of the spine (spondylosis) predominantly involves the cervical and lumbar spine (unlike DISH, which favors the thoracic spine), affecting facet (zygapophyseal) joints and intervertebral disc spaces.
  • Cervical Spondylosis Curve Shift: Loss of disc height and facet arthritis cause loss of the normal lordotic curve, progressing first to a straight neck and then to kyphosis (protracted head).
Neural Encroachment & Disc Loss
  • Degenerative Disc Disease (DDD): Progressive loss of disc height concentrates axial load onto facet joints and triggers vertebral endplate osteophytosis.
  • Neural Compression Risk: Posterior and uncovertebral osteophytes narrow exit canals, causing neuroforaminal encroachment and radicular symptoms.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson)

“Remember the anatomical contrast: Spinal OA (spondylosis) lives in the cervical and lumbar spine, narrows disc height, flattens cervical lordosis into kyphosis, and causes neuroforaminal encroachment.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A patient with neck pain, protracted head posture, and radiating arm paresthesias has lateral radiographs showing loss of disc height, facet arthropathy, and neuroforaminal encroachment from cervical spondylosis.

Anatomical OA Subsets: Knee Compartments, Hip Pole Migration & Foot/Ankle

LO 4.03 • Slide 19
Knee & Hip Mechanical Patterns
  • Knee Compartments: Divided into medial compartment (most common, often leading to varus deformity), lateral compartment (associated with valgus deformity), and patellofemoral OA.
  • Hip OA Migration Patterns: Categorized mechanically into superior/lateral pole migration (frequently progressive) versus medial/concentric migration.
Foot & Ankle OA Targets
  • 1st MTP Joint: Primary foot OA most commonly affects the 1st metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint, presenting clinically as hallux rigidus or hallux valgus.
  • Ankle & Hindfoot Rule: Involvement of the talocrural or subtalar joints is typically post-traumatic (secondary OA) rather than primary idiopathic wear.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson)

“Each lower-extremity joint has its signature mechanical pattern: medial knee drives varus, lateral drives valgus, superior/lateral hip migration tends to progress rapidly, and ankle OA is almost always post-traumatic.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Unlike primary idiopathic OA of the 1st MTP or medial knee, severe isolated talocrural joint narrowing in a 45-year-old points to post-traumatic secondary OA from a prior intra-articular ankle fracture.

Endplate Traction Osteophytes (OA) vs. Marginal Bridging Syndesmophytes (AS)

LO 5.01 • Slides 2, 38–39
OA Marginal Osteophytes
  • Origin & Orientation: In OA / degenerative joint disease, osteophytes project horizontally outward from the vertebral endplate margin or curve as a traction claw.
  • Disc Space Status: Driven by disc degeneration and loss of disc height, osteophytes enlarge surface area to distribute mechanical stress rather than fusing the intervertebral space.
  • Sacroiliac Status: Degenerative spinal OA does not cause inflammatory sacroiliitis or bilateral SI joint ankylosis.
Ankylosing Spondylitis Syndesmophytes
  • Vertical Bridging Vector: Marginal syndesmophytes ossify the outer annulus fibrosus and grow vertically to bridge adjacent disc spaces, culminating in complete spinal ankylosis.
  • Radiographic Hallmark: Continuous vertical bridging across vertebral levels creates the classic “bamboo spine” appearance.
  • Inflammatory Context: Occurs in Ankylosing Spondylitis (axial spondyloarthritis), strongly linked to the HLA-B27 gene with early targeting of the sacroiliac joints.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson)

“Look at the direction of growth on the radiograph: OA osteophytes project horizontally off the endplate like a claw, whereas AS syndesmophytes grow straight up and down vertically to bridge the disc and make a bamboo spine!”

Second-Order Board Clue

A 28-year-old HLA-B27 positive male with severe morning back stiffness and sacroiliac fusion exhibits thin vertical marginal syndesmophytes forming a bamboo spine—do not confuse with OA’s horizontal osteophytes or DISH’s bulky right-sided candle wax.

Thoracic ALL Ossification vs. Spinal OA and Ankylosing Spondylitis

LO 6.01 • Slides 33, 36–37
Diagnostic Hallmark
  • Patient Profile: Typically males > females, age > 50 years, usually asymptomatic and discovered incidentally on chest or spinal X-ray.
  • Flowing Candle Wax Criterion: Exuberant osteophytosis and ossification of the anterior longitudinal ligament (ALL) spanning at least 4 contiguous vertebrae predominantly in the thoracic spine, forming a “flowing” or “dripping candle wax” contour.
  • Metabolic Associations: Strongly associated with metabolic syndrome (diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, hyperuricemia, obesity, and hypertension).
Crucial Preserved Structures
  • Versus Spinal OA: OA targets the cervical/lumbar spine and collapses disc spaces; DISH targets the thoracic spine with complete preservation of disc spaces and spared facet joints.
  • Versus Ankylosing Spondylitis: Unlike AS, DISH shows no true intervertebral ankylosis and explicitly spares the sacroiliac joint and facet joints.
  • Asymmetry Detail: Thoracic ALL ossification typically drapes anteriorly and anterolaterally along contiguous vertebral bodies while leaving the intervertebral discs intact.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson)

“Remember the location contrast: OA is cervical and lumbar, while DISH is mainly thoracic! Look for 4 contiguous vertebrae with flowing candle wax of the ALL, preserved disc height, spared facets, and spared sacroiliac joints.”

Second-Order Board Clue

An obese 62-year-old man with type 2 DM and hypertension has an incidental chest X-ray showing bulky anterior bridging across 5 thoracic vertebrae with preserved disc spaces and normal sacroiliac joints—diagnose DISH (Forestier’s disease) rather than AS or OA.

Extraspinal Enthesopathy & Rare Cervical DISH Complications

LO 6.02 • Slides 33–36, 38
Extraspinal Entheseal Calcifications
  • Enthesophyte Vocabulary: An enthesophyte is a bony outgrowth (exostosis) developing specifically at an enthesis—where a tendon, ligament, or joint capsule inserts into bone—due to chronic tensile strain.
  • Classic Locations: Extraspinal entheseal calcifications occur prominently at the pelvic girdle/hip, ankle/foot (calcaneal spur/Achilles), and knee (quadriceps/patellar tendon).
  • Morphology: DISH pelvic and extraspinal enthesophytes appear well corticated with rounded edges rather than fluffy inflammatory periostitis.
Rare Cervical Involvement Risks
  • Esophageal Compression: When DISH involves the cervical spine anteriorly, large anterior cervical osteophytes can impinge on the esophagus, causing mild dysphagia.
  • Posterior Spinal Ligament Calcification: Ossification/calcification of the posterior longitudinal spinal ligament in the C-spine narrows the canal and risks cord compression.
  • Traumatic Cord Injury: C-spine posterior ligament calcification (noted as rare and mostly in Japanese populations) renders the neck rigid so hyperextension trauma can produce a fracture with spinal cord injury.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson)

“DISH isn’t always silent! Anterior cervical osteophytes can push on the esophagus causing dysphagia, and posterior C-spine ligament calcification can snap on hyperextension and injure the spinal cord.”

Second-Order Board Clue

An older man with known thoracic “flowing candle wax” ossification and well-corticated pelvic enthesophytes presents with progressive difficulty swallowing solid foods; lateral C-spine film confirms large anterior cervical osteophytes causing mechanical dysphagia.

Biomechanical Offloading, Analgesic Ladder & SNRI Therapy

LO 7.01 • Slide 33
Non-Pharmacologic Foundation
  • Load Reduction & Strength: Weight loss and structured physical therapy (quadriceps/periarticular strengthening) directly offload abnormal contact stress across weightbearing joints.
  • Symptom-Control Principle: Conservative modalities target pain and joint function because medical therapy does not regenerate lost hyaline cartilage.
  • Assistive Support: Activity modification and bracing/assistive devices support unstable or malaligned joints.
Pharmacologic Options
  • NSAIDs (Topical & Oral): Topical NSAIDs provide localized relief (especially knee/hand) with lower systemic exposure, while oral NSAIDs reduce pain and mild reactive synovitis when tolerated.
  • Duloxetine (Cymbalta): Explicitly highlighted in the lecture management slide as an effective SNRI option for chronic musculoskeletal OA pain.
  • Risk Balancing: Systemic oral NSAIDs must be weighed against GI, renal, and cardiovascular risk profiles in older OA patients.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson)

“Start with foundational weight loss, physical therapy, and topical or oral NSAIDs, and keep Duloxetine (Cymbalta) on your radar for chronic OA pain management.”

Second-Order Board Clue

An older patient with chronic knee OA who has comorbid chronic kidney disease or GI ulcer history should avoid chronic systemic oral NSAIDs in favor of weight loss, physical therapy, topical NSAIDs, or Duloxetine (Cymbalta).

Corticosteroid Injections, Viscosupplementation Caveat & Total Joint Arthroplasty

LO 7.02 • Slides 18, 33
Intra-Articular Interventions
  • Intra-Articular Corticosteroids: Provide targeted anti-inflammatory relief for painful OA flares and reactive synovitis when oral therapy is insufficient or contraindicated.
  • Viscosupplementation (Hyaluronic Acid?): Highlighted in the lecture slide with a deliberate question mark (“Viscosupplementation hyaluronic acid?”) reflecting mixed/controversial clinical utility compared with standard care.
  • Rule Out Infection First: Never inject a joint without confirming a cool, non-septic effusion.
Definitive Surgical Management
  • Arthroplasty Indication: Surgery (total knee or total hip arthroplasty) is reserved for end-stage, bone-on-bone OA with refractory pain and functional disability despite conservative care.
  • Pre-Surgical Workup: Confirmed via weightbearing radiographs showing severe joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis, osteophytes, or late varus/valgus deformity—without routine MRI.
  • Restorative Outcome: Replaces damaged articular surfaces and corrects mechanical axis alignment.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson)

“Notice the question mark next to ‘Viscosupplementation hyaluronic acid?’ on the management slide—its benefit is debated! When conservative meds, Duloxetine, and injections fail in end-stage OA, surgery is definitive.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A 71-year-old with severe varus knee OA refractory to weight loss, PT, NSAIDs, Duloxetine, and intra-articular corticosteroids requires weightbearing radiographs and referral for surgical arthroplasty—not a diagnostic knee MRI.

Secondary OA		 Metabolic/systemic dysregulation paired with mechanical overload driving accelerated matrix wear Weightbearing knees/hips and systemic overlap with DISH Classic OA JSN & osteophytes in patients with DM, dyslipidemia, HTN, obesity Listed explicitly as a secondary OA phenotype distinct from idiopathic wear Weight loss serves both metabolic control and biomechanical joint offloading
Rheumatoid Arthritis
Autoimmune		 Systemic autoimmune synovial pannus inflammation eroding bone at bare areas Symmetric MCPs, PIPs, wrists, feet; spares DIP; affects lungs & eyes Marginal erosions, periarticular osteopenia, positive RF / anti-CCP, WBC 2k–50k Swan neck and ulnar drift; “seronegative” means lacking RF and anti-CCP Prolonged > 60 min morning stiffness with warm, boggy symmetric MCP swelling
Erosive Hand OA
Aggressive Subset		 Aggressive inflammatory OA subset with genetic predisposition; interplay with CPPD / uric acid DIP and PIP joints in peri/menopausal women Central “gull-wing” erosions, saw-tooth deformity, rapid progression to ankylosis Exception to “OA has no erosions”—central erosions vs. RA’s marginal erosions Perimenopausal woman with severe DIP pain and fused IP joints on radiograph
Cervical & Lumbar Spondylosis
Spinal OA		 Degeneration of facet joints and intervertebral discs (DDD) with horizontal endplate spurs Cervical and lumbar spine (unlike thoracic DISH) Loss of disc height, horizontal osteophytes, loss of cervical lordosis → kyphosis Protracted head posture & neuroforaminal encroachment Radicular arm/leg pain with narrowed disc space and horizontal traction claws
DISH (Forestier’s Disease)
Hyperostosis		 Non-inflammatory ossification of anterior longitudinal ligament & chronic entheseal tensile strain Mainly thoracic spine + extraspinal pelvis, hip, ankle/foot, knee; rare C-spine Flowing candle wax across ≥ 4 vertebrae; preserved disc height; spared facets & SI; rounded enthesophytes Males > 50, usually asymptomatic; anterior C-spine causes dysphagia; posterior causes cord injury Metabolic syndrome patient with thoracic bridging and intact SI joints/discs
Ankylosing Spondylitis
Seronegative		 Chronic HLA-B27 axial spondyloarthritis ossifying outer annulus fibers vertically Sacroiliac joints and entire spine (ascending stiffness) Vertical bridging marginal syndesmophytes → bamboo spine; SI joint fusion; RF/anti-CCP negative Syndesmophytes grow vertically (AS) vs. osteophytes horizontally (OA) Young adult with inflammatory back pain, SI fusion, and seronegative labs
