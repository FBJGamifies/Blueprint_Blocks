Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip (DDH) & Pediatric Hip Pathology

Clinical Hallmark 80% female; breech, oligohydramnios, multiple gestation
Diagnostic Identifier Barlow (dislocates) & Ortolani (relocates with clunk)

DDH Pathophysiology, Intrauterine Risk & Physical Exam Maneuvers

  • Spectrum: Partial subluxation to complete low or high dislocation of the femoral head out of the acetabulum. Incidence is 1:1000 in Caucasian infants (less common in African American infants), and 80% occur in females.
  • Mechanical Risks: Cramped intrauterine packing — breech presentation, oligohydramnios, multiple gestation — limits fetal hip extension/abduction. Associated with torticollis and metatarsus adductus.
  • Physical Exam: Barlow maneuver adducts hip and presses posteriorly to provoke dislocation; Ortolani maneuver abducts and lifts to relocate the femoral head with a palpable "clunk" (not a benign soft-tissue click).
  • Galeazzi Sign & Folds: Supine with knees/hips flexed 90°; asymmetric lower knee height indicates posterior hip dislocation (more accurate in older infants than Barlow/Ortolani), alongside asymmetric gluteal/thigh skin folds.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

Barlow and Ortolani carry a high false-positive rate because physiologic hip subluxation/laxity in early infancy often resolves spontaneously without sequelae. Leg-length discrepancy at the flexed knee (Galeazzi) is more reliable once dislocated.

Second-Order Board Clues

Untreated DDH progresses to acetabular fixation/bony remodeling, flexion contractures, abnormal waddling gait, decreased mobility, and premature adult osteoarthritis.

Imaging & Treatment
Anatomic Driver Cartilaginous femoral head ossifies ~4–6 months
Clinical Hallmark Alpha angle < 60° (acetabular dysplasia) on Graf US
Diagnostic Identifier Superolateral displacement outside inferomedial quadrant + broken Shenton
Key Pitfall / Negative Clue Do NOT US before 6 wks (false +) or after 4 mos (ossification)
First-Line Regimen Pavlik harness (<6 mo, 90% success) → Spica (6–18 mo) → Open (>18 mo)

Age-Stratified Imaging Geometry & Stepped DDH Management

  • Ultrasound Window (6 weeks to 4 months): Before 6 weeks, maternal relaxin/physiologic laxity causes excessive false positives. After 6 weeks, Graf ultrasound evaluates the bony roof alpha angle (normal ≥ 60°) and cartilaginous roof beta angle (normal < 55°).
  • Radiographic Window (> 4 months): Ossification of the femoral head permits bilateral AP and frog-leg X-rays. Map the horizontal Hilgenreiner line (through triradiate cartilages) and perpendicular Perkin line (lateral acetabular edge): the femoral head ossific nucleus should sit in the inferomedial quadrant.
  • Dysplasia Signs: Superolateral femoral head migration, disrupted Shenton line (broken arc between femoral neck and obturator foramen), and elevated acetabular index (> 30° in neonates).
  • Stepped Care: < 6 months: Pavlik harness (flexes/abducts hips to center head into acetabulum; ~90% success; permits diaper care). 6–18 months: Closed reduction under anesthesia + hip spica cast. > 18 months or failed harness: Open surgical reduction/osteotomy.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

Memorize the exact age cutoffs: <6 weeks = avoid US (false positives); 6 weeks to 4 months = hip ultrasound; >4 months = AP and frog-leg pelvic radiographs.

Second-Order Board Clues

In a 7-month-old girl with delayed diagnosis, Pavlik harness fails due to soft-tissue contractures; expect closed reduction + spica casting rather than bracing.

LCPD vs. SCFE
Anatomic Driver LCPD: AVN of femoral head; SCFE: SH-I slip through proximal physis
Clinical Hallmark LCPD: 4–10 y boy + Trendelenburg gait; SCFE: obese adolescent + external rotation
Diagnostic Identifier Frog-leg lateral hip X-ray (crescent sign / ice cream falling off cone)
Key Pitfall / Negative Clue Hip pathology frequently presents as referred medial knee pain
First-Line Regimen LCPD: containment/PT; SCFE: non-weight-bearing + urgent screw pinning

Age-Based Hip Differential: Legg-Calvé-Perthes Disease vs. SCFE

  • Legg-Calvé-Perthes Disease (LCPD): Idiopathic interruption of blood flow to the femoral head/neck resulting in avascular necrosis (AVN). Typically affects children aged 4–10 years (often shorter stature or delayed skeletal maturation).
  • LCPD Presentation & Sequelae: Insidious limp, Trendelenburg gait (abductor weakness), limited internal rotation/abduction, and risk of permanent deformity of the femoral head and acetabulum.
  • Slipped Capital Femoral Epiphysis (SCFE): Classic Salter-Harris Type I slip across the proximal femoral growth plate in peripubertal/obese adolescents; presents with groin/knee pain and obligatory external rotation during hip flexion.
  • Diagnostic & Urgent Rule: Bilateral AP and frog-leg radiographs differentiate LCPD (flattened/fragmented epiphysis) from SCFE (epiphysis displaced posteriorly/inferiorly — "ice cream slipping off cone"). SCFE requires immediate non-weight-bearing and surgical pinning.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

Age stratifies pediatric hip pain: DDH (<1 yr), LCPD (4–10 yrs, AVN + Trendelenburg gait + short/delayed bone age), and SCFE (10–15 yrs, Salter-Harris I physeal slip).

Second-Order Board Clues

Obturator nerve sensory distribution causes pediatric hip disorders (LCPD, SCFE) to masquerade as isolated knee pain — always examine hip range of motion in a limping child.

Physiologic Angular Transitions vs. Pathologic Blount Disease

  • Normal Physiologic Curve: Infants show symmetric genu varum (0–2 years), transition to neutral by ~2 years, develop physiologic genu valgum peaking at 3–4 years, and settle into normal adult mild valgus by ~7 years.
  • Blount Disease (Infantile Tibia Vara): Pathologic abnormality where the medial aspect of the proximal tibial epiphysis/physis is shorter due to compressive growth inhibition, producing progressive bowed legs (varus) and intoeing between 1–3 years of age (usually painless).
  • Radiographic Confirmation: Standing AP lower-extremity X-rays demonstrate medial proximal tibial metaphyseal beaking/sloping and epiphyseal wedging (staged by Langenskiöld stages I–VI).
  • Treatment Stratification: < 3 years (early stages): Knee-ankle-foot bracing lowers risk of degenerative joint disease and permanent deformity. ≥ 3 years, severe, or bracing failure: Corrective proximal tibial osteotomy.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

Distinguish physiologic bowing (<2 years, symmetric, painless, resolves spontaneously) from Blount disease (persists/worsens after age 2–3, sharp proximal tibial angulation on X-ray; brace <3 yrs, osteotomy if older/unresponsive).

Second-Order Board Clues

A 3.5-year-old child with symmetric knock-knees (genu valgum) and normal height/exam needs parental reassurance only — surgery or bracing is not indicated unless extreme/asymmetric.

Pediatric In-Toeing
Anatomic Driver Infant: forefoot; Toddler: tibia; Child 3–8y: femoral neck
Clinical Hallmark W-sitting + inward-pointing patellae (femoral anteversion)
Diagnostic Identifier Normal hindfoot + medial forefoot (MA); Craig test (hip)
Key Pitfall / Negative Clue Screen MA infants for DDH & torticollis (cramped uterus)
First-Line Regimen Observation/reassurance (resolves by ~8y); stretch/cast only rigid MA

Anatomic Localization of In-Toeing: Metatarsus Adductus, Tibial Torsion & Anteversion

  • Metatarsus Adductus (< 1 year): Most common infant foot deformity (50% bilateral). Caused by cramped intrauterine packaging (oligohydramnios; associated with DDH and torticollis). Plantar view shows medial deviation of the forefoot with a normal hindfoot. Flexible (passively stretches past neutral) resolves spontaneously; rigid requires stretching/serial casting.
  • Internal (Medial) Tibial Torsion (1–3 years): Knees/femurs point straight forward while tibiae twist inward; often paired with tibia varus (bowed legs). Self-corrects without bracing or special shoes.
  • Increased Femoral Anteversion (3–8 years): Femoral neck rotates anteriorly at the hip, causing both knees ("kissing patellae") and feet to turn inward. Classic association with W-sitting and joint hypermobility (e.g., Ehlers-Danlos); assessed via Craig test (prone hip rotation).
  • Natural History: Both internal tibial torsion and femoral anteversion typically self-correct by approximately 8 years of age; parental education and avoiding unnecessary orthotics are key.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

Look at where the patellae point: patellae forward + feet inward = internal tibial torsion; patellae inward + W-sitting = femoral anteversion; normal hindfoot + curved forefoot = metatarsus adductus.

Second-Order Board Clues

An infant with metatarsus adductus should trigger an explicit hip exam (Ortolani/Barlow) and neck check (SCM tightness) because all three share the "packed uterus" risk profile.

Clubfoot (CAVE)
Anatomic Driver Fixed congenital talocalcaneonavicular deformity (idiopathic/familial)
Clinical Hallmark CAVE: Cavus, Adductus, Varus, Equinus
Diagnostic Identifier Rigid hindfoot equinovarus + midfoot adductus at birth
Key Pitfall / Negative Clue Do NOT confuse with metatarsus adductus (normal hindfoot)
First-Line Regimen Urgent ortho referral → Ponseti serial casting + Achilles tenotomy

Congenital Talipes Equinovarus (Clubfoot): CAVE Deformity & Ponseti Method

  • Deformity Components (CAVE): Midfoot Cavus (high medial arch), forefoot Adductus, hindfoot Varus (heel inverted), and hindfoot Equinus (plantarflexed calcaneus).
  • Etiology & Associations: Generally idiopathic with strong familial tendency (even in distant relatives); associated with DDH and neuromuscular/connective disorders such as arthrogryposis.
  • Clinical Recognition & Prognosis: Fixed deformity recognized at birth that cannot be passively corrected to neutral. Left untreated, the child walks on the lateral border or dorsum of the foot, causing a lifelong limp.
  • Ponseti Method: Early pediatric orthopedic referral for gradual serial manipulation and long-leg casting (correcting Cavus → Adductus → Varus → Equinus, often requiring percutaneous Achilles tenotomy followed by abduction bracing).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

Clubfoot affects BOTH the forefoot (adductus/cavus) and hindfoot (equinovarus) and is rigid at birth, whereas metatarsus adductus has a normal hindfoot.

Second-Order Board Clues

Always correct the C-A-V components before equinus in Ponseti casting; attempting to dorsiflex the equinus heel early creates a "rocker-bottom" foot.

Pediatric Physeal Mechanics & Fracture Classification

Lecturer Rule: Physeal tenderness + normal X-ray = Salter-Harris I until proven otherwise

Salter-Harris Physis Visualizer & Fracture Triage Workbench

Physis & SALTR I–V
Anatomic Driver Hypertrophic zone (weakest) & provisional calcification
Clinical Hallmark Type II most common (75%); Type I occult X-ray + point tenderness
Diagnostic Identifier SALTR mnemonic (draw distal bone growing downward)
Key Pitfall / Negative Clue Types III & IV enter joint space → growth arrest & OA
First-Line Regimen I/II: immobilize + follow-up; III/IV: immediate ortho consult

Growth Plate Histology & Salter-Harris Classification (Types I–V)

  • Physeal Architecture: Ordered from epiphysis to metaphysis: Reserve zone (stem cells) → Proliferative zone (chondrocyte columns) → Hypertrophic zone (mechanically weakest, swollen chondrocytes with scant matrix; most fractures shear here) → Zone of provisional calcification.
  • Type I (Slipped, ~5%) & Type II (Above, ~75%): Type I passes solely through the physis (often normal X-ray; SCFE is a displaced example). Type II extends above into the metaphysis (Thurston-Holland fragment). Both have excellent prognosis with immobilization and ortho follow-up.
  • Type III (Lower, ~10%) & Type IV (Through): Type III crosses physis and epiphysis into the joint space; Type IV traverses metaphysis, physis, and epiphysis. Both risk premature growth plate fusion and osteoarthritis → require immediate orthopedic consultation.
  • Type V (Rammed): Axial crush injury across the physis; often subtle initially but carries the highest risk of premature physeal arrest and limb-length discrepancy.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

Pain directly over an open growth plate after trauma with a normal X-ray is treated as a Salter-Harris Type I fracture until proven otherwise — immobilize rather than dismissing as a simple sprain.

Second-Order Board Clues

Using SALTR requires orienting the bone growing downward so "Above" = metaphysis (Type II) and "Lower" = epiphysis/articular surface (Type III).

Incomplete & Special Fx
Anatomic Driver Thick periosteum + porous pediatric cortex bends before breaking
Clinical Hallmark FOOSH elbow pain (supracondylar) or jumper tibial tubercle pain
Diagnostic Identifier Anterior humeral line misses capitellum / fat pad sign
Key Pitfall / Negative Clue Supracondylar neurovascular compromise (brachial/AIN/radial)
First-Line Regimen Supracondylar: immediate ortho/OR; Osgood-Schlatter: rest/NSAIDs

Incomplete Fractures, Supracondylar Humerus & Apophyseal Avulsions

  • Incomplete Cortical Patterns: Torus (buckle) fracture (compressive cortical bulge, stable, splinted), Greenstick fracture (tension-side cortex fractures while opposite cortex bends intact), and Plastic bowing (microfractures without discrete cortical line).
  • Supracondylar Distal Humerus Fracture: FOOSH injury where the anterior humeral line fails to intersect the middle third of the capitellum (often intersecting anteriorly due to posterior displacement). Danger to arm neurovascular supply → immediate orthopedic consult and common surgical pinning.
  • Apophyseal Avulsion Fractures: Repetitive muscular traction avulses secondary ossification centers (apophyses): ASIS (sartorius in runners/swimmers) and Tibial Tubercle = Osgood-Schlatter disease (patellar tendon traction in jumpers/runners).
  • Osgood-Schlatter Management: Self-limiting traction apophysitis diagnosed clinically (tender, prominent tibial tubercle); managed conservatively with activity modification, ice, NSAIDs, and quadriceps/hamstring stretching.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

On a lateral elbow X-ray, draw the anterior humeral line down the anterior cortex: if it misses or clips only the front edge of the capitellum, suspect a displaced supracondylar fracture and check distal pulses/nerves immediately.

Second-Order Board Clues

Because Osgood-Schlatter involves an apophysis (traction center) rather than a longitudinal weight-bearing epiphysis, conservative therapy suffices and growth arrest is not typical.

NAT Fracture Red Flags
Anatomic Driver Shearing traction / squeezing forces across infant metaphyses & ribs
Clinical Hallmark Fractures in non-ambulatory infant or inconsistent history
Diagnostic Identifier Metaphyseal corner (bucket-handle) & posterior rib fractures
Key Pitfall / Negative Clue Multiple fractures in different healing stages + sternal/scapular fx
First-Line Regimen Skeletal survey (<2y) + mandatory child protective reporting

Non-Accidental Trauma (NAT) High-Specificity Fracture Patterns

  • High-Specificity Skeletal Lesions: Classic metaphyseal lesions (corner / bucket-handle fractures) caused by violent yanking/torsion, posterior rib fractures (from anteroposterior chest squeezing), and sternal, scapular, or spinous process fractures.
  • Temporal & Pattern Clues: Multiple fractures in different stages of healing (acute lucency alongside subperiosteal callus), complex/non-linear skull fractures, and any long-bone fracture (e.g., spiral femur/humerus) in a non-ambulatory infant.
  • Clinical Discrepancies: Mechanism incompatible with developmental stage ("3-month-old rolled off couch"), delayed presentation, or patterned bruising/burns on torso/ears/neck (TEN-4-FACESp).
  • Diagnostic Protocol: Obtain a full radiographic skeletal survey in children < 2 years, document injuries precisely, rule out metabolic/collagen mimics (e.g., OI), and trigger child protection evaluation.
High-Risk Alert

Posterior rib fractures and metaphyseal bucket-handle fractures have near-pathognomonic specificity for non-accidental trauma in infants and rarely occur from routine CPR or minor falls.

Second-Order Board Clues

A spiral tibial fracture in a newly walking toddler ("toddler fracture") can be accidental, whereas a spiral femur or humerus fracture in a 4-month-old demands an immediate NAT workup.

OI Genetics & Subtypes
Anatomic Driver COL1A1 / COL1A2 mutations (defective Type I collagen)
Clinical Hallmark Blue sclerae, hearing loss, dentinogenesis imperfecta (IB, III, IVB)

OI Molecular Pathophysiology (COL1A1/COL1A2) & Subtype Spectrum

  • Genetic Basis: Mostly autosomal dominant or de novo mutations in COL1A1 or COL1A2 encoding Type I collagen (rare subtypes autosomal recessive). Decreased trabeculae and reduced cortical thickness cause fractures from baseline handling despite increased osteoblast/osteoclast turnover.
  • Type I (Mild, Non-deforming): Normal stature, blue sclerae, conductive/sensorineural hearing loss; subdivided into IA (no dentinogenesis imperfecta) and IB (with dentinogenesis imperfecta — fragile bluish-grayish teeth).
  • Type II (Perinatal Lethal): Severe collagen disruption causing crumpled/accordion long bones, beaded ribs, and fatal respiratory insufficiency in utero or early infancy.
  • Type III & Type IV: Type III is progressively deforming with severe scoliosis and known dentinogenesis imperfecta; Type IV causes moderate fragility and baseline long-bone deformities (IVA without DI; IVB with DI).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

Types I and IV split into A (no dentinogenesis imperfecta) and B (with dentinogenesis imperfecta), whereas Type III characteristically includes dentinogenesis imperfecta and Type II is typically perinatal lethal.

Second-Order Board Clues

Osteogenesis imperfecta fractures heal at a normal rate because osteoblast/osteoclast remodeling machinery is active; bisphosphonates target this elevated turnover.

OI Care & NAT Logic
Anatomic Driver High bone turnover + defective Type I collagen matrix
Clinical Hallmark Hypercalciuria (without renal failure) + Wormian skull bones
Diagnostic Identifier DNA testing ordered in abuse cases to rule out defense claim
Key Pitfall / Negative Clue Calcium/Vit D supplementation needed alongside bisphosphonates
First-Line Regimen Bisphosphonates (pamidronate) + Vit D/Ca + genetic counseling

OI Diagnostics, Bisphosphonate Therapy & Medicolegal NAT Differentiation

  • Radiologic & Metabolic Findings: Diffuse osteopenia, thin cortices, Wormian bones (intrasutural bones) on skull radiographs, and possible hypercalciuria (typically without renal failure).
  • Pharmacologic Therapy: Intravenous bisphosphonates (e.g., pamidronate) inhibit osteoclast-mediated resorption, increasing cortical bone density, lowering fracture frequency, and reducing chronic bone pain.
  • Supportive & Multidisciplinary Care: Ensure adequate Vitamin D and calcium to support accelerated bone turnover during injury repair, alongside genetic counseling and routine pediatric orthopedic follow-up.
  • Medicolegal Distinction from NAT: Clinicians differentiate OI from child abuse using family history, blue sclerae, dentinogenesis imperfecta, Wormian bones, and absence of patterned cutaneous trauma; conversely, in clear child abuse cases with multiple fractures, DNA testing for OI may be obtained preemptively so defense counsel cannot claim undiagnosed OI in court.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

Pamidronate (bisphosphonate) is the cornerstone medical therapy to raise bone mineral density and cut fracture pain, paired with Vitamin D/calcium and pediatric orthopedics co-management.

Second-Order Board Clues

Finding multiple fractures in different stages of healing does NOT automatically prove child abuse if osteopenia, Wormian bones, and blue sclerae are present — confirm with COL1A1/COL1A2 DNA testing.

Pediatric Scoliosis & Spinal Deformities

Lecturer Rule: Scoliometer ≥ 7° ≠ Cobb angle; brace 25–40° (Risser 0–2), fuse > 45–50°

Scoliosis Subtypes (Infantile, Juvenile, AIS) & Pulmonary / Crankshaft Risks

  • Structural Definition: Abnormal lateral curvature of the spine accompanied by vertebral rotation (often with thoracic hyperkyphosis). Idiopathic scoliosis is generally painless; severe back pain or left thoracic curve warrants red-flag workup.
  • Age-Based Idiopathic Subtypes: Infantile (< 3 years) shows a high rate of spontaneous resolution; Juvenile (4–10 years) is more progressive than AIS and risks the crankshaft phenomenon if fused posteriorly alone; Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis (AIS, 10–18 years) is the most common form, occurring during the pubertal growth spurt with marked female predilection for progression.
  • Early-Onset Cardiopulmonary Threat: Normal alveoli undergo a 10-fold increase from infancy to age 4 (continuing to age 8). Significant thoracic scoliosis before age 5 restricts thoracic volume and risks cardiopulmonary failure / disabling dyspnea.
  • Secondary Causes: Congenital (hemivertebrae, spina bifida, VACTERL syndrome) and Neuromuscular (cerebral palsy, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, neurofibroma/astrocytoma, osteoid osteoma).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

Before age 5, thoracic scoliosis is a pulmonary emergency over time because alveolar multiplication is still occurring — restricting lung space leads to cor pulmonale and respiratory failure.

Second-Order Board Clues

In juvenile scoliosis requiring surgical control, combine anterior + posterior fusion to prevent the "crankshaft phenomenon" (continued anterior growth twisting around a posterior tether).

Clinical Hallmark Adam's forward bend test + scoliometer ≥ 5–7°

Screening (Adam's Test), Cobb Angle Measurement & Risser Skeletal Maturity

  • Clinical Screening: Adam's Forward Bending Test reveals thoracic or lumbar rib-hump asymmetry caused by vertebral rotation. A scoliometer angle of trunk rotation ≥ 7° (threshold > 5–7°) indicates standing PA/lateral spine radiographs and/or referral.
  • Radiographic Cobb Angle: Measured on standardized standing PA spine X-rays between lines drawn along the superior endplate of the uppermost tilted vertebra and inferior endplate of the lowermost tilted vertebra (inter-rater error ~2°).
  • Risser Staging (Iliac Crest Apophysis): Grade 0 = no ossification; 1 = ≤ 25% (occurs after peak height velocity); 2 = 26–50%; 3 = 51–75%; 4 = > 76%; 5 = complete bony fusion.
  • Additional Maturity Indices: Triradiate cartilage (open during female peak height velocity before Risser 1), Tanner-Whitehouse hand/wrist epiphyses, and elbow ossification centers (Sauvegrain 27-point scale or simpler olecranon method).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

When referring to orthopedics, explicitly state whether degrees are Scoliometer (≥7°) or Cobb angle — they measure different phenomena and represent vastly different progression risks.

Second-Order Board Clues

Incidental mild scoliosis < 10° on a chest radiograph does NOT require orthopedic referral; curves ≥ 10° define true scoliosis.

Treatment Thresholds
Anatomic Driver Curve progression accelerates during open-physis growth spurt
Clinical Hallmark Progression ≥ 5° over 3–8 mos triggers bracing
Diagnostic Identifier Refer: ≥ 20° premenarchal/boy 12–14, ≥ 30° any pt, or +5° shift
Key Pitfall / Negative Clue Bracing HALTS progression — it does NOT straighten existing curve
First-Line Regimen <20–25° observe; 25–40° (Risser 0–2) TLSO brace; >45–50° fusion

Curve-Based Management Tree: Observation, TLSO Bracing & Spinal Fusion

  • Observation (< 20–25°): Serial clinical exams and standing PA radiographs every 4–6 months. Curves 20–29° at Risser 0–2 are followed closely and braced immediately if progression ≥ 5° occurs within 3–6 months.
  • Bracing (25–40° in Skeletally Immature Risser 0–2): TLSO / Boston brace worn 16–23 hours/day prevents curve progression and reduces surgical need (does not reverse baseline angle). Monitor every 6–8 months with out-of-brace radiographs; wean at Risser 4–5 / 2 years post-menarche (girls) or Risser 5 (boys).
  • Surgical Fusion (> 45–50°): Posterior spinal fusion with instrumentation to prevent severe cardiopulmonary compromise (>40°), chronic pain, progressive spinal deformity, and early mortality.
  • Referral Criteria: Scoliometer ≥ 7° (without Cobb), Cobb 20–29° in a premenarchal girl or boy age 12–14, Cobb ≥ 30° in any patient, or progression > 5° in any patient.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

Bracing only works while growth remains (Risser 0–2) and is evaluated via out-of-brace X-rays every 6–8 months. Progression ≥ 5° despite bracing indicates poor brace efficacy and likely surgical need.

Second-Order Board Clues

A 15-year-old girl 2.5 years post-menarche at Risser 5 with a 32° curve is skeletally mature — bracing is no longer indicated; observe instead.

Pediatric Osteopathic Manipulative Medicine (OMM) in Orthopaedics

Lecturer Rule: NEVER apply HVLA across open growth plates or in OI; check CT if plagiocephaly stalls

Pediatric OMT Principles, Cranial Sacral Mechanism & Positional Plagiocephaly

  • Pediatric Responsiveness: Children rarely have muscle fibrosis, so tissues release rapidly and post-OMT soreness is rare. Keep sessions simple and fun, respect autonomy/self-determination, switch techniques if uncomfortable, and teach parents home care (suboccipital release, ear pull).
  • Neonate / Infant Modalities: Favor Osteopathy in the Cranial Field (OCF) (including occipital condylar decompression for birth trauma, feeding issues, ankyloglossia, and colic), Balanced Ligamentous Tension (BLT), and Balanced Membranous Tension (BMT) along developing growth plates (e.g., internal tibial torsion).
  • Positional Plagiocephaly & Torticollis: Tight left sternocleidomastoid (SCM) pulls the head sidebent left, rotated right, restricting left visual tracking, feeding, and cortical/motor development — and driving secondary spinal scoliosis. Treat alongside standard PT/stretching with OCF and myofascial/BLT release.
  • Craniosynostosis Rule-Out: If cranial asymmetry fails to improve after 2–3 OMT treatments, obtain a head CT to assess suture patency and rule out premature suture fusion (craniosynostosis, NOT treated by OMT).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

Structure and function are interrelated: a misshapen calvarium and unidirectional neck rotation impair visual-motor tracking, feeding, and spinal alignment. OMT accelerates standard PT recovery, but 2–3 visits without change mandates CT for synostosis.

Scoliosis Somatic Balancing, Lower-Limb Gait OMM & Absolute Contraindications

  • Older Child / Adolescent Techniques: Beyond OCF/BLT/BMT, utilize myofascial release (MFR), soft tissue, counterstrain, articulatory, and lymphatic techniques for constipation, chronic otitis media, headaches/migraines, sprains, and gait optimization.
  • Osteopathic Structural Exam in Scoliosis: Address four modifiable contributors alongside PT/stretches (especially Cobb < 20°): poor posture (education), paraspinal muscle spasms (OMT/MFR/BLT), short leg syndrome (heel lift), and hemi-pelvis syndrome (butt lift).
  • Pelvic Torsion & Gait: Balancing pelvic innominate/sacral mechanics and lower-extremity myofascial tensions optimizes symmetric weight-bearing during childhood growth spurts.
  • Strict Absolute Contraindications: NEVER apply High-Velocity Low-Amplitude (HVLA) across open growth plates/physes or in patients with severe osteopenia or Osteogenesis Imperfecta (OI); avoid manipulative force over acute fractures, supracondylar instability, or craniosynostosis.
Safety Contraindication

HVLA across open pediatric growth plates risks iatrogenic Salter-Harris physeal injury, and any forceful thrust in Osteogenesis Imperfecta can cause acute fractures — stick strictly to gentle indirect/MFR/BLT approaches when safe.

Second-Order Board Clues

Distinguish structural short leg syndrome (treated with a heel lift while standing) from a small hemi-pelvis (treated with a butt/ischial lift while seated).
