Chronologic Definitions & Interdependent Developmental Domains

LO 1.01 • Slides 4–6, 13
Stage & Gestational Taxonomy
  • Neonate vs. Infant: Neonate spans 0–28 days of life, while infant spans 28 days to 1 year; clinically, "preterm neonate" and "preterm infant" are used interchangeably.
  • Gestational Age Thresholds: Preterm is defined as < 37 weeks, full term as 37–41 weeks, and post term as > 41 weeks.
  • Childhood & Adolescence: Child (1–12 yr) splits into Toddler (1–3 yr), Preschool (4–6 yr), and Middle Childhood (7–12 yr); Adolescent spans 13–18 years, while full human brain development continues into the early 20s.
Four Interdependent Milestone Pillars
  • Core 4 Categories: Every CDC neurodevelopmental checkpoint (2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 24 mo, 4 yr, 5 yr) requires progress in Motor (gross/fine), Cognitive, Social/Emotional, and Language/Communication.
  • Domino Delay Effect: Because these domains are tightly interconnected, a delay in one pillar (e.g., restricted neck motion or hearing loss) frequently precipitates delays in all other domains.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

"If you cut one of those four boxes out, an individual with a delay in one area may develop delays in some or all of the others—and remember, the human brain is not fully developed until our early 20s."

Second-Order Board Clue

A child with untreated unilateral restriction or sensory impairment fails to track caregivers and subsequently misses social, visual, motor, and speech milestones simultaneously.

LO 1.02 • Primitive Reflexes

Birth-to-2-Month Primitive Reflexes & ATNR/STNR Integration

Neural Origin Brainstem & spinal cord (involuntary)
ATNR (Fencer's) Action Face arm extends; skull arm flexes
ATNR Developmental Block Prevents hands meeting at midline
STNR Emergence Neck ext → arms extend, legs flex

Newborn Reflex Inventory & Cortical Integration Sequence

LO 1.02 • Slides 11–12 & Transcript 7:45–13:16
Seven Newborn Assessment Reflexes (0–2 Mo)
  • Automatic Subcortical Responses: Controlled by the brainstem and spinal cord: Stepping, Moro (Startle), Rooting, Suck/Swallow, Palmar/Plantar Grasp, Babinski, and ATNR.
  • ATNR (Asymmetric Tonic Neck Reflex): Turning the infant's head causes the face-side arm to extend and the occiput/skull-side arm to flex ("fencer's reflex").
  • Midline Cortex Gate: While ATNR dominates (0–2 mo), the infant cannot bring hands together in the midline, delaying midline visual-motor integration until ATNR integrates.
STNR & Early Milestone Red Flags (2–4 Mo)
  • STNR Progression: Once ATNR integrates into the nervous system, STNR (Symmetric Tonic Neck Reflex) emerges: cervical extension triggers arm extension and leg flexion, allowing the infant to prop on elbows and reach.
  • 2–4 Month Checkpoints: By 2 mo, infants socially smile, coo, turn toward sounds, and meet hands at midline; by 3 mo they hold objects and smile at people; by 4 mo they reach for toys, copy facial expressions, and explore with their mouth.
  • Fontanelle & Spatial Red Flags: Failure to turn the head toward someone entering the room suggests movement or hearing issues; the anterior fontanelle should close by 18 months; poor spatial judgment or lacking "me vs. you" social boundaries later signals developmental delay.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

"While the fencer's reflex (ATNR) is active in 0–2 months, the infant literally cannot meet their hands in the midline—so they aren't developing their midline visual cortex yet until ATNR integrates and STNR comes in."

Second-Order Board Clue

A 4-month-old who cannot prop on elbows or bring hands to midline to mouth toys exhibits persistent ATNR blocking transition to STNR and midline cortical integration.

LO 1.03 • 2–4 Mo & Childhood Red Flags

Early Social/Motor Milestones & Later Behavioral Red Flags

2-Month Milestone Social smile, cooing, turns to sound
3-Month Orientation Holds objects & turns head to room entry
4-Month Exploration Mouthing toys, reaching, copying faces
Toddler / Preschool Red Flags No fantasy play, no "me vs. you", poor spatial judgment

2–4 Month Cortical Emergence & Childhood Developmental Red Flags

LO 1.03 • Slides 10–13 & Transcript 10:33–13:16, 43:10–52:27
2- to 4-Month Interactive Progression
  • 2 Months (Caretaker Reward): Begins social smiling, cooing/gurgling, calming briefly, turning purposefully toward sounds, bringing hands to midline, and fussing when bored (needing cognitive engagement).
  • 3 Months: Follows objects, smiles at voices and people, begins holding objects, and—even before full head control—turns head when someone walks into the room (absence flags movement or hearing issues).
  • 4 Months: Responds to affection, copies facial expressions, leans on one arm while reaching with the other, babbles/cries in distinct ways, and explores objects with the mouth.
Childhood Spatial, Play & Social Red Flags
  • Spatial Judgment Deficit: Difficulty judging space or navigating physical obstacles indicates a spatial judgment developmental concern.
  • Fantasy Play & Daily Resistance: Lack of fantasy play or persistent resistance to dressing, sleeping, and toilet use can signal irritability or developmental delay that destabilizes multiple domains.
  • Self-Other Boundary: Failure to distinguish "me versus you" indicates unmet social-emotional developmental milestones.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

"By three months they can't support their head yet, but if you walk into the room they should be turning their head—if not, suspect a movement or hearing issue. By four months they really need to be exploring with their mouth."

Second-Order Board Clue

A 3-month-old who fails to turn toward a caregiver entering the room warrants evaluation for hearing or motor impairment, while absence of fantasy play or "me vs. you" later flags social-cognitive delay.

Selecting & Measuring Anthropometric Curves Across Childhood

LO 2.01 • Slides 7–8 & Transcript 3:55–5:52
Growth Charts vs. CDC BMI Curves
  • 0–2 Year Growth Standards: Sex-specific pink (female) and blue (male) percentile curves track head circumference (top curve), weight, and length/height, outputting percentiles (e.g., 50th percentile) at well-child visits.
  • 2–19 Year BMI Curves: CDC BMI percentile curves apply to ages 2 to 19 years and are increasingly used clinically to monitor progressive adolescent obesity.
Bedside Measurement Pearls
  • Infant Length Measurement: Because infants move continuously, nurses place a mark on the exam paper at the head and foot and measure the distance with a tape measure.
  • Head Circumference Tracking: Plotted consistently on the upper curve to monitor cranial expansion and detect microcephaly or macrocephaly relative to suture patency and brain growth.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

"Growth charts (length, weight, head circumference) cover birth to 2 years, while CDC BMI percentile curves kick in from 2 to 19 years—especially important with progressive obesity in adolescents."

Second-Order Board Clue

When asked which curve assesses adiposity in an 8-year-old or 15-year-old, select CDC BMI percentile curves (2–19 years) rather than infant head circumference charts.

LO 2.02 • Weight & Length Rules

Predictable Infant Weight & Length Velocity Benchmarks

Physiologic Weight Loss Up to 10% loss; regain by 2 weeks
2–4 Mo Daily Gain 1–2 oz (30–60 g) per day
Weight Multipliers 2× by 4–6 mo; 3× by 12 mo
Length Benchmarks 1–1.5 in/mo; 2× at 10–12 mo; 3 yr = ½ adult

Normal Weight Recovery, Doubling/Tripling & Height Multipliers

LO 2.02 • Slide 9 & Transcript 5:52–6:48
Weight Trajectory Rules
  • Neonatal Nadir & Recovery: A healthy neonate may lose up to 10% of birth weight immediately postpartum, but must regain back to birth weight by 2 weeks of age.
  • Early Velocity & Multipliers: From 2–4 months, infants average 1–2 ounces (30–60 g) daily; birth weight doubles by 4–6 months and triples by 12 months.
Length & Adult Height Rules
  • Infancy Linear Gain: Infants grow approximately 1 to 1.5 inches (2.54–3.81 cm) per month, causing birth length to double by 10–12 months (transcript notes first year).
  • Age 3 Adult Predictor: Doubling a child's height at 3 years of age approximates their eventual mature adult height.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

"A neonate can lose up to 10% of body weight after birth, but needs to regain it by 2 weeks—then double birth weight by 4–6 months, triple by 1 year, and at age 3 double their height to estimate adult stature."

Second-Order Board Clue

A 3-week-old who remains 8% below birth weight has failed the 2-week birth-weight recovery rule and requires immediate feeding/caloric and organic evaluation.

Growth Chart Faltering Criteria & Pituitary/Adrenal Signatures

LO 3.01 • Slide 14 & Transcript 52:35–53:57
Five Objective Growth Faltering Criteria
  • Static Percentile & Drop-Off: Height consistently below the 3rd or 5th percentile, or height dropping down 2+ major percentile lines and remaining low.
  • Growth Arrest & Velocity Floor: No growth for several months across well-child checks, or growth rate < 2 inches (5 cm) per year between age 4 and puberty.
  • Weight-Height Divergence: Unexplained rapid swings in weight without diet/exercise changes signal thyroid, insulin, or adrenal disorders.
Cushing's Syndrome vs. GHD Habitus
  • Excess Cortisol (Cushing's): Classic pattern of inappropriate weight gain while height gain slows, accompanied by a fatty hump between shoulders and purple/pink stretch marks.
  • Growth Hormone Deficiency (GHD): Child appears much younger than peers with a chubby abdominal build and an underdeveloped nasal bridge.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

"If a child is tracking on a curve and drops off and stays low—or has zero growth for several months—that's a hormonal red flag; look closely at body proportions like a chubby belly with an underdeveloped nasal bridge or a shoulder hump."

Second-Order Board Clue

A 9-year-old gaining weight rapidly while crossing downward across two height percentiles with violaceous abdominal striae points directly to excess cortisol (Cushing's syndrome) rather than simple exogenous obesity (which accelerates height).

Pediatric Hypothyroidism vs. Hyperthyroidism

Earliest Pediatric Sign Unexplained growth pattern change

Classic Hypo Triad Macroglossia, umbilical hernia, hoarse cry
Hyperthyroid Clues Macrocephaly, hyperactivity, weight loss

Endocrine Craniofacial, Energy & Neurocognitive Thyroid Presentations

LO 3.02 • Slide 15 & Transcript 57:07–58:18
Infantile & Childhood Hypothyroidism
  • Somatic & Cranial Clues: Persistent open anterior fontanelle after 18 months (delayed cranial/brain maturation; rule out microcephaly), large tongue (macroglossia), umbilical hernia, and a hoarse voice or cry.
  • Systemic & Neurodevelopmental Impact: Extreme fatigue, unexplained weight gain despite normal diet, declining school concentration, and language delay / intellectual disability.
Hyperthyroidism & Shared Red Flags
  • Hyperthyroid Features: Associated with macrocephaly, persistent hyperactivity, unexplained weight loss despite normal eating habits, and deteriorating school performance.
  • Screening Priority: Unexplained change in growth velocity is the most common and earliest sign of pediatric thyroid disease, often preceding overt energy or weight shifts.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

"The anterior fontanelle should close by 18 months—if it stays open, plus you see a very large tongue (macroglossia), umbilical hernia, and a hoarse cry, think pediatric hypothyroidism."

Second-Order Board Clue

A 20-month-old with a patent anterior fontanelle, umbilical hernia, macroglossia, and hoarse cry requires urgent thyroid evaluation to prevent irreversible intellectual disability and language delay.

Age-Stratified Osteopathic Indications & Technique Selection

LO 4.01 • Slide 17 & Transcript 59:04–1:00:10
Infant & Neonate Modalities
  • Target Conditions: Birth trauma, plagiocephaly, torticollis, ankyloglossia (tongue tie), feeding difficulties, and colic; cranial base / suboccipital decompression releases tension around exiting cranial nerves (such as CN IX–XII involved in suckling and swallowing).
  • Why OCF, BLT & BMT: Because neonatal bones are still ossifying along open growth plates (e.g., tibial internal torsion treated gently with BLT/BMT while treating the whole patient), gentle Osteopathy in the Cranial Field (OCF), Balanced Ligamentous Tension (BLT), and Balanced Membranous Technique (BMT) respect developing ossification centers.
Older Child & Adolescent Expansion
  • Child & Teen Presentations: Childhood constipation, chronic otitis media, headaches, autism spectrum; adolescent neck/back pain, migraines, scoliosis, sprains/strains, and ADHD.
  • Expanded Modality Set: As children grow older, clinicians add soft tissue, direct myofascial release, strain-counterstrain, articular, and lymphatic techniques—remembering these age groupings are general observations, not rigid rules.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

"For neonates and infants, stick with cranial osteopathy (OCF), BLT, and BMT because they're developing along growth plates—and always treat the whole patient, not just an isolated internally rotated tibia."

Second-Order Board Clue

When managing an infant with birth-trauma feeding difficulty or tibial torsion, choose OCF, BLT, or BMT rather than high-velocity thrusting across unossified physes.

LO 4.02 • Pediatric OMT Pearls

Pediatric Tissue Response, Autonomy & Caregiver Teaching

Tissue Response Speed Rapid response; rare muscle fibrosis
Post-OMT Soreness Rare compared to adults
Pain / Resistance Rule Change technique/location or bring back
Parent Home Techniques Suboccipital release, ear pull, mandibular pump

Clinical Pearls, Distraction & Self-Determination in Pediatric OMT

LO 4.02 • Slide 18 & Transcript 1:00:30–1:03:25
Why Pediatric Tissues Respond Quickly
  • Minimal Chronicity: Children rarely have established muscle fibrosis, allowing rapid biomechanical changes with simple, playful treatments and rare post-OMT soreness.
  • Neurologically Diverse Patients: Even very young or neurologically compromised children perceive relief and may ask parents to "take me to the doctor who fixes my legs."
Autonomy, Distraction & Home Partnership
  • Never Force Treatment: Respect the child's autonomy and self-determination—tell them to say if anything hurts so you can switch techniques, treat a different region, distract (e.g., parent sitting in front while physician treats pelvis from behind), or reschedule.
  • Caregiver Empowerment: Parents can safely perform gentle home adjuncts taught in clinic, including suboccipital release, 45° ear pull, and mandibular drainage.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

"Keep OMT simple and fun—give the child autonomy. If you don't want to cause more trouble than it's worth when they're distressed, stop and bring them back another day."

Second-Order Board Clue

During OMT on a squirming toddler who cries and resists a supine cranial hold, the best next step is switching technique/position or using caregiver distraction rather than restraining the child.

Positional Plagiocephaly vs. Craniosynostosis

Epidemiologic Driver 1994 AAP Back to Sleep (↓ SIDS)

Differentiating Amenable Positional Molding from Surgical Synostosis

LO 5.01 • Slides 19–22 & Transcript 1:04:25–1:08:16
Positional vs. Synostotic Mechanics
  • Back to Sleep Surge: Since the 1994 AAP Back to Sleep campaign reduced SIDS, supine molding increased—a 2020 observational cohort (n=424) found 91.98% plagiocephaly, 8.02% brachycephaly, 60% males, and higher severity in preterm neonates.
  • Positional Parallelogram vs. Synostosis: In positional plagiocephaly, ipsilateral occiput compression pushes the ipsilateral ear and frontal bone forward; in craniosynostosis, premature suture fusion causes shortening on one side (not amenable to OMT alone).
  • CT Imaging Threshold: Always screen for synostosis first; if an infant shows no improvement after 2 or 3 OMT treatments, order a CT scan to confirm suture patency.
Standard of Care & OMT Outcomes
  • Standard Care Stack: Screen for synostosis, increase tummy time (keep off the flat spot), physical therapy, and helmet therapy before cervical/cranial maturation completes.
  • OMT Synergy: In the 424-infant cohort, head symmetry improved within 5 OMT treatments; OMT accelerates standard care by normalizing calvarial structure so the developing brain avoids cortical restriction.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

"If a child shows up in my office and after 2 or 3 OMT treatments they are making no changes, we are getting a CT scan to diagnose the patency of those cranial sutures."

Second-Order Board Clue

An infant with cranial flattening that fails to improve after 2–3 cranial OMT sessions requires cranial CT imaging to rule out craniosynostosis (premature suture fusion).

LO 5.02 • Muscular Torticollis

SCM Dysfunction, Osteopathic Tenets & Untreated Sequelae

Tight Muscle Driver Contracted sternocleidomastoid (SCM)
Classic Cervical Posture Sidebent toward / Rotated opposite
Musculoskeletal Sequelae Scoliosis, TMJ dysfunction, facial asymmetry
Treatment Ladder PT, home stretching, tummy time, OMT → SCM release

Biomechanics of SCM Contraction, Structure-Function & Complications

LO 5.02 • Slides 19–21 & Transcript 1:05:35–1:08:56
SCM Vector & Structure-Function Link
  • Left SCM Example: A tight left SCM pulls the head into left sidebending and right rotation, preventing the infant from turning left to follow their mother or make eye contact.
  • Cortical & Spinal Ripple Effect: By Osteopathic Tenet #1 (structure and function are interrelated), persistent one-sided gaze impairs visual, motor, sensory, and social development while asymmetric loading drives spinal scoliosis.
Management & Untreated Complications
  • Conservative & Surgical Care: Physical therapy, active visual stimulation toward the restricted side, parent-taught home stretching, positioning, and OMT; refractory severe cases require surgical SCM release.
  • Long-Term Complications: Untreated torticollis/plagiocephaly leads to craniofacial asymmetry, scoliosis, TMJ dysfunction, permanent SCM shortening, and speech/language delays.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

"If a baby is sidebent left and rotated right and can't turn to follow mom, that's a tight left SCM—and as the spine develops under that asymmetry, the child can develop scoliosis and visual-motor cortex delays."

Second-Order Board Clue

An infant presenting with head tilt to the right and chin rotated toward the left shoulder has contraction of the right sternocleidomastoid and is at risk for secondary positional plagiocephaly and scoliosis.

LO 5.03 • Otitis Media & Drainage

Chronic Otitis Media with Effusion (MEE), Galbreath & Ear Pull

Key Modifiable Risks Supine bottle propping, pacifier >6 mo, ↓ breastfeeding
Comorbidities / Season Winter viruses, prematurity, Down syndrome
Untreated Sequelae Conductive hearing loss → speech/learning delay
OMT Drainage Pair Mandibular pump (Galbreath) + 45° back/out ear pull

MEE Neurodevelopmental Impact & Osteopathic Eustachian Drainage

LO 5.03 • Slides 23–24 & Transcript 1:16:38–1:17:14
Risk Factors & Untreated Neurodevelopmental Risk
  • Risk Profile: Shorter breastfeeding duration, bottle propping in supine, winter indoor viral exposure, prematurity, pacifier use > 6 months, and Down syndrome.
  • Severe Sequelae: Persistent middle ear effusion causes conductive hearing loss, leading to speech, language, and learning delays, behavioral/social issues, chronic suppurative OM with TM rupture, mastoiditis, and rarely death.
Mandibular Pumping & Temporal Ear Pull
  • Mandibular Pumping (Galbreath): Stabilize the cranium with one hand and rhythmically pump/manipulate the mandible to milk the adjacent Eustachian tube and promote middle ear fluid drainage (easily taught to caregivers).
  • Auricular / Ear Pull: Gently traction the pinna at a 45° angle backward and outward to stretch the fascia around the temporal bone and release the temporal bone itself.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

"We stabilize the cranium and pump the mandible because the Eustachian tube sits right there to help it drain, and with the ear pull we traction at a 45-degree angle back and out to stretch the temporal fascia."

Second-Order Board Clue

A toddler with recurrent otitis media after supine bottle propping is at high risk for conductive hearing loss causing speech delay; first-line caregiver-teachable OMT uses mandibular pumping and 45° posterior-lateral ear pull.

Osteopathic Structure-Function Integration & Research Outcomes

LO 5.04 • Slides 21–24 & Transcript 1:05:57–1:08:56
Osteopathic Tenets in Neurodevelopment
  • Calvarial & Gaze Mechanics: Because the brain develops inside the calvarium and visual-motor cortex maturation relies on symmetrical head turning, untreated plagiocephaly/torticollis restricts eye contact, tracking, and visual-motor/sensory-social development.
  • Spinal & Craniofacial Cascade: Persistent unilateral neck tilt during rapid growth drives compensatory scoliosis, TMJ dysfunction, and permanent SCM shortening.
Lecture-Cited Pediatric OMT Studies
  • 2020 Retrospective Cohort (424 Infants): Plagiocephaly accounted for 91.98% and brachycephaly 8.02%; head symmetry measurably improved within 5 OMT treatments, acting as a rapid adjunct to standard care.
  • 2025 AOM Scoping Review (Hyun-su Kim et al.): Synthesizes evidence for osteopathic manipulation techniques in managing pediatric acute otitis media and reducing effusion-related neurodevelopmental risks.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus

"Standard of care works well, and OMT makes it go faster and is a great adjunct. If a baby can't follow mom or make eye contact, that impairs visual, motor, sensory, and social skills—and an entire developing spine can curve into scoliosis."

Second-Order Board Clue

Applying osteopathic principles to infant plagiocephaly/torticollis targets restoring calvarial and cervical motion within ~5 OMT visits to protect visual-motor cortex maturation and spinal alignment.
