LO 1.01 • Pediatric vs. Adult

Hematogenous Seeding & Metaphyseal Loops

Pediatric Site Long bone metaphysis
Vascular Driver Nutrient artery hairpin loops
Adult Hematogenous Site Anterior vertebral endplates
Microbial Clonality Monobacterial vs. polymicrobial

Vascular Architecture Dictates Age-Specific Bone Seeding

LO 1.01 • Slides 3–16
Pediatric Metaphyseal Predilection
  • Anatomic Trap: Capillary ends of the nutrient artery make sharp loops beneath the growth plate and empty into large venous sinusoids, causing slow turbulent blood flow and pooling.
  • Trauma Trigger: Even minor blunt trauma causes local hematoma, capillary thrombosis, and avascular necrosis, creating a devitalized niche seeded by transient bacteremia.
  • Acute Systemic Course: Children present acutely with malaise, fever, chills, leukocytosis, and marked throbbing pain over the affected long bone; infection is typically monobacterial.
Adult Spine & Diagnostic Workup
  • Senile Hyperemia & Retrograde Flow: With aging, metaphyseal flow slows and phagocytic activity rises, shifting hematogenous seeding to vascular anterior vertebral endplates; valveless lumbar paracentral veins communicate with pelvic veins, allowing retrograde spread from urinary/prostate/bladder infections.
  • Insidious Adult Course: Fever, chills, and local erythema are seldom seen in adults; a draining sinus tract may evolve over months or years.
  • Imaging & Biopsy: Traditional X-ray is cheap but less sensitive early; MRI and CT are standard of care; cultures via surgical sampling or CT/radiologic needle aspiration are indispensable.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Bramblett)

“In long-bone hematogenous osteomyelitis, infection is usually monobacterial, whereas contiguous-focus osteomyelitis (such as diabetic foot ulcers or joint arthroplasty) is often polymicrobial. Always obtain bone culture before tailoring antibiotics.”

Second-Order Board Clue

An elderly man with recent urinary tract instrumentation develops progressive lumbar back pain and disc-space narrowing without high fever; retrograde spread via pelvic-to-lumbar venous anastomoses explains vertebral osteomyelitis.

LO 1.02 • Bone Necrosis

Haversian Spread, Sequestrum & Diabetic Foot

Necrosis Timeline Entrapped bone dies in 48 hours
Dead Bone Fragment Sequestrum
Periosteal Pathway Haversian system (osteon)
Chronic Management Debridement + sequestrectomy

Subperiosteal Abscess, Sequestrum Formation & Contiguous Diabetic Spread

LO 1.02 • Slides 2, 5, 16–19
Cortical Destruction & Sequestra
  • Rapid Necrosis: Bacteria proliferate in marrow, causing inflammation and bone destruction; entrapped bone undergoes necrosis within the first 48 hours.
  • Subperiosteal Tracking: Bacteria travel through the Haversian system (osteon) to reach the periosteum. In children, the loosely attached periosteum lifts, impairing cortical blood supply and forming a subperiosteal abscess.
  • Sequestrum & Sinus: Dead bone fragments form a sequestrum enveloped by irregular reactive new periosteal bone; periosteal rupture produces a soft-tissue abscess and a draining sinus tract requiring sequestrectomy.
Diabetic Foot & Open Fracture
  • Diabetic Triad: Motor neuropathy (dorsal deformity, high plantar pressure), sensory neuropathy (loss of protective sensation, skin breakdown), and autonomic neuropathy (dry skin) combine with arterial insufficiency and immune dysfunction.
  • Contiguous Etiology: Although Staphylococcus aureus is the single most common pathogen in diabetic foot osteomyelitis, contiguous spread is frequently polymicrobial.
  • Open Fracture / Hardware: Non-united fractures after intramedullary nailing (e.g., 6 months post-ORIF) harbor chronic osteomyelitis requiring combined debridement and targeted antibiotics.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Bramblett)

“Why does chronic osteomyelitis fail medical therapy alone? Because antibiotics cannot penetrate avascular sequestra or bacterial biofilms—progression from acute to chronic demands long-term antibiotics plus surgical debridement and sequestrectomy.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A pediatric tibia radiograph showing patchy diaphyseal destruction with thick, irregular periosteal new bone enveloping a dense necrotic sequestrum signals chronic osteomyelitis following subperiosteal lifting.

MSCRAMMs Anchor S. aureus to Bone Extracellular Matrix (BEM)

LO 2.01 • Slides 6–8, 10
Lab ID & BEM Adherence
  • Diagnostic Profile: Gram-positive cocci in clusters, non-motile facultative anaerobe, catalase-positive, coagulase-positive, β-hemolytic, salt- and temperature-tolerant with golden-pigmented colonies.
  • MSCRAMM Adhesins: Collagen-binding protein (cna, increased expression in MRSA), fibronectin-binding proteins (fnbA, fnbB), clumping factors (clfA, clfB), and sialoglycoprotein adhesin bind Bone Extracellular Matrix (BEM).
  • Osteoblast Bridge: Fibronectin links S. aureus to host osteoblasts, concentrating bacteria around bone-forming cells and triggering internalization.
Cytolytic Toxins & Invasion Enzymes
  • Cytolytic Toxins: Alpha-toxin creates transmembrane pores lysing RBCs, muscle, vessels, hepatocytes, and platelets; Beta-toxin does NOT form pores—it enzymatically hydrolyzes sphingomyelin; Delta acts as a surfactant; Gamma & Panton-Valentine (P-V) leukocidin are two-peptide toxins that lyse leukocytes and macrophages.
  • Invasive Enzymes & Immune Evasion: Anti-phagocytic polysaccharide capsule hides PAMPs; hyaluronidase degrades BEM hyaluronic acid; coagulase promotes bone thrombosis and necrosis; staphylokinase breaks down clots; superantigen exotoxins (SEs) over-induce cytokines causing apoptosis.
  • Treatment: β-lactams (cefazolin or ceftriaxone) for MSSA, or vancomycin for MRSA.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Bramblett)

“Note the mechanistic distinction among cytolysins: alpha-toxin punches pores in host membranes, whereas beta-toxin is enzymatic (degrading sphingomyelin) and delta acts like a detergent surfactant.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A child with acute tibial metaphyseal osteomyelitis yields Gram-positive cocci in clusters. The organism’s fibronectin-binding proteins and cna collagen adhesin explain why S. aureus is #1 in all osteomyelitis patient groups.

Why S. aureus Bone Infections Relapse: Biofilms & Intracellular SCVs

LO 2.02 • Slides 9–10
Biofilms & agr Quorum Sensing
  • Dual agr Switch: Quorum sensing via the agr system either upregulates an invasive program (hemolysins, proteases) or downregulates invasion to favor adherence and biofilm formation.
  • 1000x Tolerance Barrier: Biofilm matrix shields bacteria from host phagocytes, conceals PAMPs, and confers up to 1000-fold drug resistance.
  • Nutrient/O₂ Dormancy: Deep within biofilms, oxygen and nutrient deprivation pushes bacteria into a metabolically dormant state that blunts bactericidal antibiotics.
Small Colony Variants (SCVs)
  • Osteoblast Trojan Horse: After BEM binding, S. aureus invades osteoblasts and shifts to Small Colony Variants (SCVs)—present in 29% of osteomyelitis clinical cases.
  • Transcriptional Shift: SCVs exhibit impaired growth, repressed tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle metabolism, and depressed agr virulence, while sigB and persistence/biofilm genes are upregulated.
  • Relapse & Bone Loss: SCVs resist antibiotics and antiseptics (even triclosan); osteoblast apoptosis releases SCVs to infect neighboring cells while increasing osteoclast bone resorption.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Bramblett)

“SCVs explain unclearable, recurrent S. aureus osteomyelitis: by hiding inside osteoblasts with a repressed TCA cycle and depressed agr, they avoid killing host cells prematurely while evading antibiotics.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Recurrent osteomyelitis months after completed IV therapy and diabetic foot care is driven by intracellular SCVs (upregulated sigB, downregulated metabolism) plus biofilm dormancy.

Non-Fermenting Gram-Negative Rods in Trauma, Soaking & ICU Settings

LO 3.01 • Slides 11–12
Pseudomonas aeruginosa Profile
  • Media & Biochemistry: Obligate aerobe, oxidase-positive, catalase-positive, does not ferment carbohydrates; fruity odor; colorless colonies on MacConkey, brown/semi-clearing on BAP, blue-green colonies (pyocyanin) on nutrient agar.
  • Virulence Arsenal: Lipid A endotoxin; Type II/III secretion systems injecting Exotoxin A (blocks peptide chain elongation) and Exoenzymes S & T (disrupt actin); pyoverdin siderophore; Las A/B elastases degrading elastin & complement; heat-labile Phospholipase C hemolysin.
  • Resistance & Niche: Alginate capsule + porin exclusion + 4 efflux pumps (MexAB-OprCD, etc.) + β-lactamases/carbapenemases; causes calcaneal osteomyelitis after nail-through-sneaker puncture, diabetic foot soaking, and IVDU osteomyelitis (82% pre-1981; now 77% S. aureus).
Acinetobacter baumannii (ICU / Surgical)
  • Morphology & Biochemical ID: Non-fermentative, Gram-negative short rod (coccobacillus); glucose-oxidizing (unlike A. haemolyticus); oxidase-negative, catalase-positive, indole-negative, urease-negative, γ-hemolytic.
  • Extreme Tolerance: Highly temperature tolerant (grows up to 44°C), aiding persistence in hospital environments.
  • Clinical & Resistance Risk: Opportunistic pathogen in patients on broad-spectrum antibiotics, post-surgical recovery, or mechanical ventilation; notorious for strains resistant to nearly all antibiotics, including carbapenems.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Bramblett)

“Remember two classic Pseudomonas prompts: stepping on a nail through a rubber-soled shoe, and a diabetic patient who extensively soaks their feet in water. Also remember IVDU shifted from 82% Pseudomonas pre-1981 to 77% S. aureus today.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A 6-year-old boy steps on a nail through an athletic shoe and develops calcaneal erythema; the causative oxidase-positive, pyocyanin-producing rod uses alginate and Mex efflux pumps to resist β-lactams.

Age-Stratified Pediatric Pathogens & Sickle Cell Osteomyelitis

LO 3.02 • Slides 13–14, 21–22
Pediatric Age Shift & Kingella kingae
  • Age-Specific Etiologies: 0–3 months = S. aureus, Streptococcus agalactiae (GBS), and enteric Gram-negatives (E. coli); 3 mo–4 yr = S. aureus, S. pyogenes, S. pneumoniae (immunodeficient), Kingella kingae, Hib (unimmunized); >5 yr = S. aureus, S. pyogenes.
  • Vaccine Shift: Routine Hib vaccination caused a steep drop in H. influenzae type b joint infections, elevating K. kingae to #1 in children 3–36 months (especially daycare attendees after viral URI).
  • Kingella ID: Plump aerobic Gram-negative coccobacilli in pairs/chains; colonizes URT/throat; uses pili for joint tropism; smooth gray colonies pit blood agar with small β-hemolysis; oxidase +, catalase −, urease −, indole −.
Sickle Cell Disease & Salmonella
  • High Complication Rate: Bone and joint infections occur in ~10% of sickle cell disease patients; Salmonella spp. and S. aureus are equally common causes of acute hematogenous osteomyelitis.
  • Diagnostic Mimicry: Vaso-occlusive sickle cell crisis already causes bone pain, fever, and leukocytosis—making infection hard to confirm without CT-guided aspirates for microbiology.
  • Salmonella ID: Gram-negative rod, oxidase-negative, catalase-positive, non-lactose fermenter, and H₂S-positive (black centers on HEK agar).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Bramblett)

“Because Gram stains of synovial fluid are often negative in Kingella kingae, PCR from synovial fluid is crucial in toddlers aged 6–36 months following an upper respiratory infection.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A 4-year-old daycare toddler refuses to bear weight after a viral URI; synovial fluid shows elevated WBCs, negative Gram stain, and PCR positive for a catalase-negative, oxidase-positive Gram-negative coccobacillus → Kingella kingae.

Distinguishing Bite-Associated Osteomyelitis & Septic Arthritis

LO 3.03 • Slides 20, 31
Eikenella corrodens (Human Oral Flora)
  • Exposure Links: Normal human oral/URT/GI/GU flora; causes clenched-fist human bite septic arthritis/osteomyelitis and “needle-licker osteomyelitis” in PWID who lick needles before injecting.
  • AACEK Membership: Part of the AACEK (formerly HACEK) endocarditis group alongside Aggregatibacter, Cardiobacterium, and Kingella kingae.
  • Biochemical ID: Small straight microaerophilic Gram-negative rod (coccobacillary), oxidase-positive, reduces nitrates to nitrites, catalase-negative, urease-negative, indole-negative; classically pits/corrodes agar.
Pasteurella multocida (Cat/Dog Bites)
  • Clinical Velocity: Causes rapidly progressive soft-tissue inflammation after animal (especially cat) bites; pain near bone or joint out of proportion to injury severity signals periosteal penetration and osteomyelitis risk.
  • Biochemical & Agar ID: Aerobic, bipolar-staining Gram-negative coccobacillus; catalase +, oxidase +, indole + (distinctive fecal-like odor), citrate −, nitrate reduction +; grows well on blood/chocolate agar but poorly on MacConkey; ferments glucose/sucrose/mannose but NOT lactose.
  • Virulence & Tx: LPS, capsule, cytotoxin, iron-acquisition proteins; remains penicillin-sensitive.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Bramblett)

“Pair the two bite organisms by catalase and indole: Eikenella (human bite, needle licker) is oxidase+, catalase−, indole− and pits agar; Pasteurella (cat bite) is oxidase+, catalase+, indole+ with a foul odor and grows poorly on MacConkey.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A patient develops small-joint septic arthritis of the hand after punching someone in the mouth; synovial fluid yields a nitrate-reducing, catalase-negative Gram-negative rod that pits agar → Eikenella corrodens.

Why Bacterial Arthritis Is an Emergency: Exposed ECM & 3-Day Destruction

LO 4.01 • Slides 23–26
Synovial Seeding & Morbidity
  • Low-Shear Adhesion: Low shear conditions of synovial fluid plus preexisting joint damage (RA, OA, gout, lupus, surgery, trauma) expose host ECM (fibronectin, collagen, laminin, hyaluronic acid) for bacterial MSCRAMM attachment.
  • Routes: Usually hematogenous (overt/occult bacteremia or endocarditis); also direct inoculation (surgery, trauma, bites, percutaneous puncture) or contiguous spread.
  • 3-Day Destruction: Massive neutrophil influx creates purulent synovitis; leukocyte-derived proteases plus IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α degrade cartilage, inhibit matrix synthesis, and erode subchondral bone in as little as 3 days.
Bacterial Etiologies & Case 1
  • Gram-Positives: S. aureus (37–65% overall; 75% in RA; rising MRSA); Coagulase-negative Staph in prosthetic joints (caution: Staphylococcus lugdunensis is highly virulent/invasive); Streptococci (S. pyogenes 22%, Groups C/F/G, neonatal GBS).
  • Gram-Negative Bacilli (~20%): E. coli & enteric rods in elderly (~50% have underlying UTI) and IVDU; P. aeruginosa in IVDU, neonates, elderly, post-surgical, immunocompromised.
  • Case 1 Integration: 49F with RA on corticosteroids develops acute, warm, tender 2nd MCP joint for 4 days; arthrocentesis yields cloudy fluid with numerous neutrophils + bacteria → urgent septic arthritis (S. aureus most likely in RA).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Bramblett)

“Septic arthritis is a clinical emergency because host leukocyte proteases and IL-1/IL-6/TNF-α—not just bacterial toxins—cause cartilage destruction and subchondral bone loss in as little as three days! Always watch out for Staphylococcus lugdunensis among CoNS.”

Second-Order Board Clue

An elderly patient with acute purulent knee arthritis has no trauma history; ~50% of older adults with Gram-negative (E. coli) septic arthritis have an underlying urinary tract infection.

Distinctive Joint Tropism & Systemic Risks in Injection Drug Use

LO 4.02 • Slide 27 & Handout Key
Microbiologic Spectrum in PWID
  • Dominant Skin Flora: S. aureus causes 50–90% of PWID bone/joint infections (with MRSA accounting for 25–40%), reflecting the post-1981 shift away from Pseudomonas.
  • Broad Opportunists: Elevated rates of viridans streptococci, Bacillus spp., Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Serratia marcescens, Eikenella corrodens (needle licking), and yeast (Candida albicans).
  • 4 Associated Clinical Conditions: PWID septic arthritis frequently co-presents with (1) endocarditis, (2) bacteremia, (3) sepsis, and (4) polymicrobial joint infections.
Anatomical Predilections
  • Common Large Joints: Knee, hip, shoulder, and wrists remain most frequently involved overall.
  • PWID Hallmark “S” & Pelvic Joints: Uncommon fibrocartilaginous joints—sternoclavicular, sacroiliac, and pubic symphysis (osteitis pubis)—are classic in PWID.
  • Non-PWID Correlates: Sacroiliac or pubic symphysis osteomyelitis in non-PWID patients occurs with indwelling catheters or gyneco-urologic surgery / prolonged catheterization.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Bramblett)

“Always associate sternoclavicular, sacroiliac, and pubic symphysis infections with IV drug use—or in non-PWID, look for indwelling catheters or recent urologic/gynecologic surgery.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A 35-year-old man with IVDU presents with fever, back pain, and intermittent blood cultures growing yeast; MRI shows vertebral discitis/osteomyelitis → Candida albicans (versus MRSA if Gram-positive cocci).

LO 4.03 • Gonococcal Arthritis

Neisseria gonorrhoeae & Disseminated Gonococcal Infection

DGI Classical Triad Dermatitis, tenosynovitis, polyarthritis

Serum Resistance PorB (P1A) binds Factor H
DGI Opa Phenotype Transparent (Opa-negative)

From Asymptomatic Mucosal Carriage to DGI Triad & Monoarticular Arthritis

LO 4.03 • Slides 28–30
Clinical Progression & Host Susceptibility
  • Two DGI Presentations: (1) Classical bacteremic triad of dermatitis (rash), tenosynovitis, and migratory polyarthritis; (2) Progression to purulent monoarticular/oligoarticular septic arthritis (42–85% of DGI), with full recovery and rare sequelae when treated.
  • Host Factors: 75% of DGI occurs in women (often asymptomatic mucosal infection); terminal complement deficiencies (C5–C8), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and complement-blocking immunosuppressants dramatically raise DGI risk.
  • Lab Identification: Gram-negative diplococci requiring Chocolate agar at 37°C + 5% CO₂ (poor survival on Blood agar); oxidase +, catalase +, nitrate −, H₂S −; ferments glucose only (negative for maltose, lactose, mannose, sucrose). Cultured from synovial fluid in ~50% of septic cases.
Gonococcal Virulence & Phase Variation
  • PorB (PorA is silent): Nutrient channel with P1A and P1B alleles; interferes with neutrophil degranulation and facilitates epithelial invasion. P1A strains bind host Factor H to confer serum complement resistance and drive DGI.
  • Opacity Proteins (Opa): Strains expressing Opa cause localized mucosal infection; transparent strains lacking Opa evade immune clearance and are far more likely to cause DGI.
  • Phase/Antigenic Variation: Variable pili, Opa, lipo-oligosaccharide (LOS) biosynthesis, and PorB antigenic variation enable persistence and serum resistance.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Bramblett)

“Remember the DGI virulence paradox: Opa-positive colonies cause localized genital infection, whereas transparent Opa-negative strains with PorB P1A (binding Factor H) disseminate into the bloodstream.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A sexually active young woman or patient with C5–C8 deficiency develops wrist tenosynovitis, pustular skin lesions, and knee effusion; synovial culture grows oxidase+ Gram-negative diplococci on Chocolate agar fermenting glucose only.

Direct Synovial Infection vs. Immune Complex Viral Polyarthritis

LO 5.01 • Slides 32–34 & Handout Key
Parvovirus B19, Rubella & HBV/HCV
  • Human Parvovirus B19 (DNA): Most common viral arthritis in developed countries (respiratory spread); causes RA-like IP/MCP polyarthritis in adults (large joints in children) mediated by anti-B19 antibody immune complexes.
  • Rubella Virus: Maculopapular rash, fever, and occipital lymphadenopathy; causes direct synovial infection of small hand joints in adults (rather than child rash/LAD).
  • HBV & HCV: HBV arthritis occurs in the pre-icteric febrile prodrome (before jaundice) with urticaria via immune complex deposition in synovium; HCV causes RA-like small-joint polyarthritis or medium/large-joint oligoarthritis.
HIV, HTLV-1 & Arboviruses (CHIKV vs. Dengue)
  • HIV & HTLV-1: HIV-1 rarely causes a painful 24-hour oligoarticular arthritis/arthralgia; initiating antiretroviral therapy (ART) can trigger immune reconstitution syndrome (IRIS) unmasking autoimmune rheumatologic disease.
  • Chikungunya (CHIKV): Transmitted by Aedes aegypti mosquito (endemic in Africa, Asia, South Asia, India); causes chronic oligoarticular/polyarticular debilitating joint pain lasting up to 36 months.
  • Dengue Contrast: While arthralgia is common in Dengue (Flavivirus), true arthritis and synovitis are rare—distinguishing it from CHIKV.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Bramblett)

“Compare mechanisms cleanly: Rubella directly infects the synovium of small hand joints, whereas Parvovirus B19 and pre-icteric HBV cause arthritis via antibody–antigen immune complex deposition.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A woman working in a daycare develops symmetric MCP/PIP joint swelling with negative rheumatoid factor after a child’s slapped-cheek illness → Parvovirus B19 immune-complex polyarthritis.

Indolent Mono/Pauciarticular Chronic Bacterial Infections

LO 5.02 • Slides 35–37 & Handout Key
Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Pott Disease)
  • Epidemiology & Risks: Acid-fast intracellular bacillus; 1–3% of TB cases involve bone/joints, and only 50% have respiratory involvement; risks include lower SES, incarceration, alcohol abuse, IVDU, immunosuppressives, HIV, preexisting joint disease.
  • Clinical & Biopsy: Insidious monoarticular joint or thoracic/lumbar vertebral osteomyelitis (Pott disease) with night sweats, weight loss, vertebral collapse, paraspinal abscess; synovial biopsy shows caseating or non-caseating granulomas (culture 80% sensitive).
  • Danger & Therapy: Inappropriate intra-articular steroids accelerate joint destruction! Treat with RIPE (INH, RIF, ethambutol, pyrazinamide) × 8 wks, then INH + RIF × 6 mo. (Note: M. marinum is photochromogenic—yellow/orange pigment with light.)
Lyme, Syphilis & Whipple Disease
  • Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme): Spirochete; Stage 1 erythema migrans (3–32 d); Stage 2 disseminated fever/headache/neck stiffness (40%); Stage 3 persistent intermittent pauciarticular arthritis in 60%—affecting the knee in >95% via direct infection + T/B-cell cytokines.
  • Treponema pallidum: Early syphilis is associated with polyarthritis with synovitis.
  • Tropheryma whippelii (Whipple): Rare chronic bacterium causing intermittent arthralgia for up to 6 years, followed by chronic obstructive GI disease, then late neurological symptoms; fatal without treatment.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Bramblett)

“Only half of patients with skeletal TB have active pulmonary symptoms! Never inject intra-articular steroids into an undiagnosed chronic monoarticular joint—it can catastrophically worsen TB or fungal joint destruction.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A patient with 5 years of migratory arthralgias now develops chronic malabsorptive/obstructive GI distress and CNS signs → Tropheryma whippelii (Whipple disease).

Endemic Dimorphic Fungi (25°C Mold / 37°C Yeast)
  • Blastomyces dermatitidis: SE/NE/Central US (Ohio River Valley); pulmonary → 25% disseminate to skin/bone/GU; broad-based budding yeast; causes osteolytic lesions & monoarticular knee arthritis in healthy hosts.
  • Coccidioides immitis: SW US (Arizona/Valley fever); inhaled arthroconidia → spherules with endospores; higher risk in older, male, African American, or Filipino patients; migratory polyarthralgia/polyarthritis (1/3) and vertebral osteomyelitis; treat with Amphotericin B then fluconazole/itraconazole ≥3 yr.
  • Histoplasma & Paracoccidioides: H. capsulatum = bird/bat droppings, small intracellular yeasts in macrophages (disseminates in HIV); P. brasiliensis = Latin America, multiple buds off mother cell, isolated bone/monoarticular disease.
  • Sporothrix schenckii: Rose gardener’s disease; cutaneous thorn inoculation; cigar-shaped yeast & “daisy petal” conidiophores; 80% of extracutaneous disease is osteoarticular via hematogenous spread → chronic multifocal/polyarticular weight-bearing joints.
Opportunistic Fungi (Immunocompromised / IVDU)
  • Candida albicans: Most common with IVDU, indwelling catheters, or non-immunosuppressed parenteral inoculation; pseudohyphae, true hyphae, and blastoconidia at septa; knees represent 75% of Candida arthritis (plus IVDU vertebral osteomyelitis).
  • Aspergillus (A. fumigatus 50–67%, A. flavus 8–14%): Immunocompromised (transplant, steroids, contaminated spine injections); septate hyphae branching at 45° + conidiophores with terminal vesicles; invades vertebrae/maxillofacial/mastoid bones. A. fumigatus grows at 50°C (smokey gray-green); A. flavus is olive-green and produces aflatoxin.
  • Cryptococcus neoformans: Encapsulated yeast in HIV/immunocompromised; chronic knee arthritis or osteomyelitis; diagnose via serum capsular antigen + fungal culture.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Bramblett)

“Note two subtle traps: (1) Most patients with Candida arthritis are NOT immunosuppressed (think IVDU or catheters, 75% knees); (2) Osteoarticular sporotrichosis is 80% hematogenous polyarticular, NOT direct joint puncture.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A lung transplant recipient develops vertebral osteomyelitis with normal WBCs, CRP 205.8, ESR 101, and biopsy showing septate hyphae with 45° acute-angle branching → Aspergillus fumigatus (grows at 50°C).
