Osteoarthritis Pathogenesis, Biomechanics & Non-Pharmacological Care

Lecturer Rule: Cartilage is aneural; pain arises from bone, synovium & periosteum

Interactive Figure 1: Osteoarthritis Joint Progression & Biomechanical Triage

Select a structural stage to inspect cartilage mechanics, radiographic signs, and foundational care

LO 1.01 • Pathogenesis

Osteoarthritis Chondrocyte Imbalance & Structural Remodeling

Pathologic Trigger Cartilage fibrillation & proteoglycan loss
Cellular Driver Activated chondrocyte catabolic imbalance
Bone Remodeling Subchondral sclerosis, eburnation, osteophytes
Radiographic Hallmark Asymmetric joint space narrowing
Major Risk Factors Aging, obesity, trauma, female sex, genetics

Progressive Cartilage Breakdown Outpacing Matrix Resynthesis

LO 1.01 • Slides 4–5
Degenerative Cascade
  • Biomechanical Initiation: Mechanical wear and tear, prior joint trauma, obesity, aging, female sex, and genetics initiate cartilage fibrillation and depletion of shock-absorbing proteoglycans.
  • Chondrocyte Failure: Cartilage injury triggers resident chondrocytes to proliferate and attempt repair, but matrix breakdown steadily outpaces resynthesis.
  • Hypertrophic Bone Changes: Loss of cushioning exposes underlying bone to overload, generating subchondral sclerosis, polished ivory-like eburnation, and marginal bone spurs (osteophytes).
Pattern & X-Ray Correlates
  • Asymmetric Distribution: Unlike symmetric autoimmune inflammatory arthritides, osteoarthritis is classically asymmetric, targeting load-bearing knees, hips, and hand joints.
  • Diagnostic Radiographs: Plain X-rays confirm progressive cartilage loss via joint space narrowing paired with hypertrophic osteophyte formation and subchondral bone changes.
  • Physical Exam Triad: Patients exhibit pain on motion, reduced range of motion (ROM), and palpable crepitus that worsens with activity and improves with rest.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Keshab Paudel, BDS, MD)

“Osteoarthritis is not simple passive wear—cartilage damage activates chondrocytes, causing an imbalance where breakdown outpaces resynthesis, producing osteophytes and joint space narrowing.”

Second-Order Board Clue

An older patient with asymmetric knee pain worsening with activity and relieved by rest plus X-ray osteophytes and joint space narrowing points to hypertrophic bone remodeling rather than urate crystal synovitis.

LO 1.02 • Pain & Non-Pharm

Aneural Pain Origin, Non-Pharmacological Care & Excluded Therapies

Pain Generators Bone microfractures, synovium, periosteum
First-Line Foundation Weight loss, low-impact exercise, braces
Adjunct Modalities Physiotherapy & TENS
Not Recommended Glucosamine, methotrexate, biologics, stem cells
End-Stage Option Total joint replacement surgery

Biomechanical Offloading & Guideline-Excluded Modalities

LO 1.02 • Slides 6–7, 12
Nociception & Core Non-Pharm
  • Why OA Hurts: Articular cartilage is aneural; pain arises secondarily from subchondral bone microfractures, synovial capsule distension, and mechanical/chemical irritation of periosteum, ligaments, and meniscus.
  • Foundational Care: Every patient starts with weight reduction, regular low-impact exercise, physiotherapy, supportive knee braces, and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS).
  • Surgical Escalation: Realigning bones or total joint replacement (hip, knee, or shoulder) is reserved for disabling pain refractory to maximal medical therapy.
Evidence-Excluded Interventions
  • Over-the-Counter Supplements: Routine use of glucosamine (and chondroitin routine recommendations) lacks supporting evidence in current guidelines.
  • DMARDs & Biologics: Immunomodulators such as chloroquine, methotrexate, and biologics are not recommended because OA is primarily mechanical/degenerative rather than systemic autoimmune.
  • Regenerative Claims: Routine stem cell injections are explicitly not recommended due to insufficient clinical evidence.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Keshab Paudel, BDS, MD)

“Exercise and weight loss are core adjuncts proven to reduce pain and disability, while glucosamine, chloroquine, methotrexate, biologics, and stem cell injections are not recommended for routine OA care.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Because articular cartilage lacks nerve endings, nociceptive firing comes from periosteal and synovial irritation; the strongest non-drug intervention reducing joint loading is weight loss plus low-impact exercise.

Lecturer Rule: NSAIDs treat local inflammation; acetaminophen acts centrally without anti-inflammatory effect

Interactive Figure 2: Osteoarthritis Analgesic Triage Workbench

LO 2.01 • Central Analgesic

Acetaminophen — Central COX Inhibition & Hepatic Toxicity

Molecular Target Central CNS COX / PG inhibition
Clinical Profile Analgesic & antipyretic; minimal GI upset
Key Pitfall Negligible peripheral anti-inflammatory action
Overdose Toxicity NAPQI-mediated hepatotoxicity
Antidote Rescue N-acetylcysteine (NAC)

Central Pain Threshold Modulation vs. Lack of Synovial Anti-Inflammation

LO 2.01 • Slide 8 & Podcast
Central Mechanism & Limits
  • Central Analgesia: Inhibits prostaglandin synthesis centrally in the brain (proposed COX / COX-3 inhibition), elevating the pain threshold and reducing fever.
  • Crucial OA Limitation: Possesses little to no peripheral anti-inflammatory activity, meaning it reduces pain perception without resolving active synovial swelling.
  • Gastric Advantage: Unlike systemic NSAIDs, acetaminophen causes minimal gastric irritation and is safe for patients at elevated peptic ulcer risk.
Step-Up & Hepatic Guardrails
  • Practice Case 1 Triage: When an OA patient has inadequate relief on PRN acetaminophen with worsening stiffness and osteophytes, step up to an oral NSAID (e.g., ibuprofen) to target local inflammation.
  • Toxic Metabolite: Hepatic overdose saturates glucuronidation/sulfation, shifting metabolism to toxic NAPQI, which depletes glutathione and causes centrilobular necrosis.
  • Emergency Rescue: Administer N-acetylcysteine (NAC) to replenish hepatic glutathione stores and detoxify NAPQI.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Keshab Paudel, BDS, MD)

“Acetaminophen inhibits prostaglandin synthesis centrally to raise the pain threshold with minimal gastric irritation, but it has very little anti-inflammatory action—if OA pain persists, step up to an NSAID.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A 65-year-old woman with knee OA and osteophytes fails PRN acetaminophen; switching to ibuprofen succeeds because NSAIDs suppress peripheral prostaglandin-mediated inflammation.

LO 2.02 • First-Line OA Drugs

NSAIDs (Topical, Non-Selective Oral & Celecoxib)

First-Line OA Status Topical & oral NSAIDs (hand/knee)
Molecular Target COX-1 & COX-2 inhibition
GI & Renal Risks Peptic ulcer bleed & afferent AKI
Selective COX-2 Agent Celecoxib (GI-sparing)
COX-2 Black-Box Risk Cardiovascular thrombosis / MI

Prostaglandin Pathway Blockade & Organ Toxicity Trade-Offs

LO 2.02 • Slides 6, 9–10 & Podcast Q2
Arachidonic Acid & Route Selection
  • Prostaglandin Cascade: Membrane phospholipids release arachidonic acid via PLA2; cyclooxygenase (COX-1 / COX-2) converts it into prostaglandins that sensitize nociceptors and drive synovitis.
  • Topical Preference: Guidelines strongly recommend topical NSAIDs early for hand and knee OA to achieve local joint concentrations while minimizing systemic GI and renal exposure.
  • Non-Selective Risks: Blocking constitutive COX-1 diminishes protective gastric mucus/bicarbonate (GI bleeding / peptic ulcer) and impairs renal afferent arteriole dilation (renal vasoconstriction / AKI).
Celecoxib & Thrombotic Imbalance
  • COX-2 Selectivity: Celecoxib selectively spares COX-1 in gastric mucosa, lowering upper GI ulceration and bleeding risk when switching from naproxen.
  • Cardiovascular Hazard: Selective COX-2 blockade suppresses endothelial vasodilatory/anti-aggregatory prostacyclin (PGI2) while sparing platelet COX-1 thromboxane A2 (TXA2).
  • Podcast Case 2 Alert: A 70-year-old switched from naproxen to celecoxib for GI discomfort faces a heightened risk of myocardial infarction / cardiovascular thrombosis.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Keshab Paudel, BDS, MD)

“Topical and oral NSAIDs are strongly recommended first-line therapies for hand and knee OA; always weigh gastric ulceration and nephrotoxicity against cardiovascular risk when selecting selective COX-2 agents.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Switching an elderly OA patient from naproxen to celecoxib reduces peptic ulcer risk by sparing COX-1, but increases myocardial infarction risk via unopposed platelet TXA2.

LO 2.03 • Intra-Articular Agents

Intra-Articular Corticosteroids & Hyaluronic Acid Injections

Steroid Mechanism PLA2 blockade via lipocortin-1
Clinical Duration Short-term relief (4–8 weeks)
Frequency Cap Max 3–4 injections/year (cartilage atrophy)
Hyaluronic Acid Synovial fluid lubrication

Upstream Eicosanoid Suppression vs. Synovial Viscosupplementation

LO 2.03 • Slides 11–12
Intra-Articular Corticosteroids
  • Molecular Cascade: Induces synthesis of lipocortin-1 (annexin-1), which inhibits phospholipase A2 (PLA2) and blocks release of arachidonic acid, suppressing both prostaglandins and leukotrienes.
  • Clinical Utility: Provides rapid, short-term (4 to 8 weeks) relief during acute osteoarthritic knee flare-ups when oral therapy is insufficient.
  • Strict Frequency Limit: Repeat injections must be capped at 3–4 times per year to prevent accelerated cartilage atrophy and joint degeneration.
Hyaluronic Acid Injections
  • Biophysical Role: Hyaluronic acid is an endogenous constituent of synovial fluid that restores viscoelasticity and lubricates articular surfaces.
  • Comparative Trade-Off: More expensive than steroid injections and shows variable overall clinical benefit across trials, but may maintain longer-lasting symptom improvement in selected knee OA patients.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Keshab Paudel, BDS, MD)

“Intra-articular corticosteroid injections provide short-term relief of knee OA flare-ups for four to eight weeks, whereas hyaluronic acid injections are more expensive but can sustain improvement longer.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Injecting intra-articular triamcinolone suppresses inflammation upstream of COX by inducing lipocortin-1 to inhibit phospholipase A2, but must be limited to 3–4/year to avoid cartilage thinning.

LO 2.04 • Neuromodulators

Topical Capsaicin, Duloxetine (SNRI) & Tramadol

Capsaicin Target TRPV1 (vanilloid-1) receptor agonist
Capsaicin Downstream Depletes substance P in C-fibers
Duloxetine Class SNRI (inhibits NE & 5-HT reuptake)
Tramadol Role Step-up if NSAIDs/APAP fail

Nociceptive Depletion & Descending Pain Pathway Modulation

LO 2.04 • Slides 11–12
Topical Capsaicin (TRPV1)
  • Vanilloid Binding: Derived from hot peppers, capsaicin binds the TRPV1 receptor (vanilloid receptor 1) on peripheral sensory nociceptors.
  • Neurotransmitter Depletion: Sustained TRPV1 activation depletes substance P from nociceptive nerve endings, blunting pain signal transmission to the spinal cord (causes transient initial burning).
Duloxetine & Tramadol
  • Duloxetine (SNRI): Inhibits presynaptic reuptake of norepinephrine and serotonin, enhancing descending spinal inhibitory pain pathways in chronic OA.
  • Tramadol Escalation: Conditionally considered when pain remains uncontrolled despite NSAIDs and acetaminophen, balancing analgesia against opioid/CNS adverse effects.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Keshab Paudel, BDS, MD)

“Capsaicin binds TRPV1 receptors to deplete substance P from sensory fibers, duloxetine inhibits NE and serotonin reuptake, and tramadol is reserved when NSAIDs and acetaminophen fail.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A patient applying a topical chili-pepper cream for hand OA experiences initial warmth followed by analgesia because TRPV1 stimulation exhausts axonal substance P stores.

LO 3.01 • Metabolic Defect

Hyperuricemia, Evolutionary Uricase Loss & Secondary Drivers

Solubility Threshold >6.8 mg/dL at 37°C (normal <6 mg/dL)
Human Enzyme Defect Absence of uricase (urate oxidase)
Drug Triggers Furosemide, thiazides, PZA, ethambutol
Pathologic Triggers Leukemia/lymphoma chemo (TLS)
Lifestyle Triggers Alcohol & purine-rich red meat

Supersaturation Mechanics & Secondary Hyperuricemia

LO 3.01 • Slides 13, 15
Urate Chemistry & Uricase Gap
  • Biochemical Prerequisite: Normal serum uric acid is 2–4 mg/dL (<6 mg/dL). While not all hyperuricemic individuals develop gout, persistent hyperuricemia exceeding the 6.8 mg/dL solubility limit at 37°C is required for crystal precipitation.
  • Evolutionary Vulnerability: Most mammals express uricase to oxidize poorly soluble uric acid into water-soluble allantoin; humans lack uricase, leaving uric acid as the terminal purine product.
Secondary Etiologies & Precipitants
  • Cell Turnover & Meds: Secondary hyperuricemia arises from chemotherapy in leukemia/lymphoma (massive purine release) or drugs impairing urate excretion: furosemide, thiazides, pyrazinamide, ethambutol, and low-dose aspirin.
  • Acute Precipitants: Excessive alcohol intake (increases urate synthesis and blocks renal clearance via lactate) and purine-rich diets (red meat) trigger flares.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Keshab Paudel, BDS, MD)

“Hyperuricemia does not always lead to gout, but gout is always preceded by hyperuricemia—humans lack uricase, which in other mammals converts uric acid to soluble allantoin.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A hypertensive patient started on hydrochlorothiazide or furosemide develops acute first-toe pain because diuretics compete with urate at renal organic anion transporters, pushing serum urate above 6.8 mg/dL.

LO 3.02 • Synovitis & Microscopy

Podagra, Inflammatory Cascade & MSU vs. CPPD Microscopy

Classic Presentation Nocturnal 1st MTP podagra (50%)
Pathogenesis Loop MSU phagocytosis → IL-1 & PG → PMNs
Gout Microscopy Needle-shaped, negatively birefringent
Pseudogout Contrast Rhomboid CPPD, positively birefringent
Chronic Sequelae Subcutaneous tophi & urate stones

Nocturnal Precipitation, Neutrophil Amplification & Crystal Polarimetry

LO 3.02 • Slides 14–15
Acute Flare & Chronic Sequelae
  • Why the Big Toe at Night: Acute gout strikes at night or early morning in the 1st metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint (podagra, ~50%) because cooler peripheral temperatures and nocturnal temperature drops lower urate solubility.
  • Inflammatory Amplification: Synoviocytes and mononuclear phagocytes engulf MSU crystals, releasing prostaglandins, lysosomal enzymes, and IL-1, recruiting polymorphonuclear cells (PMNs) that amplify synovitis, fever, and erythema.
  • Chronic Gout: Repeated deposits form chalk-like subcutaneous tophi, joint deformities, and urate lithiasis (kidney stones).
Gout vs. Pseudogout Confirmation
  • MSU (Gout): Synovial fluid shows sharp needle-like crystals that are negatively birefringent (yellow under parallel polarized light, blue under perpendicular light) with hyperuricemia.
  • CPPD (Pseudogout): Calcium pyrophosphate deposition typically targets the knee joint without hyperuricemia, exhibiting rhomboid / round-edged crystals that are positively birefringent (blue parallel, yellow perpendicular).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Keshab Paudel, BDS, MD)

“Remember the memory hook: yeLLow under paraLLel polarized light = negatively birefringent needle-shaped monosodium urate crystals in gout; pseudogout has rounder calcium pyrophosphate crystals and no hyperuricemia.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A nocturnal red, hot 1st MTP joint yields intracellular needle-shaped yellow crystals parallel to the compensator; synovial macrophages triggered this flare via IL-1 release and PMN chemotaxis.

Acute Gout Flare Management & Countermeasures

Lecturer Rule: Never use aspirin for acute gout; never alter ULT mid-flare

Interactive Figure 4: Acute Gout Flare Clinical Triage & Pathway Circuit

LO 4.01 • Acute NSAIDs

High-Dose NSAIDs (Indomethacin) & Low-Dose Aspirin Trap

First-Line Agents Indomethacin, naproxen, diclofenac
Indomethacin Bonus Inhibits COX + crystal phagocytosis
CNS Side Effect Frontal headache (indomethacin)
Contraindicated Drug Low-dose aspirin (URAT1 retention)

Rapid COX Suppression vs. Salicylate Urate Retention

LO 4.01 • Slides 17, 24 & Transcript
High-Dose NSAID Pharmacology
  • Primary Flare Action: High-dose NSAIDs (indomethacin, naproxen, diclofenac) rapidly inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX), halting prostaglandin-driven synovitis and pain.
  • Dual Action of Indomethacin: Indomethacin is a potent anti-inflammatory that also directly inhibits urate crystal phagocytosis, making it a classic replacement for colchicine.
  • Adverse Profile: Monitor for GI ulceration, nephrotoxicity, and indomethacin-associated frontal headache / dizziness.
Why Aspirin Fails in Acute Gout
  • Low-Dose Aspirin Paradox: Low-dose aspirin is strictly contraindicated for acute gout pain because salicylate enhances proximal tubular urate reabsorption (URAT1 uric acid retention), worsening hyperuricemia.
  • Probenecid Antagonism: Salicylates also directly antagonize uricosuric excretion by probenecid.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Keshab Paudel, BDS, MD)

“Indomethacin is favored in acute gout because besides potently blocking COX, it inhibits urate crystal phagocytosis—and remember, aspirin is not indicated to manage acute gout pain.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Treating acute podagra with low-dose aspirin worsens the flare because low-dose salicylates stimulate URAT1-mediated urate reabsorption; switch to indomethacin or naproxen.

LO 4.02 • Cytoskeletal Blockade

Colchicine — Microtubule Inhibition & Narrow Therapeutic Index

Molecular Target Binds intracellular beta-tubulin
Cellular Arrest Blocks neutrophil chemotaxis & inflammasome
Dose-Limiting Toxicity Severe diarrhea & vomiting
Drug Interactions CYP3A4 & P-glycoprotein inhibitors
Contraindication Severe renal or hepatic impairment

Interrupting Neutrophil Migration Without Blocking COX or Urate Synthesis

LO 4.02 • Slides 17, 24 & Practice Q3
Microtubule Disruption
  • Cytoskeletal Binding: Colchicine binds intracellular beta-tubulin, preventing polymerization of tubulin dimers into functional microtubules.
  • Anti-Inflammatory Result: Without microtubules, granulocytes/neutrophils cannot migrate (chemotaxis arrest), phagocytose MSU crystals, or assemble the inflammasome—abruptly terminating the acute flare.
  • Dual Gout Role: Used both for acute flares and at low doses as prophylaxis when initiating urate-lowering therapy.
Toxicity & Clearance Pitfalls
  • GI Warning Sign: Its narrow therapeutic index makes dose-limiting diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting (due to increased gut motility and mucosal turnover arrest) the earliest toxicity threshold.
  • Metabolic Clearance Traps: Co-administration with strong CYP3A4 or P-glycoprotein inhibitors precipitates severe colchicine toxicity (myopathy, marrow suppression).
  • Organ Contraindication: Avoid or contraindicated in severe renal or hepatic impairment where clearance fails.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Keshab Paudel, BDS, MD)

“Colchicine binds tubulin to stop neutrophil chemotaxis and phagocytosis of urate crystals, but its most common adverse effect is significant diarrhea and vomiting from increased gut motility.”

Second-Order Board Clue

An acute gout patient develops watery diarrhea after starting a drug that arrests mitotic/cytoskeletal spindle assembly; that agent binds beta-tubulin and must be avoided with CYP3A4/P-gp inhibitors.

LO 4.03 • Steroids & ULT Rule

Corticosteroids in CKD & The Golden Mid-Flare ULT Rule

Preferred Population CKD or NSAID/colchicine contraindicated
Pathway Suppressed Both prostaglandins & leukotrienes
Systemic Side Effects HPA suppression, hyperglycemia, HTN
Critical ULT Rule NEVER start, stop, or alter ULT mid-flare

Broad Inflammatory Rescue & Preventing Paradoxical Crystal Mobilization

LO 4.03 • Slides 17, 24 & Transcript
Steroid Rescue in Renal Disease
  • Dual Eicosanoid Suppression: Oral or intra-articular corticosteroids inhibit both prostaglandin and leukotriene synthesis alongside multiple cytokine pathways.
  • Renal/GI Safe Harbor: Ideal first-line acute alternative in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) or active peptic ulcer disease who cannot tolerate NSAIDs or colchicine.
  • Adverse Profile: Chronic or repeated exposure causes HPA axis suppression, hyperglycemia, hypertension, opportunistic infections, and osteoporosis.
Golden Rule for Urate-Lowering Therapy
  • Do Not Touch ULT Mid-Flare: NEVER start, stop, or adjust the dose of allopurinol, febuxostat, or probenecid during an acute gout attack.
  • Mechanistic Rationale: Abrupt rises or drops in serum uric acid destabilize synovial urate crystal surfaces, shedding microcrystals that paradoxically intensify and prolong the acute flare.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Keshab Paudel, BDS, MD)

“Pharmacologic management differs sharply between acute flares and chronic prevention: corticosteroids suppress both PGs and leukotrienes during flares, while xanthine oxidase inhibitors are reserved for chronic maintenance.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A patient with Stage 4 CKD and acute podagra should receive corticosteroids rather than indomethacin or colchicine—and existing allopurinol must be continued unchanged.

LO 5.01 • XO Inhibitors

Allopurinol (Oxypurinol) & Febuxostat — Synthesis Inhibition

Allopurinol Target Competitive xanthine oxidase inhibitor
Active Metabolite Oxypurinol (alloxanthine)
SJS / TEN Genetic Risk HLA-B*5801 (Han Chinese/Thai/Korean)
Lethal Interaction Azathioprine / 6-MP (reduce dose ~75%)
Febuxostat Warning Non-purine XO; black-box CV death

Blocking Hypoxanthine-to-Urate Conversion & Purine Antimetabolite Toxicity

LO 5.01 • Slides 24 & Practice Q4
Allopurinol & Oxypurinol Kinetics
  • First-Line Urate Lowering: Allopurinol is converted by xanthine oxidase into its long-acting active metabolite oxypurinol (alloxanthine); both inhibit xanthine oxidase, blocking hypoxanthine → xanthine → uric acid and accumulating more water-soluble hypoxanthine for renal excretion.
  • Hypersensitivity & Pharmacogenomics: Skin rash is the most common adverse effect; screen for HLA-B*5801 in Han Chinese, Thai, and Korean patients to prevent fatal Stevens-Johnson syndrome / TEN.
  • Lethal Azathioprine / 6-MP DDI: Xanthine oxidase inactivates 6-mercaptopurine (6-MP) and azathioprine; co-administration with allopurinol causes life-threatening myelosuppression unless the immunosuppressant dose is drastically reduced (by ~67–75%, or to 1/3–1/4 dose; noted as reduced by 1/3 on slide summary).
Febuxostat Alternative & CV Risk
  • Non-Purine Selectivity: Febuxostat is a structurally non-purine selective xanthine oxidase inhibitor reserved for patients intolerant of or allergic to allopurinol.
  • Black-Box Cardiovascular Warning: Carries a black-box warning for increased risk of cardiovascular death compared with allopurinol, requiring careful cardiac risk assessment.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Keshab Paudel, BDS, MD)

“Allopurinol is converted to active oxypurinol to inhibit xanthine oxidase and raise soluble hypoxanthine; if paired with azathioprine, reduce the azathioprine dose sharply to prevent severe toxicity.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A transplant patient on azathioprine develops severe pancytopenia after starting allopurinol because xanthine oxidase blockade prevents 6-mercaptopurine inactivation.

LO 5.02 • Uricosurics

Probenecid — URAT1/OAT4 Blockade & OAT1/3 Interactions

Tubular Target Inhibits proximal URAT1 / OAT4
Renal Threshold Requires CrCl >50 mL/min
Stone Contraindication Avoid in nephrolithiasis / high urine urate
OAT1/3 Interactions Raises penicillin, cephalosporin, MTX
Salicylate Antagonism Aspirin blocks uricosuric action

Blocking Proximal Urate Reabsorption While Delaying Organic Acid Secretion

LO 5.02 • Slides 24, Transcript & Podcast
Renal Transport Mechanism
  • Uricosuric Action: Probenecid inhibits proximal tubular URAT1 and OAT4 transporters, blocking uric acid reabsorption and promoting net renal urate excretion.
  • Renal Function Gate: Requires intact tubular function (CrCl >50 mL/min); ineffective and contraindicated in moderate-to-severe renal impairment.
  • Nephrolithiasis Risk: Floods urine with uric acid—contraindicated in patients with prior uric acid kidney stones unless urine is aggressively alkalinized and hydrated.
Classic Drug-Drug Interactions
  • OAT1/3 Secretion Blockade: Probenecid competitively inhibits renal organic anion secretion (OAT1/3), prolonging half-lives and raising serum levels of penicillins, cephalosporins, and methotrexate.
  • Aspirin Blockade: Co-administered aspirin completely abolishes probenecid's uricosuric efficacy and must be avoided.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Keshab Paudel, BDS, MD)

“Probenecid carries two critical warnings: aspirin completely blocks its uricosuric effect, it fails below a CrCl of 50 mL/min, and it delays renal excretion of penicillins and cephalosporins.”

Second-Order Board Clue

A patient with chronic gout and a history of urate nephrolithiasis or CrCl 38 mL/min must NOT receive probenecid; choose a xanthine oxidase inhibitor instead.

LO 5.03 • Uricases & Prophylaxis

Pegloticase, Rasburicase & Mandatory 3–6 Month Flare Prophylaxis

Enzymatic Action Converts uric acid → soluble allantoin
Clinical Niche Refractory tophaceous gout / TLS
Absolute Contraindication G6PD deficiency (H2O2 hemolysis)
Immunogenicity Risk Infusion reactions & anti-drug antibodies
ULT Prophylaxis Rule Low-dose colchicine/NSAID × 3–6 mo

Replacing Missing Human Uricase & Shielding ULT Initiation

LO 5.03 • Slides 24, Practice Q5 & Transcript
Recombinant Uricase Pharmacology
  • Biologic Restoration: Pegloticase (pegylated recombinant porcine uricase) is reserved for severe refractory tophaceous gout failing allopurinol; rasburicase targets acute hyperuricemia in tumor lysis syndrome. Both oxidize uric acid directly to water-soluble allantoin.
  • G6PD Contraindication: Oxidation of uric acid generates hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), causing severe hemolytic anemia and methemoglobinemia in patients with G6PD deficiency (absolute contraindication).
  • Infusion Reactions: Pegloticase requires IV monitoring for anaphylaxis/infusion reactions and loss of response from anti-drug antibodies.
Paradoxical Mobilization Prophylaxis
  • Why ULT Triggers Flares: Starting allopurinol, febuxostat, or probenecid rapidly lowers serum urate, causing existing articular tophi and crystal microdeposits to remodel and shed into synovial fluid.
  • Mandatory Co-Prescription: Always co-prescribe low-dose colchicine or an NSAID for 3–6 months when initiating ULT until serum urate stabilizes below target.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Keshab Paudel, BDS, MD)

“Pegloticase is the pharmaceutical answer to our evolutionary lack of uricase for refractory tophaceous gout, while initiating any urate-lowering drug requires low-dose colchicine or NSAID prophylaxis.”

Second-Order Board Clue

Before infusing pegloticase or rasburicase to convert uric acid to allantoin, screen for G6PD deficiency because enzymatic H2O2 production triggers acute oxidative hemolysis and methemoglobinemia.

Topical Capsaicin
OA Topical Adjunct		 Binds TRPV1 (vanilloid-1) receptor on sensory C-fibers Depletes substance P; transient initial local burning sensation Topical cream for localized hand/knee OA pain Isolated from hot peppers; decreases nociceptive transmission peripherally Analgesia via axonal substance P depletion
Duloxetine & Tramadol
OA Central Adjuncts		 Duloxetine: SNRI (NE/5-HT reuptake block); Tramadol: weak opioid + monoamine Modulates descending CNS pain pathways; tramadol carries opioid CNS risks Added when acetaminophen and NSAIDs fail to control chronic OA pain Duloxetine is useful for chronic musculoskeletal pain; tramadol is third-tier Consider duloxetine in refractory OA + comorbid mood/neuropathic features
Hyaluronic Acid
OA Viscosupplement		 Restores endogenous synovial fluid viscosity & lubrication Variable clinical efficacy; higher cost than steroid injections Intra-articular knee injection for prolonged symptom control More expensive than steroids but can maintain relief longer in selected patients Lubricates articular surfaces without systemic or PLA2 steroid effects
Gout (MSU) vs. Pseudogout (CPPD)
Crystal Diagnostics		 Lack of uricase + urate >6.8 mg/dL → MSU phagocytosis → IL-1 & PMNs Nocturnal 1st MTP podagra (50%), tophi, urate stones; CPPD favors knee Gout: needle, negatively birefringent (yellow parallel); CPPD: rhomboid blue parallel Triggers: alcohol, red meat, chemo, furosemide, thiazides, PZA, ethambutol Remember yeLLow under paraLLel light = MSU gout crystals
Indomethacin & Acute NSAIDs
Acute Gout First-Line		 Inhibits COX; indomethacin also inhibits urate crystal phagocytosis Rapid flare relief; GI bleed, AKI, frontal headache (indomethacin) High-dose oral regimen for acute flare with normal renal/GI status Commonly replaces colchicine; low-dose aspirin is contraindicated Low-dose aspirin causes URAT1 uric acid retention
Colchicine
Acute Gout / Prophylaxis		 Binds beta-tubulin → blocks microtubule polymerization → halts PMN chemotaxis & inflammasome Dose-limiting diarrhea, vomiting, narrow therapeutic index Acute flares + 3–6 mo ULT prophylaxis; avoid in severe renal/hepatic failure Most common side effect is watery diarrhea from increased gut motility Toxic accumulation with CYP3A4 & P-gp inhibitors
Systemic / IA Corticosteroids
Acute Gout in CKD		 Suppresses both prostaglandins & leukotrienes + cytokine cascades HPA suppression, hyperglycemia, HTN, infections, osteoporosis Preferred acute flare therapy in CKD or GI ulcer patients Rule: NEVER start, stop, or adjust ULT during an active flare Safe flare bridge in Stage 4 CKD where NSAIDs/colchicine fail safety
Allopurinol
Chronic ULT First-Line		 Competitive xanthine oxidase inhibitor via active oxypurinol; ↑ soluble hypoxanthine Skin rash / SJS-TEN (HLA-B*5801); marrow toxicity with thiopurines Chronic ULT; screen Han Chinese/Thai/Korean; co-give 3–6 mo prophylaxis Reduces uric acid synthesis; reduce azathioprine/6-MP dose sharply (~75%) Blocks XO inactivation of azathioprine / 6-mercaptopurine
Febuxostat
Non-Purine XO Inhibitor		 Selective non-purine xanthine oxidase inhibition Alternative when allopurinol-intolerant; black-box CV death warning Oral chronic ULT; co-prescribe 3–6 mo flare prophylaxis Useful in allopurinol hypersensitivity if cardiovascular risk is low Monitor for higher cardiovascular mortality vs allopurinol
Probenecid
Chronic Uricosuric		 Inhibits proximal tubule URAT1 / OAT4 → blocks urate reabsorption Ineffective if CrCl <50 mL/min; triggers urate stones Inhibits OAT1/3 raising penicillin, cephalosporin, MTX; blocked by aspirin Contraindicated in nephrolithiasis and renal insufficiency Aspirin abolishes uricosuric action; boosts penicillin levels
Pegloticase & Rasburicase
Recombinant Uricase		 Converts insoluble uric acid directly into water-soluble allantoin Infusion reactions; hemolysis & methemoglobinemia in G6PD deficiency Pegloticase: refractory tophaceous gout; Rasburicase: tumor lysis syndrome Pharmaceutical replacement for the uricase enzyme humans evolutionarily lack Absolute contraindication: G6PD deficiency (H2O2 generation)
