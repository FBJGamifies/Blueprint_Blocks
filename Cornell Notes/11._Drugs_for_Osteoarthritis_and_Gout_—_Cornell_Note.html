LO 1.01 • OA Pathology

Osteoarthritis Pathogenesis & Pain Sources

Disease Identity Asymmetric degenerative disorder
Major Risk Factors Age, obesity, trauma, female sex
Radiographic Hallmark Joint space narrowing & osteophytes
True Pain Origin Capsule, periosteum, microfractures
First-Line Pillar Exercise, weight loss, physiotherapy

Structural Cartilage Failure & Multimodal Management Ladder

LO 1.01 • Slides 4–6
Degenerative Mechanism
  • Asymmetric Mechanical Stress: OA is the most common degenerative articular cartilage disorder associated with hypertrophic bone changes. Because mechanical wear targets heavily used or previously injured joints, it presents asymmetrically, driven by advancing age, obesity, past trauma, female sex, and genetics.
  • Chondrocyte Resynthesis Imbalance: Initial cartilage damage stimulates increased chondrocyte activity; however, enzymatic cartilage breakdown persistently outpaces resynthesis, resulting in progressive cartilage loss, joint space narrowing, and compensatory marginal bone spurs (osteophytes).
Nociception & Non-Pharm Care
  • Secondary Nociceptive Activation: Because hyaline cartilage itself is aneural, pain arises from mechanical and chemical irritation of surrounding innervated structures: synovial capsule distention, subchondral microfractures, periosteal irritation, and ligament or meniscus damage.
  • Treatment Ladder: Physical exam reveals pain and limitation on range of motion. Management begins with non-pharmacological pillars—weight loss, low-impact exercise, physiotherapy, and TENS—and advances to joint realignment or total joint replacement for refractory disability.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Paudel) “Think of OA as a structural failure—cartilage has no pain nerves, so the agonizing pain is a secondary alarm bell from bone microfractures, capsule distention, and periosteal irritation.”
Second-Order Board Clue Older patient with asymmetric knee pain worsening with activity and improving with rest, physical exam showing crepitus and limited ROM, and X-ray showing osteophytes and joint space narrowing points directly to osteoarthritis over inflammatory arthritis.

Acetaminophen (APAP) & NAPQI Toxicity

Central Target Brain COX / COX-3
Key Limitation Poor peripheral anti-inflammatory
Toxic Threshold >10 g acute overdose
Toxic Metabolite NAPQI via CYP2E1
Specific Antidote N-acetylcysteine

Central Pain Threshold Elevation & Glutathione Depletion Crisis

LO 1.02 • Slide 7
Pharmacology & Ceiling
  • Central Mechanism: Acetaminophen inhibits cyclooxygenase (COX-3 in the brain) to reduce central PGE2, raising the pain threshold and providing potent antipyretic relief with minimal gastric irritation.
  • Safety vs. Efficacy Trade-Off: Because peripheral action is poor, APAP lacks meaningful anti-inflammatory activity—if joint swelling/inflammation persists, step up to NSAIDs. Chronic overuse risks renal papillary necrosis (analgesic nephropathy); standard ceilings cap at 4 g/day (2–3 g/day in elderly or hepatic impairment).
Overdose & NAC Rescue
  • CYP2E1 Bioactivation: Acute doses >10 g saturate normal conjugation, shunting APAP into hepatic CYP450 2E1 N-hydroxylation (induced by chronic alcohol use) to form reactive N-acetyl para-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI).
  • Antidote Mechanism: NAPQI rapidly depletes hepatic glutathione, causing centrilobular hepatic necrosis, renal tubular necrosis, hypoglycemia, and death. Administer N-acetylcysteine (NAC) (or methionine) to replenish glutathione stores.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Paudel) “Acetaminophen is the safest GI starting point for OA pain, but remember: chronic alcohol induces CYP2E1, accelerating NAPQI buildup and glutathione exhaustion.”
Second-Order Board Clue Patient with chronic alcohol intake presents with nausea, vomiting, hypoglycemia, and transaminitis after supratherapeutic APAP ingestion → immediate therapy restores glutathione via N-acetylcysteine.

Arachidonic Acid Blockade, Gastric/Renal Injury & Topical Sparing

LO 2.01 • Slides 8–10
COX Inhibition & GI/Renal Toxicity
  • Isoform Divergence: Non-selective NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, indomethacin, diclofenac) inhibit both constitutive COX-1 (housekeeping cytoprotection) and inducible COX-2 (inflammatory prostaglandin synthesis).
  • GI & Renal Injury: Loss of COX-1 mediated PGE2 and PGI2 removes gastric mucosal protection (causing peptic ulceration and GI bleeding; warrants PPI co-prescription in high-risk patients) and blocks renal afferent arteriolar vasodilation, causing Na+ and water retention, CHF worsening, AKI, and renal papillary necrosis.
Contraindications & Topical Route
  • Special Warnings: NSAIDs cause bleeding, asthma exacerbation (shunting arachidonic acid toward leukotrienes), pruritus, and confusion. Aspirin specifically causes dizziness/tinnitus and is strictly contraindicated in children with viral infections due to Reye syndrome.
  • Topical Diclofenac: Avoid systemic NSAIDs in peptic ulcer disease, chronic liver disease, CHF, and renal disease; use topical NSAIDs (diclofenac gel) for localized knee or hand OA to minimize systemic exposure.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Paudel) “If you inhibit both COX-1 and COX-2, you relieve joint inflammation, but you risk punching holes in the stomach lining and precipitating sodium retention and papillary necrosis.”
Second-Order Board Clue Child recovering from varicella or influenza given aspirin develops acute encephalopathy and fatty liver → Reye syndrome; asthmatic patient developing bronchospasm after ibuprofen → leukotriene shunting.

The GI-Cardiovascular Trade-Off of Selective COX-2 Inhibition

LO 2.02 • Slide 10 & Practice Q2
Vascular Prostacyclin vs. TXA2
  • Spared Platelet COX-1: Celecoxib selectively blocks inducible COX-2 while sparing constitutive COX-1 in gastric mucosa and platelets, reducing peptic ulcer incidence relative to naproxen or ibuprofen.
  • Prothrombotic Shift: Endothelial cells rely on COX-2 to synthesize vasodilatory, anti-aggregatory prostacyclin (PGI2), whereas platelets rely solely on COX-1 for pro-aggregatory thromboxane A2 (TXA2). Blocking COX-2 leaves TXA2 unopposed.
Clinical Contraindications
  • Major Adverse Events: Selective COX-2 inhibition carries an elevated risk of myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke, mandating extreme caution or avoidance in coronary artery disease and cerebrovascular disease.
  • Shared Renal Risk: Sparing COX-1 does not protect the kidney; celecoxib still causes sodium retention and renal impairment in vulnerable patients.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Paudel) “That is the huge clinical trade-off: when you switch a patient from naproxen to celecoxib for stomach pain, you trade gastric safety for myocardial infarction and stroke risk.”
Second-Order Board Clue 70-year-old OA patient develops epigastric pain on naproxen and is switched to celecoxib → the most serious life-threatening risk to monitor is myocardial infarction.

Upstream Cytokine Blockade, Nociceptive Depletion & Viscosupplementation

LO 2.03 • Slides 11–12
Intra-Articular Steroids & HA
  • Steroid Cascade: Triamcinolone acetonide or methylprednisolone binds cytosolic receptors to upregulate lipocortin-1 (annexin-1), inhibiting phospholipase A2 (PLA2), suppressing COX-2 and cytokines (IL-1, IL-2, IL-6, TNF-α, IFN-γ). Provides short-term relief (4–8 weeks) for acute knee flares; limit to ≤3–4 injections/year to avoid cartilage destruction.
  • Hyaluronic Acid: Restores synovial fluid viscosity and articular lubrication; more expensive than steroids but maintains longer symptom improvement.
Capsaicin, Tramadol & Duloxetine
  • Topical Capsaicin: Binds TRPV1 (vanilloid-1) receptors on nociceptive C-fibers, causing initial burning followed by substance P depletion and reduced pain transmission.
  • Tramadol & Duloxetine: Tramadol (Schedule IV) combines weak μ-opioid agonism with norepinephrine/serotonin reuptake inhibition (risks: seizures, serotonin syndrome, abuse). Duloxetine is a pure SNRI enhancing descending pain inhibition without inhibiting inflammation.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Paudel) “Intra-articular corticosteroids act like an upstream chemical sledgehammer via annexin-1 and PLA2 blockade for 4–8 weeks of flare relief, whereas hyaluronic acid lubricates longer.”
Second-Order Board Clue Patient on an SSRI given tramadol for refractory OA develops clonus, hyperthermia, and agitation → serotonin syndrome (also lowers seizure threshold).

LO 3.01 • Gout Pathogenesis

Hyperuricemia, Uricase Loss & Podagra

Evolutionary Defect Human uricase deficiency
Saturation Threshold Serum urate >6.8 mg/dL
Classic Acute Site 1st MTP joint (Podagra)
Secondary Triggers Thiazides, loop diuretics, chemo
Inflammatory Cascade IL-1, PGs & PMN influx

Metabolic Urate Crystallization & Synovial Amplification Loop

LO 3.01 • Slides 13–15
Evolutionary Basis & Triggers
  • Uricase Pseudogene: Most mammals possess uricase (urate oxidase) on chromosome 1p22 to convert poorly soluble uric acid into soluble allantoin; humans lack functional uricase. Normal serum urate is 2–4 mg/dL (<6 mg/dL); exceeding the solubility limit (>6.8 mg/dL) precipitates monosodium urate (MSU) crystals.
  • Acute Triggers & Secondary Causes: Hyperuricemia always precedes gout. Acute attacks strike at night/early morning—50% in the 1st metatarsophalangeal joint (podagra) where lower peripheral temperature lowers MSU solubility Ksp—precipitated by alcohol or purine-rich red meat. Secondary causes include leukemia/lymphoma chemotherapy, furosemide, thiazides, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol.
Acute & Chronic Tissue Pathology
  • Phagocytic Amplification: Synoviocytes and mononuclear phagocytes engulf MSU crystals, releasing IL-1, prostaglandins, and lysosomal enzymes, driving chemotaxis and massive polymorphonuclear (PMN) neutrophil infiltration into the joint space.
  • Chronic Sequelae: Persistent crystal deposition produces chalk-like subcutaneous tophi, destructive joint deformities, and radiolucent uric acid kidney stones (urate lithiasis).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Paudel) “Hyperuricemia doesn’t always cause gout, but gout is always preceded by hyperuricemia—because humans lack the uricase enzyme that other mammals use to excrete allantoin.”
Second-Order Board Clue Patient started on hydrochlorothiazide or pyrazinamide wakes up at 3 AM with an erythematous, exquisitely tender 1st MTP joint → drug-induced hyperuricemia triggering podagra.

Compensator Polarized Microscopy & Classic Radiographic Signatures

LO 3.02 • Slide 14
Synovial Fluid Microscopy
  • Gout (MSU): Demonstrates sharp needle-like crystals exhibiting intense negative birefringence—appearing yellow when parallel to the red compensator slow axis and blue when perpendicular. Always linked to hyperuricemia.
  • Pseudogout (CPPD): Caused by calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate deposition, classically targeting the knee joint without hyperuricemia. Crystals appear rhomboid or round-edged under microscopy with weakly positive birefringence (blue when parallel).
Radiographic & Therapeutic Link
  • X-Ray Signatures: Chronic gout produces classic punched-out ("rat-bite") marginal erosions with overhanging sclerotic cortical edges, whereas CPPD exhibits linear calcification of articular fibrocartilage known as chondrocalcinosis.
  • Clinical Crossover: Although pseudogout does not respond to urate-lowering drugs, its acute flare treatment mirrors acute gout (NSAIDs, colchicine, or corticosteroids).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Paudel) “Lock in the mnemonic: MSU crystals are sharp needles and negatively birefringent—yeLLow under paraLLel polarized light, blue under perpendicular—whereas CPPD has rounded/rhomboid edges in the knee.”
Second-Order Board Clue Elderly patient with acute knee swelling, normal serum uric acid, linear meniscal calcification (chondrocalcinosis), and rhomboid weakly positive crystals → pseudogout (CPPD); manage acutely like gout, never start allopurinol.

Acute Anti-Inflammatory Choices & Paradoxical Flare Triggers

LO 4.01 • Slides 17–19
Indomethacin & Steroid Alternatives
  • Indomethacin (NSAIDs): High-dose NSAIDs (indomethacin, naproxen, diclofenac) have largely replaced colchicine. Beyond inhibiting COX, indomethacin uniquely inhibits urate crystal phagocytosis by PMNs. Watch for potent CNS toxicity (severe frontal headache, dizziness, confusion) and avoid in renal failure, active PUD, or CHF.
  • Corticosteroids: Oral prednisone burst or intra-articular steroids are first-line when NSAIDs and colchicine are contraindicated (severe CKD or decompensated heart failure). Long-term systemic risks include HPA suppression, hyperglycemia, hypertension, osteoporosis, and opportunistic infections.
Two Critical Acute Flare Pitfalls
  • No Low-Dose Aspirin: Low-dose aspirin competitively inhibits uric acid secretion at proximal tubular URAT1 / OAT transporters, causing paradoxical hyperuricemia and worsening gout.
  • Never Start/Alter ULT Mid-Flare: Initiating or changing allopurinol, febuxostat, or probenecid during an acute flare triggers rapid serum urate drops, promoting crystal dissolution and surface shedding that prolongs and worsens the attack. Wait 2–4 weeks post-resolution (keep stable if already on chronic ULT).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Paudel) “Indomethacin is favored over colchicine because it suppresses COX and stops urate phagocytosis—and never forget that low-dose aspirin causes hyperuricemia.”
Second-Order Board Clue Patient with acute podagra and Stage 4 CKD (eGFR 22 mL/min) cannot receive NSAIDs or full-dose colchicine → administer intra-articular or oral corticosteroids and delay allopurinol until flare resolves.
LO 4.02 • Colchicine

Colchicine: Microtubule Arrest & Narrow Window

Molecular Target Intracellular tubulin heterodimers

Tubulin Polymerization Blockade, Prophylaxis & Drug-Toxicity Warnings

LO 4.02 • Slides 18–19 & Practice Q3
Microtubule & Inflammasome MOA
  • Tubulin Polymerization Block: Colchicine binds intracellular tubulin, preventing polymerization into microtubules and disrupting mitotic spindles. This arrests granulocyte migration, urate crystal phagocytosis, degranulation, and suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome activation.
  • Prophylactic Coverage: Used both for acute flares (especially within 24–36 hours) and as prophylaxis for 3–6 months when initiating urate-lowering therapy to prevent mobilization flares.
GI, Chronic & CYP3A4 Toxicities
  • Narrow Therapeutic Index: Short-term use increases gut motility, causing severe cramping, vomiting, and profuse watery diarrhea. Chronic use causes myopathy, neutropenia, aplastic anemia, and alopecia.
  • Fatal Drug Interactions: Colchicine is a CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein substrate; co-administration with strong inhibitors like clarithromycin or azole antifungals (especially in CKD/hepatic disease) causes fatal multi-organ toxicity.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Paudel) “Colchicine shuts down neutrophil recruitment by binding tubulin, and while NSAIDs largely replaced it for acute attacks because of diarrhea, it remains crucial for ULT initiation prophylaxis.”
Second-Order Board Clue Gout patient on maintenance colchicine prescribed clarithromycin for community-acquired pneumonia develops profuse diarrhea, pancytopenia, and rhabdomyolysis → CYP3A4/P-gp inhibition.

Xanthine Oxidase Blockade, Pharmacogenomics & Purine Antimetabolites

LO 5.01 • Slides 21–24 & Practice Q4
Allopurinol & HLA-B*5801
  • Indications & Target: Initiate ULT for ≥2 flares/year, tophi, CKD stage ≥2, or uric acid nephrolithiasis; target serum urate <6.0 mg/dL (<5.0 mg/dL in severe tophaceous gout) with 3–6 months of colchicine/NSAID prophylaxis.
  • Allopurinol Mechanism & AHS: Competitive purine-analog inhibitor of xanthine oxidase (converts hypoxanthine → xanthine → uric acid), generating long-acting non-competitive oxypurinol (alloxanthine) (t½ 15–30 hr). Causes maculopapular rash and potentially fatal allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome (AHS / DRESS); screen HLA-B*5801 before starting in Han Chinese, Thai, Korean, or African descent patients.
6-MP Interaction & Febuxostat
  • Azathioprine / 6-MP Crisis: Azathioprine and 6-mercaptopurine (6-MP) require xanthine oxidase for metabolic inactivation. Co-administration with allopurinol triggers catastrophic myelosuppression and pancytopenia—mandating a 50–75% (lecture notes: reduce by 1/3 to 1/4) dose reduction or alternative therapy.
  • Febuxostat: Non-purine selective xanthine oxidase inhibitor, more potent than allopurinol and useful in allopurinol intolerance; carries an FDA black box warning for increased cardiovascular mortality compared to allopurinol.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Paudel) “Allopurinol is our preferred workhorse via its long-acting metabolite alloxanthine, increasing water-soluble hypoxanthine—always check for skin rash and reduce azathioprine dose drastically.”
Second-Order Board Clue Lupus or IBD patient maintained on azathioprine starts allopurinol and develops severe neutropenia and thrombocytopenia → impaired xanthine oxidase clearance of 6-MP.

Proximal Convoluted Tubule Transport & Stone Prevention

LO 5.02 • Slide 20
Tubular Reabsorption & Contraindications
  • Uricosuric Mechanism: Probenecid (and sulfinpyrazone) inhibits apical URAT1 and OAT4 anion exchangers in the proximal convoluted tubule, blocking urate reabsorption to increase urinary uric acid excretion.
  • Strict Prerequisites: Completely ineffective and contraindicated when CrCl <50 mL/min, and strictly contraindicated in patients with uric acid kidney stones or urate overproduction. Requires vigorous hydration and urine alkalinization (potassium citrate) to prevent intra-tubular stone crystallization.
Basolateral OAT Drug Interactions
  • Prolonged Drug Half-Lives: Probenecid also inhibits basolateral OAT1 and OAT3, reducing renal tubular secretion and increasing duration of action of penicillins, cephalosporins, methotrexate, naproxen, ketoprofen, and indomethacin.
  • Salicylate Blockade: Aspirin directly antagonizes and blocks the uricosuric effect of probenecid.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Paudel) “Probenecid forces uric acid out in the urine, so you must check two things: creatinine clearance must be at least 50 mL/min, and aspirin must be off the med list.”
Second-Order Board Clue Patient taking methotrexate for rheumatoid arthritis starts probenecid and develops mucositis and pancytopenia → impaired OAT1/OAT3 tubular secretion causing MTX toxicity.

Bypassing Human Uricase Deficiency: Allantoin Conversion & G6PD Risk

LO 5.03 • Slides 17, 23 & Practice Q5
Pegloticase in Refractory Gout
  • Mechanism & Route: Pegloticase is a recombinant pegylated mammalian urate oxidase (uricase) administered as an IV infusion every 2 weeks for severe refractory tophaceous gout failing oral XOIs. It converts insoluble uric acid into soluble, inert allantoin.
  • Antibody Monitoring: Carries high risk of infusion reactions and anaphylaxis; formation of anti-drug antibodies causes loss of efficacy, tracked by a rising pre-infusion serum urate >6 mg/dL, triggering immediate discontinuation.
G6PD Crisis & Rasburicase
  • G6PD Contraindication: Enzymatic oxidation of uric acid to allantoin generates hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) as a byproduct. Both pegloticase and rasburicase are strictly contraindicated in G6PD deficiency due to acute oxidative hemolytic anemia and methemoglobinemia.
  • Rasburicase in TLS: Non-pegylated recombinant urate oxidase approved for acute prevention and treatment of hyperuricemia in Tumor Lysis Syndrome (↑K+, ↑phosphate, ↓Ca++, ↑uric acid).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Paudel) “Pegloticase is our pharmaceutical answer to the single human enzyme deficiency—converting uric acid directly into water-soluble allantoin every 2 weeks when allopurinol fails.”
Second-Order Board Clue Lymphoma patient receiving rasburicase for tumor lysis syndrome develops Heinz bodies, bite cells, dark urine, and chocolate-brown blood → unrecognized G6PD deficiency assaulted by H2O2.

Lecture Q3, Q4 & Q5 Solutions

Comorbidity & Pharmacogenomic Traps
  • Acute Podagra + Stage 4 CKD + Immediate ULT Request: Indomethacin and colchicine are unsafe at eGFR 22 mL/min → treat with oral or intra-articular corticosteroids; delay allopurinol 2–4 weeks post-resolution (crystal shedding worsens flares) and cover with prophylaxis.

OA & Acute Gout		 Inhibit constitutive COX-1 & inducible COX-2; indomethacin also blocks urate phagocytosis Peptic ulcer/GI bleed, Na+/water retention, AKI, renal papillary necrosis, tinnitus (ASA) Topical diclofenac for localized OA; PPI co-therapy in GI risk; avoid in PUD, CHF, CKD Indomethacin replaces colchicine in acute gout; aspirin causes Reye syndrome in viral kids NSAID-induced bronchospasm → leukotriene shunting; ASA low-dose worsens gout
Celecoxib
Selective COX-2		 Selectively inhibits COX-2 (↓endothelial PGI2), sparing COX-1 (↑unopposed platelet TXA2) Lower peptic ulcer rate vs. naproxen; retains renal Na+ retention risk Avoid in coronary artery disease or cerebrovascular disease Classic clinical trade-off: stomach safety exchanged for major CV events Switched from naproxen to celecoxib → monitor for myocardial infarction & stroke
Intra-Articular Corticosteroids
OA Flare & Acute Gout		 Bind cytosolic receptors → ↑lipocortin-1 (annexin-1) → ↓PLA2, ↓COX-2, ↓cytokines 4–8 weeks knee OA relief; systemic steroids cause HPA suppression, hyperglycemia, HTN Limit IA knee injections to ≤3–4/yr; first-line acute gout in Stage 4 CKD / CHF Upstream chemical sledgehammer halting both prostaglandins and leukotrienes Acute podagra + eGFR 22 mL/min → intra-articular or oral steroid
Capsaicin, Tramadol & HA
OA Adjuvants		 Capsaicin: TRPV1 → ↓substance P; Tramadol: μ-opioid + SNRI; HA: joint lubricant Tramadol (Schedule IV): seizures, serotonin syndrome; HA: longer duration than steroid Duloxetine (SNRI) for chronic pain without local anti-inflammatory effect Hyaluronic acid is costlier than steroids but sustains symptom relief longer Tramadol + SSRI causing clonus & fever → serotonin syndrome
Colchicine
Acute Gout & Prophylaxis		 Binds tubulin → blocks microtubule polymerization, PMN migration, phagocytosis & NLRP3 Short-term: profuse watery diarrhea; Chronic: myopathy, neutropenia, alopecia Acute gout (<36h) & 3–6 mo prophylaxis when starting ULT; avoid with CYP3A4/P-gp inhibitors Shuts down recruitment phase; replaced by NSAIDs acutely due to GI toxicity Gout + clarithromycin causing pancytopenia & myopathy → CYP3A4/P-gp block
Allopurinol & Febuxostat
Chronic ULT (XOI)		 Inhibit xanthine oxidase (oxypurinol is long-acting); febuxostat is selective non-purine XOI Allopurinol: rash, AHS/DRESS; Febuxostat: FDA black box CV mortality warning Target urate <6 mg/dL; screen HLA-B*5801 (Asian/African); reduce azathioprine/6-MP 50–75% Never start during an acute flare; wait 2–4 weeks with colchicine/NSAID cover Lupus patient on azathioprine started on allopurinol → lethal pancytopenia
Probenecid
Chronic Uricosuric		 Inhibits apical URAT1 / OAT4 (blocks urate reabsorption) & basolateral OAT1/OAT3 Risk of uric acid stones; aspirin blocks uricosuric action Contraindicated if CrCl <50 mL/min or prior urate stones; hydrate + K+ citrate Prolongs half-lives of penicillins, cephalosporins, and methotrexate MTX toxicity after gout pill → probenecid blocking OAT1/OAT3 secretion
Pegloticase & Rasburicase
Recombinant Uricase		 Converts insoluble uric acid → soluble allantoin + H2O2 byproduct Anaphylaxis/infusion reactions; anti-drug antibodies cause urate rebound >6 mg/dL Pegloticase IV q2w for refractory tophaceous gout; Rasburicase for Tumor Lysis Syndrome Replaces the uricase enzyme lost during human evolution Dark urine & bite cells post-rasburicase → G6PD deficiency hemolysis
