LO 1.01 • Bone Anatomy

Long Bone Zones & Connective Tissues

Pediatric Growth Plate Physis (radiolucent on X-ray)
Periosteum Role Attaches ligaments and tendons
Ligament Function Bone-to-bone, restricts motion planes
Tendon Function Muscle-to-bone, transmits forces
Joint Type Synovial joints (greatest motion)

Long Bone Regions, Physis Closure & Viscoelastic Soft Tissues

LO 1.01 • Slides 3–8
Osseous & Physeal Architecture
  • Physis vs. Adult Bone: In a child, the physis sits between the epiphysis (proximal/distal ends) and metaphysis (connecting to the diaphysis shaft) and appears radiolucent on X-ray; in adults it closes into solid bone and length growth stops.
  • Periosteum & Marrow: Dense periosteum anchors ligaments and tendons to the cortex, while the central medullary cavity houses marrow and articular cartilage caps joint surfaces.
  • Synovial Articulations: Most extremity joints are synovial, classified by permitted motion and allowing the greatest mobility.
Ligaments, Tendons & Trauma Definitions
  • Viscoelasticity Limits: Both ligaments and tendons stretch under tension and recoil, yet ligaments fail to regain original length if stretched past a threshold or for a prolonged period.
  • Dislocation vs. Subluxation: Dislocation eliminates all articular surface contact, whereas subluxation preserves partial articular contact.
  • Sprain vs. Strain: A sprain tears joint ligaments forced beyond normal motion planes; a strain tears muscle fibers or tendon from overuse or excessive tension.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hayes)

"Knowing the mechanism of injury is often undervalued—determine energy/speed, feeling of a pop, post-injury function, and neurologic status before ordering at least two radiographic projections."

Second-Order Board Clue

A pediatric radiolucent line between epiphysis and metaphysis or a parallel longitudinal apophysis at the 5th metatarsal base is normal anatomy and must not be misread as a fracture.

LO 1.02 • Growth Plate

Salter-Harris Classification (SALTR)

Most Common Type Salter-Harris Type II (~75%)
Mnemonic Anchor SALTR (Slipped, Above, Lower, Transverse, Rammed)
Worst Prognosis Type V (crush injury)
High-Risk Zones Proliferative and reserve zones (III–V)
Major Complication Premature closure & limb shortening

SALTR Growth Plate Planes, Histology Zones & Trampoline Vignette

LO 1.02 • Slides 12–13 & Board Q1
SALTR Anatomical Planes
  • Type I (Slipped) & II (Above): Type I passes straight across the physis; Type II traverses the physis and exits up through the metaphysis (~75% of cases, good prognosis).
  • Type III (Lower) & IV (Transverse): Type III exits down through the epiphysis; Type IV cuts across metaphysis, physis, and epiphysis (~10% of cases).
  • Type V (Rammed/Ruined): Axial compression crushes the physis, carrying the worst prognosis for growth arrest.
Histologic Risk & Board Vignette
  • Prognostic Gradient: Prognosis worsens from Type I to V because Types III–V injure the proliferative and reserve zones, risking premature physeal closure and limb shortening.
  • Trampoline Case (Board Q1): An 8-year-old girl falls from a trampoline with a tender swollen medial malleolus; X-ray shows fracture through the posterior distal tibia physis and metaphysis = Salter-Harris Type II.
  • X-Ray Orientation Trap: Remember the wrist sits above the hand anatomically—even if a distal radius radiograph is displayed inverted, metaphyseal extension remains Type II (Above).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hayes)

"Remember approximately 75% of Salter-Harris fractures are Type 2. Remember the wrist is above the hand even when the X-ray is oriented opposite."

Second-Order Board Clue

When a fracture plane involves the epiphysis (Types III–V), disruption of the proliferative and reserve zones predisposes the child to physeal bridging and asymmetric growth disturbance.

LO 1.03 • Pediatric Patterns

Torus, Greenstick & Toddler’s Fractures

Torus (Buckle) Outer cortical layer broken
Greenstick Fracture Bend with crack partially through one side
Toddler’s Fracture Non-displaced spiral distal tibia (<8 yrs)
Long Bone Geometry Transverse, oblique, spiral, comminuted

Incomplete Pediatric Fractures & Long Bone Fracture Morphology

LO 1.03 • Slide 18 & Eponym List
Incomplete Pediatric Patterns
  • Torus Fracture: An incomplete pediatric fracture in which only the outer cortical layer is broken/buckled without full-thickness cortical disruption.
  • Greenstick Fracture: Seen in children when bone bends and cracks on one side partially through the shaft while the opposite cortex remains intact.
  • Toddler’s Fracture: A non-displaced spiral fracture of the distal tibia occurring in children under 8 years old following low-energy rotational trauma.
Complete Long Bone Orientations
  • Axis Relationships: Transverse runs perpendicular to the long axis; oblique angles across the shaft; spiral rotates along the axis from torque.
  • Fragment Patterns: Comminuted produces more than two fragments; avulsion pulls off a bone chip; impacted drives bone into a contiguous region.
  • Imaging Baseline: Always obtain at least two projections (AP and lateral) and inspect cortical margins and alignment.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hayes)

"When reading an X-ray, systematically inspect cortical margins, bone alignment, and typical fracture patterns across at least two orthogonal views."

Second-Order Board Clue

A child under age 8 refusing to bear weight after a minor twisting fall with a spiral distal tibial line has a Toddler’s fracture from low-energy rotational torque.

LO 1.04 • Pediatric Elbow

Supracondylar Humeral Fracture & Volkmann Contracture

Anatomical Site Distal transverse humerus above epicondyles
Mechanism Hyperextension (FOOSH in children)
Anteromedial Danger Median nerve & brachial artery
Anterolateral Danger Radial nerve (wrist drop)
Ischemic Sequela Volkmann’s contracture (claw-like hand)

Neurovascular Vectors in Supracondylar Fractures & Forearm Ischemia

LO 1.04 • Slides 28–29 & Board Q10
Fragment Displacement & Nerve Injury
  • Anteromedial Displacement (Board Q10): A 6-year-old girl falls onto an outstretched right arm with a diminished radial pulse; the proximal fragment spikes anteromedially, injuring the median nerve and brachial artery over the medial epicondyle.
  • Median Nerve Deficits: Sensory loss over the palmar first 3 digits and lateral palm plus weak thumb flexion/opposition/abduction and 2nd/3rd digit flexion.
  • Anterolateral vs. Posterior Vectors: Anterolateral proximal displacement injures the radial nerve (wrist-drop); hyperflexion on a flexed elbow displaces posteriorly and threatens the ulnar nerve behind the medial epicondyle.
Brachial Artery & Volkmann Contracture
  • Pediatric Epidemiology: Supracondylar fractures are common in children (rare in adults) and require urgent distal neurovascular exams.
  • Ischemic Sequela: Vascular compromise of the brachial artery and forearm ischemia culminate in Volkmann’s contracture, producing a shortened, deformed claw-like hand.
  • Hume Comparison: Distinguish supracondylar fractures from a pediatric Hume fracture (olecranon fracture with anterior radial head dislocation after hyperextension).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hayes)

"Supracondylar fractures are common in children, rare in adults, and classically linked to Volkmann’s contracture—obtain a true lateral elbow view whenever possible."

Second-Order Board Clue

Diminished radial pulse plus anteromedial displacement of the proximal humeral fragment pinpoints concurrent compression of the brachial artery and median nerve.

LO 1.05 • Toddler Elbow

Nursemaid’s Elbow (Radial Head Subluxation)

Affected Structure Annular ligament entrapment
Age & Mechanism Child <4–5 yrs, sudden axial traction
Arm Posture Adducted, elbow flexed, forearm pronated
Key Negative Clue No visible swelling or deformity
Reduction Maneuver Supination with elbow flexion

Annular Ligament Entrapment & Radiohumeral Reduction

LO 1.05 • Slides 30–31 & Board Q2
Pathophysiology & Clinical Vignette
  • Thin Annular Ligament: In children <4–5 years, the annular ligament anchoring the radial head to the ulna is thin; sudden pulling up on the hand causes it to slip over the radial head and become entrapped in the radiohumeral joint.
  • Board Q2 Presentation: A 3-year-old boy whose sister pulled his arm refuses to move it, holding it against his abdomen with the elbow flexed and forearm pronated and no visible swelling.
Maneuver & Distractor Exclusions
  • Definitive Reduction: Closed reduction is achieved by supination of the forearm with flexion of the elbow (restoring radial head alignment).
  • Rule Out Distractors: Distal biceps tendon rupture causes a mid-upper arm "Popeye" mass in older adults; elbow UCL tears occur in repetitive valgus pitching; RCL tears follow acute varus trauma with fracture/dislocation.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hayes)

"Also called pulled elbow, slipped elbow, or toddler’s elbow—the toddler holds the arm stationary, adducted, and slightly flexed without swelling."

Second-Order Board Clue

Axial traction in a toddler without deformity implicates entrapment of the annular ligament, treated immediately via forearm supination and elbow flexion.

LO 2.01 • Shoulder Girdle

Clavicle Fracture & AC Joint Separation

Weakest Clavicle Zone Middle one-third (most common)
Distal Fragment Pull Deltoid muscle (inferolateral)
Proximal Fragment Pull SCM and trapezius (superomedial)
AC Type III Hallmark Disrupted AC + coracoclavicular ligaments
Clavicle Treatment Figure-of-eight splint or sling

Clavicular Muscle Displacement Vectors & AC Separation Grades I–III

LO 2.01 • Slides 35–36, 39 & Board Q7
Clavicle Mechanics & Muscle Pull
  • Epidemiology & Site: Clavicle fractures are the most common shoulder injury in children/adolescents after falling horizontally onto the shoulder or an outstretched hand; the middle third is weakest and most often fractured.
  • Distal Clavicle Vector (Board Q7): A 3-year-old boy falls off a jungle gym onto outstretched arms with anterior shoulder crepitus; the deltoid (plus arm weight) pulls the distal fragment inferolaterally while the sternocleidomastoid and trapezius pull the proximal fragment superomedially.
  • Management: Middle-third fractures rarely cause neurovascular injury and heal non-operatively with a figure-of-eight splint or sling.
Acromioclavicular (AC) Separation Grading
  • Type I: Partial tear of the acromioclavicular ligament with no radiographic displacement.
  • Type II: Complete disruption of the AC ligament causing widening of the AC joint; usually from a downward blow to the shoulder tip without nerve injury.
  • Type III: Disruption of both the acromioclavicular and coracoclavicular ligaments, widening both joints (seen on AP stress view).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hayes)

"Age predicts shoulder trauma: clavicle fractures predominate in children/adolescents, shoulder dislocations and AC separations in middle age, and proximal humerus fractures in older adults."

Second-Order Board Clue

Inferolateral displacement of a distal clavicle fragment after a childhood FOOSH is driven by tone of the anterior deltoid combined with upper-extremity weight.

LO 2.02 • Glenohumeral Joint

Anterior vs. Posterior Shoulder Dislocation

Anterior Incidence 95% (abduction + external rotation)
Nerve at Risk Axillary nerve (deltoid paralysis)
Impaction / Avulsion Hill-Sachs & Bankart lesions
Posterior Views Axillary or scapular Y view

Glenohumeral Instability, Axillary Neuropathy & Impaction Eponyms

LO 2.02 • Slides 37–38, Eponyms & Board Q8
Anterior Dislocation & Axillary Nerve
  • Mechanism & Exam (Board Q8): A 20-year-old quarterback tackled mid-throw (abducted, extended, externally rotated arm) develops flattening of the deltoid, acromion prominence, anterior axillary fullness, and pinprick insensitivity over the lateral shoulder.
  • Neuropathy & Reduction: Axillary nerve injury paralyzes the deltoid and teres minor and numbs lateral shoulder skin; plain X-ray confirms diagnosis and closed reduction typically restores nerve function.
  • Osseous Eponyms: Bankart fracture avulses the inferior anterior glenoid labrum; Hill-Sachs fracture impacts and compresses the posterior humeral head against the glenoid rim.
Posterior Dislocation & Rotator Cuff
  • Posterior Mechanism: Uncommon; caused by direct anterior force or indirect adduction, extension, and internal rotation.
  • Radiographic Trap: Posterior dislocation may be missed on a standard AP view—order a special axillary projection and/or scapular Y view.
  • Associated Fracture Alert: Shoulder dislocations accompanied by a greater tuberosity fracture represent a key orthopedic emergency scenario; soft tissue/cartilage injuries require MRI or arthrography.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hayes)

"95% of shoulder dislocations are anterior; posterior dislocations can look deceptively normal on an AP film, so always add an axillary or scapular Y view."

Second-Order Board Clue

Lateral shoulder sensory loss and deltoid flattening after a throwing tackle signify axillary nerve stretch from an anterior glenohumeral dislocation rather than an AC separation.

Soft-Tissue Fat Pad Clues, Holstein-Lewis Nerve Risk & Metastatic Bone Lesions

LO 2.03 • Slides 17, 27, 32–34 & Eponyms
Elbow Fat Pads & Humeral Shaft
  • Indirect Intra-Articular Sign: When an osseous fracture line is subtle on a true lateral elbow film, elevated anterior and posterior fat pad signs strongly point to an occult radial head fracture (confirm with oblique view).
  • Radial Nerve Entrapment: A midshaft spiral humerus fracture or distal-third Holstein-Lewis fracture traps or injures the radial nerve, causing wrist-drop.
  • Outcome Gradient: Proximal and midshaft humeral fractures generally heal well (sling/splint/brace), whereas distal fractures carry worse functional outcomes.
Pathologic Fractures & Bone Metastases
  • Pathologic Mechanism: Minor or no trauma fractures bone weakened by osteoporosis, osteomalacia (vitamin D lack), osteomyelitis, Paget disease, osteogenesis imperfecta, or tumors.
  • Metastatic Profiles: Most adult malignant bone tumors are metastatic—breast cancer in women (lytic lesions; lecture case showed elevated Ca²⁺ and alkaline phosphatase), prostate cancer in men (blastic lesions), and lung cancer 2nd in both sexes.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hayes)

"Soft tissue abnormalities like anterior and posterior fat pad signs are valuable indirect clues that can be diagnostic when osseous injuries are not apparent, especially in the elbow."

Second-Order Board Clue

A minimally traumatic humeral fracture in a woman with lytic lesions, hypercalcemia, and elevated alkaline phosphatase indicates metastatic breast cancer.

LO 2.04 • Forearm & Wrist

Galeazzi, Monteggia, Colles, Smith & Barton

Galeazzi (GRU) Radius fx + DRUJ dislocation
Monteggia (MUR) Ulna fx + radial head dislocation
Colles Fracture Distal radius with dorsal displacement

Forearm Coupling Mnemonic ("GRUesome MURder") & Distal Radius Eponyms

LO 2.04 • Slides 25–26 & Eponym List
Forearm Fracture-Dislocations
  • Galeazzi (GRU): Fracture of the distal-third radius shaft paired with dislocation of the distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ) after a blow to the forearm.
  • Monteggia (MUR): Proximal ulna fracture paired with radial head dislocation; remember GRUesome MURder (first bone fractured, second dislocated).
  • Essex-Lopresti: Comminuted radial head fracture with interosseous membrane disruption and DRUJ subluxation after a fall from height.
Distal Radius Eponyms
  • Colles Fracture: Extra-articular distal radius bent backwards (dorsal displacement) from FOOSH; classic in elderly women with osteoporosis and 50% associated with an ulnar styloid fracture.
  • Smith & Barton: Smith fracture causes volar displacement after falling onto a flexed wrist; Barton fracture involves the articular surface with radiocarpal dislocation.
  • Chauffeur’s Fracture: Intra-articular fracture of the radial styloid from forced ulnar deviation of the wrist.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hayes)

"Elbow evaluation is unnecessary in isolated distal forearm injury without elbow symptoms, unless the patient has distracting injuries or is unresponsive/incommunicative."

Second-Order Board Clue

Whenever one forearm bone shaft fractures, inspect both the proximal and distal joints: a radial shaft fracture demands ruling out DRUJ dislocation (Galeazzi).

LO 2.05 • Carpal & Hand

Scaphoid, Bennett, Rolando, Boxer’s & Skier’s Thumb

Scaphoid Complication Avascular necrosis (retrograde flow)
Snuffbox Tenderness Between EPL and EPB/APL tendons
Bennett vs. Rolando Palmar beak vs. comminuted 1st MC base
Boxer’s Splint 5th MC neck → ulnar gutter splint
Gamekeeper’s Thumb Ulnar collateral ligament tear/avulsion

Anatomical Snuffbox Retrograde Vascularity & Metacarpal/Thumb Eponyms

LO 2.05 • Slides 22–24 & Board Q9
Scaphoid & Snuffbox Borders (Board Q9)
  • Clinical Vignette (Board Q9): A 56-year-old man falls forward walking his dog onto an outstretched hand (dorsiflexion/extreme ulnar deviation) with tenderness between extensor pollicis longus (medial border) and abductor pollicis longus / extensor pollicis brevis (lateral border); proximal = radial styloid, floor = scaphoid & trapezium.
  • AVN Mechanism: Initial X-rays may be normal; the dorsal scaphoid branch of the radial artery enters distally and flows in a retrograde direction, so fracture risks proximal pole avascular necrosis and nonunion.
  • Wrist Canal Contrasts: Lunate palmar dislocation causes carpal tunnel syndrome (median nerve); hook of hamate fracture causes Guyon canal syndrome (ulnar nerve).
Hand & Thumb Injuries (20× Carpal Fx)
  • Bennett vs. Rolando: Bennett fracture (most frequent thumb fx) is an oblique intra-articular fx-dislocation of the 1st metacarpal base with a palmar beak fragment held by the palmar beak ligament (axial load/fist fight); Rolando is a comminuted 1st MC base fx.
  • Boxer’s Fracture (20% of hand fx): Transverse 5th metacarpal neck (or 4th) fracture from punching a solid object; treated with reduction + ulnar gutter splint, K-wire, or plate fixation.
  • Gamekeeper’s / Skier’s Thumb: Sprain or avulsion of the ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) from the thumb proximal phalanx base (ski pole/volleyball/FOOSH).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hayes)

"Phalangeal and metacarpal fractures occur 20 times more often than carpal injuries—yet a missed scaphoid fracture with normal initial X-rays destroys proximal pole blood supply."

Second-Order Board Clue

Snuffbox tenderness with a normal initial wrist film requires immobilization because interrupting retrograde radial artery perfusion causes proximal avascular necrosis.

Osteoporotic Load-Bearing Fractures & Axial Skeleton Eponyms

LO 3.01 • Slides 10, 34 & Eponym List
Osteoporotic & Pathologic Axial Trauma
  • Vertebral Compression Fracture: Common spine fracture affecting up to 50% of people >80 years old; diagnosed on lateral radiographs (MRI or bone scan gauges acuity) and managed with observation and pain management.
  • Weakened Load-Bearing Sites: Osteoporosis predominantly causes pathologic fractures at the pelvis/sacrum, proximal femur, and thoraco-lumbar transition.
Spine & Pelvis Eponyms (Source List)
  • Cervical Spine: Jefferson = C1 bursting compression; Hangman’s = bilateral C2 pedicle fx (extension/distraction); Clay shoveller’s = C6/C7 spinous process fx (forced hyperflexion).
  • Thoracolumbar & Pelvis: Chance = horizontal vertebral body fx (MVA hyperflexion); Holdsworth = unstable thoracolumbar fx-dislocation; Duverney = isolated iliac wing; Malgaigne = vertical pelvic fx through both pubic rami + ilium/SI joint.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hayes)

"Falls are often low/medium-energy injuries where you must look for the weakest link; osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures affect half of elders over 80."

Second-Order Board Clue

A lateral spine film showing anterior vertebral wedging in an octogenarian requires MRI or bone scan only if fracture acuity is uncertain; treatment remains observation and analgesia.

Knee Ligament Mechanics, Sunrise Patella & Bumper Valgus Collisions

LO 3.02 • Slides 40–41 & Board Q3, Q6
ACL, PCL & MCL Vignettes
  • ACL Tear (Board Q6): A 12-year-old soccer player jumps and twists with internal rotation/valgus stress and lands on an extended knee; exam shows excessive anterior tibial translation (Lachman / anterior drawer) and rapid hemarthrosis via the middle geniculate artery.
  • PCL Contrast: Extends from medial femoral condyle to posterior tibia; injury causes posterior laxity (posterior drawer test).
  • MCL Tear (Board Q3): A 19-year-old football player struck laterally develops severe knee pain, swelling, and medial joint-line tenderness—suspect MCL injury (standard knee X-rays are NOT gold standard for ligament tears).
Patellar & Tibial Plateau ("Bumper") Fractures
  • Patellar Fracture: Caused by a direct blow or indirect quadriceps tendon tension; fractures occur far more often than patellar dislocations and are best visualized on a Sunrise view.
  • Tibial Plateau ("Bumper") Fracture: Vehicle bumper strikes lateral knee (forced valgus), compressing the lateral tibial plateau; occult if depression is absent on standard X-ray.
  • Associated Damage: Frequently damages the ACL, MCL, and medial meniscus, risking post-traumatic arthritis and malunion.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hayes)

"When depression is not present on a standard X-ray, a tibial plateau 'bumper' fracture can be difficult to recognize—always check the ACL, MCL, and medial meniscus."

Second-Order Board Clue

Noncontact pivoting with immediate knee effusion implicates bleeding from the middle geniculate artery supplying a ruptured anterior cruciate ligament.

High Ankle Mechanism Dorsiflexion and/or eversion (syndesmosis)

Ligament/Tendon Grading (I–III), Lateral vs. Syndesmotic Sprains & Achilles Risk

LO 3.03 • Slides 19–20 & Board Q4, Q9b
Sprain & Strain Severity Grading
  • 1st-Degree (Mild): Fibers stretched but intact; minimal swelling/tenderness, no laxity → treated with early mobilization.
  • 2nd-Degree (Moderate): Partial tear (1/3 to nearly all fibers); moderate swelling, painful range of motion, partial loss of function without gross instability → slower return to activity.
  • 3rd-Degree (Severe): Complete rupture (may avulse bone), hemarthrosis, gross instability with no firm endpoint → immobilization, non-weight-bearing, and orthopedic referral (avascular ligaments heal slowly).
Ankle Ligaments & Achilles Rupture
  • Lateral Ankle Sprain (Board Q9b): Inversion of a plantar-flexed foot most commonly tears the weak anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL); stronger forces injure calcaneofibular and posterior talofibular ligaments.
  • Medial vs. High Ankle: The strong medial deltoid ligament resists tearing (forced eversion usually avulses the medial malleolus); dorsiflexion/eversion causes a high ankle (syndesmotic) sprain with distal tibiofibular tenderness but minimal swelling.
  • Achilles Tendon (Board Q4): Formed by gastrocnemius and soleus inserting onto posterior calcaneus; fluoroquinolone use is a classic board risk factor for rupture during pivoting/acceleration.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hayes)

"Due to the avascular nature of ligaments, third-degree sprains heal slowly and require immobilization, non-weight-bearing, orthopedic referral, and physical therapy."

Second-Order Board Clue

Unlike a lateral inversion sprain with rapid swelling over the ATFL, dorsiflexion/eversion tenderness at the distal tibiofibular joint without major swelling signals a syndesmotic sprain.

5th Metatarsal Mimics, Early-Negative Stress X-Rays & Ankle-Foot Eponyms

LO 3.04 • Slide 11, Eponyms & Board Q5
5th Metatarsal & Stress Fractures
  • Normal Apophysis Trap (Board Q5): A 16-year-old boy has lateral midfoot pain after injury; X-ray shows a line parallel to the long axis of the 5th metatarsal = normal fifth metatarsal apophysis (contusion, NOT a fracture).
  • Jones Fracture: True transverse fracture at the base of the 5th metatarsal extending into the intermetatarsal joint after ankle inversion.
  • March (Stress) Fracture: Repetitive forces in soldiers or athletes outpace bone remodeling; early X-rays may be negative while follow-up shows a periosteal stress reaction resembling bone expansion.
Midfoot & Proximal Fibula Eponyms
  • Lisfranc vs. Chopart: Lisfranc = midfoot fracture-dislocation (plantar-flexed foot with rotational force or direct trauma); Chopart = dislocation across talonavicular and calcaneocuboid joints.
  • Maisonneuve vs. Bosworth: Maisonneuve = spiral fracture of the proximal fibula from ankle external rotation; Bosworth = distal fibula fx with posterior dislocation of proximal fibula behind tibia.
  • Gosselin Fracture: V-shaped distal tibia fracture extending into the tibial plafond from high-energy axial load (falls/MVA).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hayes)

"Stress fractures of the metatarsals may not be visible on initial X-rays—follow-up films reveal a periosteal stress reaction with the appearance of bone expansion."

Second-Order Board Clue

Differentiate a pediatric fifth metatarsal apophysis (longitudinal, parallel to shaft) from a Jones fracture (transverse into the intermetatarsal joint).

Orthopedic Emergencies, Bedside Pressure Measurement & Fasciotomy

LO 3.05 • Slides 14 & Board Q5b
Compartment Syndrome Pathophysiology
  • Three Slide Emergencies: Lecture Slide 14 highlights open fractures, shoulder dislocation with greater tuberosity fracture, and anterior compartment syndrome of the leg.
  • Early vs. Late Findings (Board Q5b): Swelling and edema after tibia/fibula or forearm fractures raise fascial pressure—pain and paresthesias appear early, whereas pallor, neurologic deficit, and pulselessness are late signs of irreversible injury.
Immediate Next Step & Contraindications
  • Diagnostic & Surgical Threshold: Next immediate step at bedside is measurement of compartment pressures; fasciotomy is indicated when pressure exceeds >30 mmHg.
  • Critical Contraindications: Do NOT place a cast (creates a constricting ring over edematous tissue); do NOT delay for angiography (vascular injury unlikely) or ultrasonography (DVT unlikely early).
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hayes)

"Pain and paresthesias are early symptoms of compartment syndrome—never wait for pallor or pulselessness, and never wrap a swollen tibia/fibula fracture in a constricting cast."

Second-Order Board Clue

After tibia-fibula fracture, escalating pain and paresthesias mandate immediate bedside compartment pressure measurement; readings >30 mmHg require emergent fasciotomy.

Salter-Harris Type II
Pediatric Physis		 Fracture crosses physis and metaphysis ("Above") Most common (~75%); pediatric distal radius or distal tibia AP/lateral X-ray; good prognosis vs. Types III–V Wrist sits above hand even if radiograph is inverted Types III–V injure proliferative & reserve zones
Supracondylar Humerus
Pediatric Elbow		 Transverse distal humerus above epicondyles via hyperextension Swollen pediatric elbow + diminished radial pulse True lateral elbow X-ray; urgent neurovascular check Classically leads to Volkmann’s contracture (claw hand) Anteromedial spike injures median nerve + brachial artery
Nursemaid’s Elbow
Pediatric Subluxation		 Annular ligament slips over radial head (child <5 yrs) Arm held flexed & pronated; NO swelling or deformity Supination + elbow flexion closed reduction Called pulled, slipped, or toddler’s elbow Sudden axial traction without deformity rules out fracture
Clavicle Fracture
Shoulder Girdle		 Fall on shoulder/FOOSH; weakest at middle third Anterior shoulder crepitus; distal fx pulled inferolaterally X-ray; figure-of-eight splint or sling Most common pediatric/adolescent shoulder injury Deltoid pulls distal fragment down; SCM/trap pull proximal up
Anterior Shoulder Dislocation
Glenohumeral		 Abduction, extension, external rotation (95% of shoulder disloc) Deltoid flattening, acromion prominence, lateral shoulder numbness Plain X-ray + closed reduction (axillary/Y view for posterior) Impaction causes Hill-Sachs; glenoid avulsion causes Bankart Axillary nerve deficit recovers after closed reduction
Galeazzi vs. Monteggia
Forearm		 Forearm blow: GRU vs. MUR coupling Galeazzi = radius fx + DRUJ; Monteggia = ulna fx + radial head AP/lateral forearm including wrist & elbow joints Mnemonic: GRUesome MURder (fx first, disloc second) Distal forearm without elbow symptoms skips elbow X-ray if alert
Colles vs. Smith/Barton
Distal Radius		 Colles = dorsal displacement (FOOSH); Smith = volar (flexed wrist) Elderly osteoporotic woman; 50% with ulnar styloid fx AP/lateral wrist X-ray (Barton = intra-articular + radiocarpal disloc) Chauffeur’s = intra-articular radial styloid (ulnar deviation) Colles dorsal angulation vs. Smith volar angulation
Scaphoid Fracture
Carpal		 FOOSH dorsiflexion/ulnar deviation interrupts retrograde radial artery Point tenderness in anatomical snuffbox (EPL vs. EPB/APL) Initial X-ray often normal; immobilize to prevent nonunion Most commonly fractured carpal bone Proximal pole avascular necrosis from distal-to-proximal flow
Bennett, Boxer’s & UCL
Hand & Thumb		 Bennett = 1st MC axial load; Boxer’s = punch; UCL = valgus thumb Bennett palmar beak; Boxer’s 5th MC neck; Skier’s UCL avulsion Boxer’s: reduction + ulnar gutter splint / K-wire / plate Hand/MC fractures occur 20× more often than carpal fx Rolando = comminuted 1st MC base vs. oblique Bennett
ACL vs. MCL vs. Bumper
Knee		 ACL = noncontact pivot; MCL/Bumper = lateral valgus blow ACL: rapid hemarthrosis, +Lachman; MCL: medial joint tenderness Sunrise view for patella; check ACL/MCL/medial meniscus in Bumper fx Bumper fx can hide on standard X-ray if no depression Middle geniculate artery tear causes rapid ACL effusion
ATFL vs. Syndesmosis & Achilles
Ankle		 Inversion plantar-flexed → ATFL; dorsiflexion/eversion → syndesmosis ATFL: anterolateral swelling; High ankle: distal tib-fib pain, little swelling Grade 3 sprain: immobilization, NWB, ortho referral Medial deltoid ligament is stronger (eversion avulses malleolus) Fluoroquinolone use is a classic risk factor for Achilles rupture
Jones vs. March & Apophysis
Foot		 Jones = inversion; March = repetitive stress; Lisfranc = plantar-flexion twist Jones: base 5th MT into joint; Apophysis: parallel to shaft (normal) March fx: early X-ray negative, later periosteal reaction Do not misread normal 5th MT apophysis as a fracture Lisfranc = midfoot fx-dislocation; Chopart = talonavicular/calcaneocuboid
Acute Compartment Syndrome
Orthopedic Emergency		 Post-fx bleeding/edema raises pressure inside closed fascia Early: pain & paresthesias; Late: pallor, neuro deficit, pulselessness Bedside compartment pressure (>30 mmHg → fasciotomy) Never place a constricting cast on suspected compartment swelling Do not delay with angiography or US; measure pressure immediately
