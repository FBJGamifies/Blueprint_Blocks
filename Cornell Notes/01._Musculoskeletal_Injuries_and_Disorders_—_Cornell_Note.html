LO 1.01 • Pathomechanics

Whiplash Mechanism & Clinical Spectrum

Mechanism of Injury Acceleration-deceleration injury
Structural Targets ALL and PLL tears
Epidemiology Women greater than men
#1 Presentation Neck pain and headache

Cervical Flexion–Extension Injury & Multisystem Symptom Profile

LO 1.01 • Slides 3–5
Mechanism & Tissue Vulnerability
  • Flexion–Extension Force: Whiplash is an acceleration-deceleration injury (commonly MVC, sports injuries, or falls) that is not defined by a single universal pathophysiology.
  • Ligament & Disc Disruption: Rapid hyperextension and hyperflexion may tear the anterior longitudinal ligament and posterior longitudinal ligament, strain cervical soft tissues, herniate intervertebral discs, or exacerbate underlying cervical spondylosis.
  • Sex Distribution: Epidemiology demonstrates a higher incidence in women than men.
Clinical Presentation & Associated Symptoms
  • Primary Complaints: The #1 presenting symptoms are neck pain and headache (including occipital headache).
  • Musculoskeletal & Neuropathic Spread: Patients frequently report stiffness, interscapular pain, upper limb pain or paresthesias, and temporomandibular dysfunction.
  • Vestibular, Cognitive & Psychosocial: WAD encompasses visual disturbances, tinnitus, dizziness, cognitive symptoms, and psychosocial sequelae including anxiety and depression.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Cervical flexion–extension injuries may tear the anterior and posterior longitudinal ligaments and other soft tissues, herniate intervertebral disks, or exacerbate cervical spondylosis—and always screen vestibular and cognitive systems alongside the neck."
Second-Order Board Clue A female passenger after a rear-end MVC develops occipital headache, neck stiffness, jaw pain (temporomandibular dysfunction), and dizziness without focal weakness; recognize the broad symptom constellation of Whiplash-Associated Disorder rather than pursuing central stroke workup prematurely.
LO 1.02 • Severity Grading

Whiplash Grades (I–III) & Long-Term Prognosis

Grade I Hallmark Symptoms without physical signs
Grade II Hallmark Decreased ROM and tenderness
Grade III Hallmark Neurological signs present
2-Year Recovery 40% complete recovery

Stratifying Whiplash Severity and Predicting Chronic Symptom Persistence

LO 1.02 • Slides 6–7
Grade I, II, and III Classification
  • Grade I Injury: Neck pain, stiffness, and tenderness with no physical signs on musculoskeletal or neurologic exam.
  • Grade II Injury: Grade I symptoms plus objective physical signs of decreased range of movement and point tenderness.
  • Grade III Injury: Presence of neurological signs (weakness, sensory loss, absent reflex, or long-tract signs). Severe injuries may involve disc herniation, ligament rupture, fracture, or avulsion of vertebral endplates with cervical root or cord damage.
Severity-Linked Prognosis Data
  • Neck Pain > 6 Months: Persists in 44% of Grade I, 81% of Grade II, and up to 90% of Grade III patients.
  • Headache > 6 Months: Persists in 37% of Grade I, 37% of Grade II, and 70% of Grade III patients.
  • 2-Year General Outcome: Only about 40% report complete recovery at 2 years, while about 45% continue to have major complaints at 2 years post-injury.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "The difference between a trivial injury and one of more significance should be based on the presence or absence of neurological signs—which directly defines Grade III and drives chronicity up to 90%."
Second-Order Board Clue A patient 1 week post-MVC has restricted cervical rotation, paraspinal tenderness, normal strength, intact sensation, and symmetric 2+ reflexes; classify as Grade II whiplash rather than Grade III, noting an 81% risk of neck pain beyond 6 months.
LO 1.03 • Physical Exam

C-Spine Palpation, Neuro Screen & Clearance

Most Common Exam Sign Decreased range of motion
Collar Retention Trigger Spinous process tenderness or step-off
Exam Pitfall Distracting injuries or intoxication
Clearance Rule Awake, alert, no midline pain

Structured Musculoskeletal, Neurological & Cervical Clearance Evaluation

LO 1.03 • Slides 8–9
MSK Palpation & Muscle Tenderness
  • Neutral C-Spine Palpation: Palpate spinous processes and paraspinal muscles while maintaining a neutral cervical spine; if any tenderness at spinous processes or step-offs are found, immediately maintain cervical collar.
  • Classic Tenderness Distribution: Decreased range of motion is the most common finding, followed by tenderness or spasm in paraspinal, trapezius, masseter, temporalis, and suboccipital muscles.
  • Neuro & Systems Screen: Perform upper-extremity motor and sensory testing (C5–T1 roots) alongside screening of the vestibular and cognitive systems.
Serial Exams & Clinical C-Spine Clearance
  • Serial Re-Evaluation: Perform serial examinations until the patient is fully alert and cooperative; remain vigilant for distracting injuries or intoxication / altered mental status (AMS).
  • No-Imaging Clearance Criteria: Cervical spine precautions may be discontinued without imaging only in awake, alert patients who have no midline tenderness, no neurologic deficits, and no pain with active range of motion.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Always keep a neutral C-spine while palpating spinous processes; any midline tenderness or step-off means keep the collar on—and never clear a spine without imaging if the patient is intoxicated or has a distracting injury."
Second-Order Board Clue An awake, sober patient post-fender-bender has no midline spinous process tenderness, full painless active neck rotation, and an intact upper-extremity neuro exam; next best step is discontinue cervical precautions without imaging.
LO 1.04 • Management

WAD Treatment, Collar Limit & Referral Rules

Soft Collar Maximum No longer than 72 hours
Pharmacotherapy NSAIDs and short-term relaxors
Active Rehab Protocol Isometrics then resisted pulleys
MRI & Referral Triggers Neuro signs or pain > 6 weeks

Early Mobilization Strategy, Collar Restrictions & Escalation Thresholds

LO 1.04 • Slide 10
Acute Medications & Immobilization Limit
  • Analgesia & Spasm Control: Initiate NSAIDs or acetaminophen alongside short-term muscle relaxors, plus moist heat for 15 to 20 minutes four to six times per day.
  • Strict 72-Hour Collar Cap: Use a soft cervical collar for no longer than 72 hours; prolonged cervical immobilization delays the return of normal, pain-free motion.
  • Activity Guidance: Encourage patients to continue with their usual activities rather than prolonged rest.
Targeted Exercise, MRI & Specialist Referral
  • Neck-Specific Exercises: Progressive isometric strengthening followed by resisted pulleys has been shown to decrease associated radiating pain.
  • Advanced Imaging Trigger: If neurological signs are present (Grade III), imaging of the cervical spine with MRI is indicated.
  • Subacute Referral Threshold: Persistence of pain for more than 6 weeks indicates referral to a specialized service, often best managed with a multidisciplinary team approach.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Soft collar for no longer than 72 hours—prolonged immobilization delays pain-free motion! Get them moving with usual activities and neck-specific isometrics followed by resisted pulleys."
Second-Order Board Clue A patient with Grade I whiplash asks for a soft cervical collar for 3 weeks; explain that immobilization beyond 72 hours worsens outcomes, and if new upper-extremity weakness appears, order cervical spine MRI.

LO 2.01 • Fascial Anatomy

Fascial Compliance & Extremity Compartments

Fascial Property Stiff, fibrous, low compliance
Lower Leg (4) Ant, Lat, Deep/Sup Post

Non-Compliant Fascial Boundaries & Anatomic Compartment Maps

LO 2.01 • Slides 11–15
Core Definition & Fascial Mechanics
  • Definition: Acute extremity compartment syndrome is increased pressure within a fascial compartment leading to decreased perfusion pressure and tissue hypoxemia, causing irreversible impairment if prolonged.
  • Low-Compliance Envelope: Fascia encasing upper and lower extremity muscle groups is stiff and fibrous with low compliance, meaning small fluid or blood volume increases rapidly spike intracompartmental pressure.
Compartment Counts by Anatomic Region
  • Lower Leg (4 Compartments): Anterior, Lateral, Deep posterior, and Superficial posterior.
  • Thigh (3 Compartments): Medial, Posterior, and Anterior.
  • Forearm (3 Compartments): Volar, Dorsal, and Mobile wad.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Because fascia is stiff and fibrous with low compliance, it cannot expand when swelling occurs—you must know all 4 lower leg, 3 thigh, and 3 forearm compartments so none are missed during decompression."
Second-Order Board Clue During surgical decompression of the forearm, releasing only the dorsal and volar compartments leaves the mobile wad compressed; similarly, a lower leg release must address all four compartments including the deep posterior compartment.
LO 2.02 • Pathophysiology

3 Etiologic Triggers & Ischemic Vicious Cycle

Mechanism #1 (Trauma) Arteriole vasodilation and edema
Mechanism #2 (Volume) Extravasation or coagulopathy
First Vessels Collapsed Efferent venules and lymphatics
Common Ending ROS and cytokine permeability

Traumatic, Nontraumatic, and Exertional Pathways Converging on Capillary Collapse

LO 2.02 • Slides 16–19
Three Distinct Initiating Mechanisms
  • #1 Trauma, Burns, Ischemia–Reperfusion: Increased precapillary arteriole vasodilation increases capillary filtration and collapses venules, raising interstitial fluid and intracompartmental pressure.
  • #2 Nontraumatic (Added Volume): Drug/IV fluid extravasation, bleeding disorders, or coagulopathy directly increase extravascular fluid and compress microvasculature externally.
  • #3 Exertional: Transient, exercise-induced muscle hyperemia and fluid shifts inside a non-compliant fascial compartment.
Common Ending Microvascular Cascade
  • Outflow Obstruction First: Low-pressure efferent outflow vessels (capillaries, venules, lymphatics) collapse first, causing venous congestion and decreasing the arterio-venous gradient.
  • Endothelial ROS Damage: Reduced capillary perfusion pressure causes tissue ischemia, generating reactive oxidative species that damage endothelial cells and raise vascular permeability.
  • Inflammatory Amplification: Neutrophils drawn to ischemic regions release cytokines and chemical mediators that further worsen permeability and fluid extravasation, perpetuating the cycle.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Efferent (outflow) capillaries, venules, and lymphatics collapse first—that drops the arterio-venous gradient, triggers ROS and neutrophil cytokine release, and locks the compartment into a self-perpetuating edema cycle."
Second-Order Board Clue A hemophilia patient develops tense forearm swelling without a fracture after a minor bump; recognize nontraumatic compartment syndrome driven by collapse of efferent venules and lymphatics rather than arterial occlusion.
LO 2.03 • Diagnosis & Peds

ΔP (MPP) Formula & Pediatric 3 A's

Normal Tissue ICP 0 to 10 mmHg
ΔP (MPP) Equation Diastolic BP minus ICP
Fasciotomy Threshold ΔP ≤ 30 or ICP ≥ 30 mmHg
Pediatric 3 A's Anxiety, Agitation, Analgesics

Intracompartmental Pressure Measurement & Pediatric Predictor Criteria

LO 2.03 • Slides 20–24
Adult Pressure & Perfusion Thresholds
  • Normal Baseline: Normal tissue intramuscular pressure is 0–10 mmHg.
  • Perfusion Gradient Formula: ΔP (Muscle Perfusion Pressure / MPP) equals Diastolic blood pressure − intracompartmental pressure.
  • Emergent Fasciotomy Cutoff: When tissue pressure rises within 30 mmHg of diastolic pressure (ΔP ≤ 30 mmHg) OR absolute intracompartmental pressure is ≥ 30 mmHg, capillary blood flow is obstructed and emergent fasciotomies are indicated.
Pediatric Presentation: The 3 A's
  • Evidence Gap in Children: There are no similar published pressure-threshold studies in children, making clinical vigilance paramount.
  • #1 Pediatric Predictor: An increase in pain medication requirement is the #1 predictor of compartment syndrome in children.
  • Classic Pediatric Triad (3 A's): Children present with Anxiety, Agitation, and Increased Analgesic requirement.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Memorize ΔP = Diastolic BP minus intracompartmental pressure: if ΔP is 30 mmHg or lower, or absolute ICP is 30 mmHg or higher, go straight to emergent fasciotomy. In kids, watch the 3 A's—especially escalating analgesic demand!"
Second-Order Board Clue A trauma patient with BP 100/58 mmHg has a measured anterior compartment pressure of 32 mmHg; calculate ΔP = 26 mmHg (≤ 30 mmHg), confirming severe muscle/nerve hypoxia and dictating emergent fasciotomy.
LO 2.04 • Clinical Exam

Bedside Protocol: Today's 5 P's vs. Historical 5 P's

Serial Exam Frequency Every 2 hours at bedside
First Bedside Action Remove circumferential dressing
Historical 5 P's Rule Too late for tissue salvage
Today's 5 P's Pain on passive stretch

Early Recognition via Serial Assessments & Avoiding Late-Sign Traps

LO 2.04 • Slides 31–32
Serial Bedside Assessment Protocol
  • Frequency & Dressing Release: Maintain high clinical suspicion with serial bedside assessments every 2 hours, and immediately remove any circumferential dressing or splint/cast.
  • Direct Skin & Mental Status Check: Assess mental status and pain medication requirement, and inspect skin directly for ecchymosis, blistering, and loss of skin wrinkles (edema).
Today's 5 P's vs. Historical 5 P's
  • Historical 5 P's (TOO LATE!): Pallor, poikilothermia, pulselessness, paresthesia, and paralysis indicate irreversible late ischemia; notably, distal pulses are often intact early because capillary outflow collapses before large arteries.
  • Today's 5 P's: Pain on passive stretch, Pain out of proportion to injury, Pain, Pain, Pain—these are the early actionable findings.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "The historical 5 P's—pallor, poikilothermia, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis—are TOO LATE! Today's 5 P's are Pain on passive stretch, Pain out of proportion, Pain, Pain, Pain. And check every 2 hours!"
Second-Order Board Clue A tibial shaft fracture patient in a cast has normal 2+ dorsalis pedis pulses and warm toes, yet screams with passive toe extension; do not be reassured by intact pulses—immediately split/remove circumferential dressings and evaluate compartment pressure.
LO 2.05 • Ischemia & Surgery

Ischemia Time Limits, AKI & Fasciotomy Contraindication

Nerve Death Window 4 hours (axonotmesis)
Muscle Death Window 8 hours (necrosis)
Rhabdomyolysis Risk Over 40% causing AKI
Missed > 8 Hours Rule Do NOT perform fasciotomy

Irreversible Tissue Windows, Systemic Myoglobinuria & Operative Timing

LO 2.05 • Slides 25–30, 33
Tissue Ischemia Windows & Rhabdomyolysis
  • Nerve Vulnerability: Nerves tolerate ischemia poorly, reaching irreversible axonotmesis at 4 hours.
  • Muscle Tolerance: Skeletal muscle is more resistant to anaerobic metabolism, reaching irreversible necrosis at 8 hours.
  • Systemic Renal Toxicity: Rhabdomyolysis occurs in > 40% of acute compartment syndrome cases; massive myoglobin release causes acute kidney injury (AKI) and kidney failure.
Fasciotomy Technique & > 8-Hour Contraindication
  • Emergent Decompression: Within the salvage window, perform emergent fasciotomy (e.g., lateral/dual-incision lower leg release of all compartments) followed by delayed closure once edema subsides.
  • Missed Compartment Syndrome (> 8 hrs / Contractures): Advised NOT to release via fasciotomy after 8 hours of ischemia, because opening already necrotic muscle markedly increases the risk of infection.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Nerves die at 4 hours, muscle at 8 hours—and if a missed compartment syndrome presents over 8 hours with contractures, DO NOT open it because releasing necrotic muscle skyrockets infection risk."
Second-Order Board Clue A patient presents 14 hours after a crush injury with a firm, insensate, paralyzed leg and tea-colored urine; avoid fasciotomy (> 8-hour infection risk) and aggressively manage rhabdomyolysis-induced AKI.

LO 3.01 • Epidemiology & Etiology

CMT Incidence, Risk Factors & SCM Compartment Theory

Incidence & Sex Ratio 0.3–2.0% and 3:2 M:F
Obstetric Risk Factors Oligohydramnios and breech
Pathophysiology SCM venous obstruction
Terminal Mechanism SCM compartment syndrome

Perinatal Intrauterine Crowding & Ischemic Fibrosis of the Sternocleidomastoid

LO 3.01 • Slides 34–37
Epidemiology & Perinatal Risk Factors
  • Prevalence & Demographics: Most common cause of infantile torticollis, occurring in 0.3–2.0% of infants with a 3:2 male-to-female ratio.
  • Intrauterine & Delivery Risks: Driven by limited intrauterine space and birth trauma: oligohydramnios, first pregnancy, breech delivery, and traumatic delivery.
Vascular-Ischemic Pathogenesis
  • Muscle Injury Cascade: Suspected SCM injury from intrauterine or perinatal compression and stretching causes venous outflow obstruction.
  • Resulting Contracture: Compression decreases blood supply and triggers a localized SCM compartment syndrome, leading to muscle fibrosis and contracture.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Notice how CMT links right back to compartment syndrome: compression and stretching of the SCM obstructs venous outflow, decreases blood supply, and creates a localized compartment syndrome leading to fibrotic contracture."
Second-Order Board Clue A first-born male infant delivered breech with oligohydramnios develops a firm right neck nodule at 3 weeks of life; the underlying mechanism is venous outflow obstruction and SCM compartment syndrome.
LO 3.02 • SCM Anatomy

SCM Attachments, Posture Vector & CN XI Risk

SCM Origins (2 Heads) Manubrium and medial clavicle
SCM Insertions Mastoid and lateral occiput
Classic Deformity Posture Tilt toward, chin away
Nerve at Surgical Risk Cranial nerve XI

Biomechanics of Sternocleidomastoid Contracture & Operative Nerve Protection

LO 3.02 • Slides 38–39
Anatomic Attachments & Function
  • Origins: Sternal head from the anterior surface of manubrium sterni; clavicular head from the superior surface of medial third of clavicle.
  • Insertions: Lateral mastoid process on temporal bone and lateral occipital bone.
  • Unilateral Action: Normal contraction produces ipsilateral neck flexion and contralateral head rotation—explaining why a shortened left SCM tilts the head left and turns the chin right.
Innervation & Operative Risk
  • Motor Nerve: Innervated by cranial nerve XI (spinal accessory nerve).
  • Surgical Vulnerability: CN XI is directly at risk during operative release or Z-lengthening of the SCM, requiring meticulous identification and protection.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Because the SCM causes ipsilateral neck flexion and contralateral head rotation, contracture gives you head tilt TOWARD the affected side and chin rotation AWAY—and watch out for CN XI during surgery!"
Second-Order Board Clue An infant tilts their head toward the right shoulder with the chin pointing toward the left shoulder; the contracted muscle is the right sternocleidomastoid, and operative release places cranial nerve XI at iatrogenic risk.
LO 3.03 • Exam & Packaging

Physical Exam & Associated Packaging Disorders

Palpation & Timing Palpable mass < 4 weeks
ROM Characteristic Painless passive ROM < 1 yr
Hip Association DDH (5–15% association)
Cranial Asymmetry Contralateral plagiocephaly

Newborn Clinical Diagnosis & Mandatory Screening for Packaging Anomalies

LO 3.03 • Slides 38, 40
Inspection, Palpation & Newborn Exam
  • Clinical Posture & Mass: Usually noted during newborn exam or within the first four weeks of life; inspection shows neck tilt toward affected SCM, head rotated away, head extended and protracted, with a palpable neck mass in the contracted SCM.
  • Key Diagnostic Feature: Painless passive neck ROM! Clinicians can typically bring the head through painless passive ROM until about 1 year of age.
  • Complete Baby Exam: Assess visual function, auditory function, neurologic exam, and screen hips, feet, and spine.
Associated Packaging Disorders
  • Hip Dysplasia: Screen carefully for developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH), present in 5–15% of CMT cases.
  • Foot & Spine Anomalies: Evaluate for metatarsus adductus, calcaneovalgus feet, and congenital atlanto-occipital abnormalities.
  • Skull Flattening: Positional plagiocephaly (asymmetric flattening of the skull) occurs on the contralateral side because the infant rests on the side toward which the chin is rotated.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Painless passive neck ROM! That is huge for boards—and because CMT is a packaging disorder, always check the hips for DDH (5–15% association), feet for metatarsus adductus/calcaneovalgus, and the skull for contralateral plagiocephaly."
Second-Order Board Clue A 3-week-old newborn has a painless left SCM mass with left head tilt and right chin rotation; in addition to passive stretching, perform Ortolani/Barlow maneuvers to screen for developmental dysplasia of the hip (5–15%) and inspect the right occiput for plagiocephaly.
LO 3.04 • Imaging & DDx

Imaging Selection & Torticollis Differential

Palpable Mass Present Ultrasound (hyperechoic SCM)
No Palpable Mass AP and lateral C-spine XR
Painful Rotation Rule-Out Dynamic C1–C2 CT scan
Bony Fusion Triad Klippel-Feil syndrome

Targeted Imaging Modalities & Distinguishing Non-Muscular Torticollis

LO 3.04 • Slides 41–44
Imaging Indications & Findings
  • Ultrasound: Indicated for head tilt/rotation with decreased ROM and a palpable mass; reveals a larger and hyperechoic SCM (due to fibrosis) compared to the contralateral side.
  • Cervical Spine Radiographs (AP & Lateral): Indicated when head tilt/rotation occurs with no palpable mass to rule out bony anomalies.
  • Dynamic CT & Brain/C-Spine MRI: Dynamic CT at C1–C2 (head straight, then max rotation left and right) rules out atlantoaxial rotatory subluxation; MRI brain and C-spine rules out non-muscular and central causes.
High-Yield Differential Diagnosis
  • Atlantoaxial Rotatory Subluxation: Critically painful (unlike painless CMT); often post-traumatic or post-infectious (Grisel's disease).
  • Klippel-Feil Syndrome: Classic triad of short webbed neck, low posterior hairline, and limited cervical range of motion.
  • Other Causes: Ophthalmologic, vestibular, and central or peripheral nervous system lesions.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "No palpable mass? Start with AP and lateral C-spine radiographs to rule out bony issues like Klippel-Feil. Painful torticollis after trauma or pharyngitis? Think Grisel's disease / atlantoaxial rotatory subluxation and order a dynamic C1–C2 CT!"
Second-Order Board Clue A child presents with acute painful neck tilt following an upper respiratory / pharyngeal infection without a neck mass; diagnose Grisel's disease (atlantoaxial rotatory subluxation) via dynamic C1–C2 CT rather than CMT stretching.

Passive Stretching, Surgical Release & Untreated Sequelae

Nonoperative Indications < 1 yr and < 30° ROM loss
Stretching Vector Tilt away, chin toward

Facial Remodeling Cutoff Release before 10 years old

Conservative Stretching Vectors, 1-Year Surgical Cutoff & Long-Term Deformities

LO 3.05 • Slides 45–47
Nonoperative Passive Stretching
  • Indications: Condition present for < 1 year and < 30° limitation in ROM.
  • Exact Stretching Technique: Apply lateral head tilt away from the affected side combined with chin rotation toward the affected side (reversing the contracture vector).
  • Expected Outcome: 90–95% respond to passive stretching within the first year of life.
Operative Release & Untreated Sequelae
  • Surgical Indications & Procedure: Indicated after failed response to at least 1 year of stretching; perform bipolar release of SCM or Z-lengthening.
  • Operative Outcomes: 92% success even in older children; facial asymmetry can improve as long as release is done prior to 10 years of age.
  • Untreated Sequelae: Left untreated or unnoticed, CMT causes permanent rotational deformity, positional plagiocephaly, craniofacial deformities (facial asymmetry and facial hemihypoplasia), and compensatory scoliosis.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "90–95% of infants resolve with passive stretching in the first year—tilt away and rotate chin toward the tight SCM. If stretching fails for at least 1 year, bipolar release or Z-lengthening achieves 92% success and corrects facial asymmetry if done before age 10."
Second-Order Board Clue A 6-year-old with untreated right CMT has persistent right head tilt and mild facial hemihypoplasia; proceeding with bipolar SCM release now still permits improvement in facial asymmetry because the child is under 10 years of age.
