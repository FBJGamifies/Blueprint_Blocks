Joint Stabilizers, Effusion Mechanics & Synovial Fluid Analysis

Lecturer Rule: Never aspirate a suspected neoplasm — image (X-ray then MRI) first

Synovial Fluid Analysis & Crystal Discriminator Workbench

Select a diagnostic synovial profile to inspect WBC thresholds, polarized birefringence, and clinical action.

LO 1.01 • Joint Architecture

Joint Stabilizer Spectrum (Static vs. Dynamic)

Primary Stability Rule Bony congruency inversely sets ROM
Static Stabilizers Labrum, menisci, ligaments, capsule
Dynamic Stabilizers Tendons and muscles
Low-Congruency Example Shoulder needs rotator cuff control

Bony Congruency Trade-Off & Static vs. Dynamic Stabilization

LO 1.01 • Slide 4
Bony Stability vs. Mobility Spectrum
  • Congruency Trade-Off: Inherent joint stability is largely secondary to the surface area of bony contact (bony congruency), which operates inversely to range of motion (ROM).
  • Low Stability / High Mobility: The glenohumeral joint has minimal bony contact and highest ROM, so it relies heavily on dynamic muscular control from the rotator cuff plus its glenoid labrum.
  • Medium vs. High Stability: Hinge and bicondylar joints (elbow, knee) offer intermediate bony stability, whereas the sacroiliac joint and pubic symphysis possess maximum bony stability and minimal mobility.
Static vs. Dynamic Stabilizers
  • Static Stabilizers: Passive fibrocartilaginous and collagenous constraints including the labrum (shoulder & hip), menisci (medial/lateral knee and pubic symphysis), ligaments (more prone to failure at specific joint positions), and joint capsules.
  • Dynamic Stabilizers: Active contractile elements—specifically muscles and their tendons—that generate compressive joint forces during motion.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "The more bony surface area touching, the more stable the joint—and the less it moves. Ligaments are static stabilizers, yet they fail predictably when a joint lands in an unguarded position."
Second-Order Board Clue A patient with recurrent shoulder instability despite intact bones points to failure of static capsulolabral stabilizers or rotator cuff dynamic stabilization compensating for shallow glenoid congruency.
LO 1.02 • Effusion Mechanics

Joint Effusion Presentation & Arthrocentesis

Core Definition Sign of underlying pathology, not diagnosis
Position of Comfort Slightly flexed joint to relax capsule
ROM Hallmark Lack of terminal extension
Diagnostic Procedure Sterile arthrocentesis (5-test panel)

Capsular Distension Mechanics & Sterile Arthrocentesis Panel

LO 1.02 • Slides 5–6
Clinical Presentation & Biomechanics
  • Definition: An effusion is an abnormal excessive accumulation of fluid inside a body space (two main medical types: pleural effusion and joint effusion). It is a clinical sign, not a conclusive diagnosis.
  • Why Joints Flex: Excess intra-articular fluid raises capsular pressure; patients hold the joint in a slightly flexed position because that position maximizes capsular volume and relaxes tension, producing lack of terminal extension and restricted ROM.
Arthrocentesis Lab Panel
  • Sterile Technique: Diagnosis is supported by needle aspiration of synovial fluid (arthrocentesis) under strict sterile technique.
  • Five Mandatory Lab Tests: Always send synovial fluid for Gram stain, cell count and differential, culture, glucose level, and crystal analysis.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "An effusion is never the final answer—it tells you something inside the joint is irritated. Always check terminal extension; capsular swelling physically blocks full extension first."
Second-Order Board Clue A patient refusing full knee or hip extension and resting in slight flexion has capsular distension from joint effusion; immediate diagnostic step (after imaging) is sterile arthrocentesis.
LO 1.03 • Synovial Analysis

Fluid Thresholds, Crystals & Neoplasm Rule

Septic Joint Cutoff >50,000 WBC/mm³ (>90% PMN)
Inflammatory vs. OA 2,000–50,000 vs. <2,000 WBC/mm³
MSU vs. CPPD Crystals Needle negative vs. rhomboid positive
Critical Exclusion Never aspirate suspected neoplasm

Diagnostic Cell-Count Brackets, Polarized Microscopy & Chondrocalcinosis

LO 1.03 • Slides 7–9
Cell Count, Glucose & Hemarthrosis
  • Normal vs. Non-Inflammatory: Normal fluid has <200 WBC/mm³ (<25% PMN); non-inflammatory (e.g., osteoarthritis) has <2,000 WBC/mm³.
  • Inflammatory vs. Septic: Inflammatory effusions fall between 2,000–50,000 WBC/mm³ (>75% PMN); septic joints exceed >50,000 WBC/mm³ (>90% PMN) with synovial glucose <50% serum glucose.
  • Trauma & Neoplasm: Acute trauma or repetitive overuse yields hemarthrosis. If tumor/neoplasm is suspected, obtain X-ray then MRI—do NOT aspirate!
Gout vs. Pseudogout Microscopy
  • Gout (MSU): Monosodium urate crystals are needle-shaped with strong negative birefringence (yellow when parallel to compensator axis, blue when perpendicular).
  • Pseudogout (CPPD): Calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate crystals are rhomboid-shaped with weak positive birefringence (blue when parallel, yellow when perpendicular) and deposit in menisci as chondrocalcinosis.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Always get X-rays first—if you suspect a tumor or neoplasm, stop! You must NEVER stick a needle into a suspected neoplasm because you can seed the biopsy tract."
Second-Order Board Clue Knee radiograph showing linear meniscal calcification (chondrocalcinosis) paired with weakly positively birefringent rhomboid crystals confirms pseudogout (CPPD).

Ankle Mortise Biomechanics & Tibiotalar Effusions

Lecturer Rule: Bilateral ankle swelling without trauma points to systemic etiology

Ankle Mortise Biomechanics & Sprain Triage Analyzer

Select a talar position or clinical scenario to visualize mortise congruency, ligament failure, and radiographic criteria.

LO 2.01 • Mortise Kinematics

Dynamic Stability of the Tibiotalar Mortise

Joint Architecture Mortise and tenon (tibia, fibula, talus)
Most Stable Position Dorsiflexion (wide anterior talus)
Position of Vulnerability Plantarflexion (narrow posterior talus)
Neurovascular Exam DP/PT pulses & pitting edema

Trapezoidal Talar Dome Geometry & Ankle Physical Exam

LO 2.01 • Slides 10–13
Dynamic Bony Congruency
  • Mortise & Tenon Design: The tibiotalar joint acts as a mortise (distal tibia and fibula) gripping the talar dome.
  • Why Dorsiflexion Stabilizes: Because the talar dome is wider anteriorly than posteriorly, dorsiflexion wedges the broad anterior talus tightly into the mortise, maximizing bony contact.
  • Why Plantarflexion Fails: In plantarflexion, only the narrower posterior talar dome sits inside the mortise, creating bony play and making it the position of vulnerability.
Focused Ankle Exam
  • Full MSK Sequence: Document MOI/HPI (OLD CARTS, occupation/hobbies for overuse), inspection, palpation, ROM, manual muscle testing (MMT), and special tests.
  • Vascular Check: Always palpate dorsalis pedis (DP) and posterior tibial (PT) pulses and assess for pitting edema to separate intra-articular mortise effusion from vascular overload.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Tibiotalar congruency is dynamic! In dorsiflexion, the wide front of the talus locks the mortise tight; in plantarflexion, the narrow back of the talus lets the ankle roll."
Second-Order Board Clue A basketball player landing with a plantarflexed ankle loses bony mortise stability because the narrow posterior talar dome sits in the mortise, shifting load to the lateral ligaments.
LO 2.02 • Sprains & Radiographs

Ankle Sprain Mechanisms & 3-View Mortise Criteria

Lateral Inversion Sprain Plantarflexed: ATFL, CFL, PTFL
High Ankle Sprain Forced dorsiflexion (syndesmosis)
Standard X-Ray Series 3 weight-bearing: AP, Lateral, Mortise
Normal Mortise Metrics Overlap >1 mm; medial space 3 mm

Ligament Injury Patterns by MOI & Weight-Bearing Radiograph Interpretation

LO 2.02 • Slides 12 & 14
Mechanism-to-Ligament Mapping
  • Lateral Inversion Sprain: Landing with the ankle plantarflexed and inverting stresses the lateral collateral complex sequentially: ATFL (anterior talofibular ligament, injured first), CFL (calcaneofibular), and PTFL (posterior talofibular).
  • High Ankle Sprain: Forced dorsiflexion drives the wide anterior talus like a wedge between tibia and fibula, rupturing the syndesmosis: AITFL, PITFL, and interosseous membrane.
3 Weight-Bearing Radiographic Views
  • Standard Views: Order 3 weight-bearing views—AP, Lateral, and Oblique / Mortise (internal rotation brings the malleoli parallel to show joint space).
  • Normal Mortise Criteria: Distal tibiofibular overlap >1 mm, equal horizontal and medial clear space (3 mm), lateral clear space <3–6 mm, and visible fibular fossa.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Always order weight-bearing AP, Lateral, and Mortise views when the patient can tolerate them—non-weight-bearing films can miss subtle syndesmotic widening."
Second-Order Board Clue Forced dorsiflexion with external rotation causing widened medial clear space (>3 mm) and loss of tibiofibular overlap (<1 mm) indicates syndesmotic (high ankle) sprain injuring the AITFL and interosseous membrane.
LO 2.03 • Effusion DDX

Tibiotalar Effusion Differential & Systemic Clues

Intra-Articular DDX Trauma, arthritis, septic, gout, CPPD, tumor
Bilateral Red Flag Bilateral + no MOI = systemic cause
Systemic Triplet Cardiogenic, renal, vascular
Anatomic Definition Swelling inside mortise joint

Separating Intra-Articular Tibiotalar Effusions from Systemic Edema

LO 2.03 • Slides 10–11 & 15
Local Intra-Articular Causes
  • True Tibiotalar Effusion: Defined as ankle swelling confined inside the mortise joint capsule.
  • Six Core Local Etiologies: Acute trauma or chronic repetitive overuse, arthritis (umbrella term: inflammatory vs. OA), septic joint, gout, pseudogout, and tumor / neoplasm.
Systemic Differential Clues
  • Bilateral Without MOI: Bilateral ankle swelling in the absence of a mechanism of injury points away from orthopedic sprain toward systemic pathology.
  • Systemic Causes: Evaluate for cardiogenic (heart failure), renal insufficiency (volume overload), and vascular (chronic venous insufficiency) or systemic inflammatory disease.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Always ask if both ankles are swollen! Bilateral ankle swelling without a mechanism of injury makes you think heart failure, renal volume overload, or venous insufficiency—not an orthopedic joint effusion."
Second-Order Board Clue Symmetric bilateral ankle swelling with pitting edema and no trauma history warrants workup for cardiogenic, renal, or chronic venous insufficiency rather than arthrocentesis.

Bursae Anatomy, Bursitis Pathology & Aspiration Rules

Lecturer Rule: Never aspirate through cellulitis — know a bursa from a joint

Bursitis vs. Joint Effusion Anatomy & Aspiration Safety Workbench

Compare superficial bursal sacs against true intra-articular joint capsules and review Dr. Hickerson's 5 safety rules.

LO 3.01 • Bursal Anatomy

Bursa Function & Bursa-vs-Joint Aspiration Rule

Structure & Function Fluid sac reducing friction & shock
Terminology Distinction NOT a joint; NOT arthrocentesis
Primary Lab Question Rule out septic vs. non-septic
Anatomic Locations Between bone, tendon, muscle, skin

Periarticular Gliding Cushions & Procedural Nomenclature

LO 3.01 • Slides 16–17
Anatomy & Physiology of Bursae
  • Definition: A bursa is a small, fluid-filled sac acting as a cushion and gliding surface situated between bones, muscles, tendons, and skin.
  • Mechanical Role: Reduces friction and provides shock absorption during repetitive gliding across bony prominences.
  • Bursitis: Inflammation of a bursa ("-itis"), producing focal extra-articular swelling rather than deep capsular distension.
Aspiration Terminology & Goal
  • Not an Arthrocentesis: Because bursae are NOT joints, aspirating a bursa is technically not an arthrocentesis (it is a bursal aspiration).
  • Diagnostic Focus: Send aspirated bursal fluid for the same synovial panel (Gram stain, cell count/diff, culture, glucose, crystals), though clinically you are primarily looking for septic vs. non-septic bursitis.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Bursae are NOT joints! Calling a bursal tap an arthrocentesis—or worse, putting a needle through superficial bursal infection into the deep knee joint—is a classic dangerous mistake."
Second-Order Board Clue Focal anterior knee swelling superficial to the patella with preserved passive intra-articular joint motion localizes to the prepatellar bursa rather than an intra-articular knee effusion.
LO 3.02 • Bursitis Entities

Prepatellar (Septic) & Trochanteric Bursitis

Prepatellar Alias Housemaid's knee (over patella)
Septic Entry Portal Cellulitis and/or abrasion over bursa
Abscess Indicator Loculated fluid collection
Trochanteric Driver Aseptic IT band friction

Septic Prepatellar Entry Portals vs. Aseptic Trochanteric Friction

LO 3.02 • Slides 19–21
Septic Prepatellar Bursitis
  • Clinical Picture: Prepatellar bursitis ("housemaid's knee") causes fluid swelling directly anterior to the patella.
  • Infection Route: Septic prepatellar bursitis is often accompanied by superficial cellulitis and/or skin abrasion overlying the bursa, which serves as the direct entry point for skin bacteria.
  • Abscess Sign: Presence of a loculated fluid collection on imaging indicates abscess formation requiring drainage.
Trochanteric Bursitis
  • Pathomechanics: Classic example of common aseptic bursitis driven by repetitive mechanical friction of the iliotibial (IT) band gliding over the greater trochanter.
  • Imaging Clue: Coronal T2-weighted MRI demonstrates focal hyperintensity (bright fluid signal) over the greater trochanteric bursa beneath the IT band.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Look at the skin over a swollen prepatellar bursa—an abrasion or surrounding cellulitis is the entry portal for infection, and loculated pockets mean an abscess has formed."
Second-Order Board Clue Lateral hip pain over the greater trochanter with coronal T2 MRI hyperintensity reflects aseptic trochanteric bursitis caused by IT band friction.
LO 3.03 • Clinical Pearls

Dr. Hickerson's 5 Aspiration Safety Rules

Rule 1 (Imaging) No aspiration without imaging first
Rule 2 (Skin Barrier) Never tap through cellulitis
Rule 3 (Antibiotics) No empiric IV antibiotics unless septic
Rule 4 (Technique) No pin-cushioning / traumatic tap

The Five Non-Negotiable Joint & Bursa Aspiration Clinical Pearls

LO 3.03 • Slide 18
Rules 1–3: Imaging, Skin & Antibiotics
  • Pearl 1 — Image First: Do NOT aspirate any joint without imaging first (essential to rule out fracture, hardware, or suspected neoplasm).
  • Pearl 2 — Respect Cellulitis: Do NOT aspirate through cellulitis, because passing a needle through infected skin inoculates bacteria directly into a sterile joint space.
  • Pearl 3 — Hold Empiric IV Antibiotics: Do NOT start empiric IV antibiotics unless the patient is in sepsis—then call an ortho consult to rule out a septic joint before cultures are sterilized.
Rules 4–5: Tap Quality & Anatomy
  • Pearl 4 — Avoid Traumatic Taps: Do NOT "pin-cushion" the joint or create a "traumatic tap" (blood contaminates cell counts); obtain a clean sample, send it to the lab, then call the ortho consult.
  • Pearl 5 — Space Identification: Know the anatomic difference between a superficial bursa and a deep intra-articular joint before selecting an access point.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Don't start IV antibiotics before arthrocentesis unless the patient is in sepsis! Sterilizing the joint before we get fluid ruins our culture diagnosis—and don't pin-cushion the joint and dump it on ortho."
Second-Order Board Clue An afebrile, hemodynamically stable patient with a warm swollen knee and overlying skin erythema must NOT have a needle passed through cellulitis and should NOT receive empiric IV antibiotics prior to sterile diagnostic aspiration.

Neuropathic (Charcot) Arthropathies

Lecturer Rule: Charcot foot erythema decreases with elevation; get ESR/CRP in shoulder/elbow

Neuropathic Arthropathy Diagnostic Discriminator

Select a neuropathic stage or anatomic site to trace spinal cord tract damage, bedside elevation testing, and management.

LO 4.01 • Upper Extremity Charcot

Charcot Shoulder & Elbow (Syringomyelia)

Bottom Line Driver Loss of protective sensation
Cord Lesion Syrinx damaging decussating STT
Sensory Hallmark Dissociative anesthesia
Key Labs to Order ESR and CRP (rule out septic joint)

Syringomyelia Cord Tract Destruction & Shoulder/Elbow Presentation

LO 4.01 • Slides 22–24
Spinal Cord Pathophysiology
  • Primary Syrinx Lesion: A fluid-filled cavity (syrinx) in the central cervical spinal cord damages the decussating fibers of the lateral spinothalamic tract, abolishing pain and temperature sensation.
  • Dissociative Anesthesia: Produces classic dissociative anesthesia where proprioception and motor function remain preserved while pain/temperature are lost—resulting in loss of protective sensation.
  • Syrinx Enlargement: As the syrinx expands into the dorsal column and anterior horn, patients develop areflexia, loss of motor strength, and muscle atrophy.
Clinical Presentation & DDX
  • Demographics & Exam: Typically middle-aged patients presenting with loss of ROM (#1 finding), a warm swollen joint, and instability.
  • Trauma & Pain Profile: Approximately 30% link to a traumatic event, and 50% are painless despite dramatic destruction.
  • Mandatory Differential: Mimics infection closely, so always obtain ESR and CRP to rule out a septic joint.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Bottom line for all Charcot joints: loss of protective sensation! In the shoulder or elbow, think cervical syrinx—and because 50% are warm, swollen, and painless, always draw ESR/CRP to rule out septic arthritis."
Second-Order Board Clue Middle-aged patient with a warm, severely destroyed, painless elbow, preserved deep touch/proprioception, and lost pinprick/temperature in a cape distribution has neuropathic arthropathy secondary to cervical syringomyelia.
LO 4.02 • Diabetic Charcot Foot

Diabetic Charcot Foot, Elevation Test & Offloading

Bedside Discriminator Erythema decreases with elevation
Radiographic Hallmark Joint obliteration & fragmentation
Acute First-Line Tx Total contact casting (TCC)
Chronic Orthotic / Op CROW boot; fusion if ulcerated

Distinguishing Charcot Foot from Cellulitis & Offloading Protocol

LO 4.02 • Slides 25–26
Elevation Test & X-Ray Destruction
  • Clinical Presentation: Chronic progressive disorder caused by diabetic loss of protective sensation, producing a warm, erythematous, swollen foot.
  • Crucial Elevation Test: Erythema in acute Charcot decreases with foot elevation (hyperemic dependent rubor), whereas cellulitis/infection erythema remains unchanged!
  • Radiographs: Reveal obliteration of joint space and fragmentation of both articular surfaces leading to subluxation or dislocation ("bag of bones" / rocker-bottom foot).
Medical, Orthotic & Surgical Care
  • Systemic & Acute Offloading: Optimize medical management for diabetes and initiate a trial of total contact casting (TCC) for acute Charcot deformities without skin breakdown.
  • Brace & Operative Criteria: Transition to a Charcot Restraint Orthotic Walker (CROW boot); operative management (fusion/reconstruction) is indicated for recurrent infections, severe deformity, or severe skin breakdown/ulceration.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Elevate the foot on the exam table! If the redness fades with elevation, it's acute Charcot—put them in a total contact cast as long as there's no skin breakdown, then a CROW boot."
Second-Order Board Clue A diabetic patient with a warm, red, swollen midfoot whose erythema blanches on elevation and X-ray shows bone fragmentation and subluxation needs total contact casting (TCC) rather than IV antibiotics for cellulitis.

Meniscal and Labral Injuries & Joint Kinematics

Lecturer Rule: Severe knee OA with crepitus does NOT need an MRI for meniscus tear

Meniscal & Labral Tear Biomechanics Workbench

Inspect vascular healing zones, load-transmission shifts, FAI impingement mechanics, and imaging thresholds.

LO 5.01 • Shoulder Labrum

Glenoid Labrum & Posterior Instability

Tissue & Function 50% socket depth; cavity-compression
Most Common Symptom Vague nonspecific pain & clicking
Provocative Activities Bench press, push-ups, heavy doors
Exam & Imaging +Posterior apprehension; MR arthrogram

Capsulolabral Cavity-Compression & Diagnostic MR Arthrography

LO 5.01 • Slides 27–30
Anatomy & Injury Pathomechanics
  • Static Stabilizer: Composed of fibrocartilaginous tissue, the glenoid labrum creates cavity-compression and provides 50% of the glenoid socket depth.
  • Microtrauma & Dislocation: Repetitive microtrauma to the capsulolabral complex or recurrent shoulder dislocations produce vague, nonspecific shoulder pain (most common symptom), mechanical clicking, and perceived instability with ROM.
Clinical Clues & Diagnostic Imaging
  • Posterior Loading Clues: Pain worsens with directed posterior force—patients report difficulty pushing heavy doors, performing bench press, or doing push-ups.
  • Exam & Gold-Standard Study: Look for a positive posterior apprehension test and order a Magnetic Resonance (MR) arthrogram of the shoulder.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "Without the glenoid labrum, you lose half your socket depth! If a patient struggles with bench press, push-ups, or heavy doors and has posterior apprehension, order an MR arthrogram."
Second-Order Board Clue Weightlifter with vague deep shoulder pain during bench press and a positive posterior apprehension test has a posterior glenoid labral tear best visualized by MR arthrogram.
LO 5.02 • Hip Labrum

Hip Labral Tears, FAI (Cam/Pincer) & Dysplasia

Shape & Vascularity Horseshoe; peripheral 1/3 vascular
FAI Subtypes Cam (femoral bump) vs. Pincer (rim)
Adult Hip Dysplasia Shallow acetabulum → edge loading
Diagnostic Sensitivity MR arthrogram (92% sensitive)

Acetabular Edge Loading, Femoroacetabular Impingement & Groin Symptoms

LO 5.02 • Slides 31–34
Anatomy & 5 Etiologic Drivers
  • Anatomy: Horseshoe-shaped fibrocartilage continuous with the transverse acetabular ligament that deepens the acetabulum; only the peripheral 1/3 is vascularized.
  • FAI & Dysplasia: Femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) tears the labrum via Cam (bony prominence at femoral head-neck junction) or Pincer (acetabular overcoverage). Adult hip dysplasia features a shallow acetabulum lacking anterior/lateral coverage, causing edge loading, labral tears, and secondary OA.
  • Other Drivers: Trauma (hip dislocation/subluxation), capsular laxity (hypermobility), and joint degeneration.
Presentation & Imaging
  • Symptoms: Vague groin pain accompanied by mechanical hip pain, snapping, clicking, or a sensation of locking.
  • Diagnostic Study: An MR arthrogram is 92% sensitive for detecting hip labral tears.
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "In adult hip dysplasia, the shallow socket puts edge loading straight onto a floppy labrum—and secondary osteoarthritis actually starts with labral tears! MR arthrogram catches 92% of them."
Second-Order Board Clue Young adult with mechanical groin pain, snapping, and femoral head-neck bony prominence has Cam-type FAI causing a hip labral tear confirmed on MR arthrogram (92% sensitivity).
LO 5.03 • Knee Menisci

Meniscal Zones, Kinematics & OA MRI Decision Rule

Vascularity Rule Outer 1/3 Red Zone heals; inner 2/3 white
Load Transmission 50% extension; 85% flexion
ACL-Deficient Knee Menisci become primary stabilizers
Special Tests & MRI Rule +McMurray / +Apley; NO MRI in severe OA

Red vs. White Vascular Zones, Acute vs. Degenerative Tears & Crepitus

LO 5.03 • Slides 35–41
Vascularity, Load & Kinematics
  • 4 Menisci & Vascularity: You have 4 knee menisci (2 medial, 2 lateral). Only the outer 1/3 ("Red Zone") is vascularized and has healing potential; the inner 2/3 ("White Zone") is avascular.
  • Load & Stability: Increases congruency and contact area to reduce point loading and absorb shock (transmits 50% weight-bearing load in extension, 85% in flexion). Lateral meniscus has 2x the excursion of the medial meniscus and is less stabilizing; menisci become primary stabilizers in the ACL-deficient knee.
Tear Subtypes, Exam & Crepitus Rule
  • Tear Patterns (Applies to Labrum Too): Chronic degenerative (>3 mo episodic symptoms, older or intense hobby/occupation), acute (<2 wk with specific rotational MOI, any age), and acute-on-chronic.
  • Exam Findings: Knee effusion, lack of terminal ROM, deep flexion pain, joint line TTP, locking/catching/buckling, +Apley and +McMurray.
  • Imaging & Crepitus: Crepitus is a clicking/grating sound or feeling from worn surfaces, tears, or effusions. Severe advanced OA with crepitus does NOT need an MRI to rule out a meniscus tear!
Lecturer High-Yield Focus (Dr. Hickerson) "If a patient already has severe advanced osteoarthritis with bone-on-bone crepitus on X-ray, do NOT order an MRI to look for a meniscus tear—it won't change management!"
Second-Order Board Clue A patient with a chronic ACL-deficient knee relies on the medial/lateral menisci as primary stabilizers, placing the less-mobile medial meniscus at high risk for secondary tearing.
